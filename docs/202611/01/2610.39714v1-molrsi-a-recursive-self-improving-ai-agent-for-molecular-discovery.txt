AI agent for Molecular Discovery

Abstract
Molecular discovery requires navigating vast combinatorial search spaces while balancing competing bi-
ological objectives. AI agents can automate expert-deﬁned molecular design protocols, but automation
alone does not address how to optimize workﬂows for new tasks or improve their development through
experience. Here we introduce MolRSI, an AI agent that combines systematic optimization of workﬂows
and model architectures with persistent cross-task memory to recursively improve its search policy for
molecular discovery. Workﬂow performance, reﬁnement outcomes, and execution feedback guide sub-
sequent searches, allowing the agent to improve both its workﬂows and its strategy for developing them.
We evaluate MolRSI in two challenging molecular discovery settings that require balancing competing
properties. In antibiotic discovery, MolRSI developed a screening workﬂow balancing antibacterial activity,
cytotoxicity and structural novelty, achieving an experimental hit rate of 15.9%, compared with 8.7% for a
previous expert-led workﬂow using the same training data and screening library. In de novo peptide–MHC
binder design, MolRSI developed workﬂows that jointly consider target binding and speciﬁcity, producing
eight experimentally validated hits against MART-1 and SARS-CoV-1 pMHCs with preferential binding over
tested oﬀ-targets while Claude Science completely failed. These ﬁndings demonstrate how experience-guided
workﬂow optimization can address competing discovery objectives while improving the search strategies
used in subsequent campaigns.

Introduction
Molecular discovery requires searching vast chemical and biological spaces for candidates that satisfy multiple
competing objectives, including potency, binding speciﬁcity, toxicity, structural novelty, and stability [1, 2, 3, 4].
These demands have been traditionally addressed by experimental workﬂows comprised of library preparation,
screening, counter-screening, and hit validation [1, 2]. Recently, computational workﬂows have shown great
success in molecular design [5, 2, 3]. They often involve diﬀerent molecular representations, model architectures,
training objectives, and optimization strategies with the available data [6, 7]. They must also be integrated
with evaluation and selection procedures that reﬂect the discovery objectives [1, 2]. Virtual screening, for
example, requires jointly considering predictions of activity [8] and toxicity alongside structural constraints
[1, 9], while protein design requires the coordination of target preparation, backbone generation [5], sequence

determine which candidates advance to experimental testing [1, 3], making task-speciﬁc model and workﬂow
optimization central to successful discovery.
Finding an eﬀective workﬂow for a given task is a challenging search problem [10, 11]. The growing diversity
of molecular representations, model architectures, training strategies, and optimization strategies creates a
vast space of possible conﬁgurations whose performance varies across biological targets, available data, and
desired properties (Figure 1a) [6, 3, 7]. Navigating this space requires an eﬀective search policy for designing
and training models, evaluating candidate workﬂows, and deciding how to customize them [12, 13]. For human
scientists, this policy is shaped by experience across projects. Previous successes and failures provide lessons in
workﬂow and model engineering: which architectures suit the available data, which training strategies improve
generalization, and which practical constraints must be addressed. These lessons help researchers identify
strong starting points and prioritize beneﬁcial changes (Figure 1b). As experience accumulates, scientists
can adapt models and workﬂows to new problems with fewer rounds of trial and error. This expertise is
particularly valuable, as the evaluation of each candidate requires expensive wet lab validation. Each project
can contribute not only a workﬂow for a given task but also experience that improves the strategy used to
develop future workﬂows.
Recent advances in scientiﬁc agents have begun to automate aspects of model and workﬂow development that
traditionally depend on human expertise [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 13, 19, 20]. Biomni retrieves relevant tools, databases
and software from a uniﬁed biomedical environment, then uses language model reasoning and code execution
to compose them into workﬂows [21]. In protein design, Claude Science has produced experimentally validated
binders through campaigns guided by expert-written protocols specifying design objectives and evaluation
criteria [22, 23]. Developing and executing these workﬂows produces evidence about which approaches
succeed and which attempts fail [17]. However, the reported studies do not establish how this experience
improves workﬂow development in subsequent problems. When campaigns remain isolated, lessons from one
project cannot inform the search policy for the next. Retaining and applying these lessons across tasks would
allow agents to build on earlier work [24, 25] and develop new workﬂows with less repeated trial and error.
Here we introduce MolRSI, a molecular discovery agent that couples model and workﬂow engineering
with persistent cross-task memory to improve its search policy (Figure 1c). The agent converts scientiﬁc
methods and associated code into validated, executable skills, then uses experience-guided Monte Carlo
tree search (MCTS) to assemble and reﬁne candidate workﬂows, including their model architectures and
training strategies. Inspired by how experienced researchers draw lessons from past projects, MolRSI
records these choices and their outcomes in persistent memory together with the context of each task. Past
workﬂow performance and modiﬁcation outcomes guide selection and reﬁnement on subsequent tasks, while
records of execution failures and veriﬁed repairs help the agent resolve similar problems. Each search adds to
this experience and changes how later models and workﬂows are developed, forming a cycle of recursive
self-improvement (RSI).
We evaluate MolRSI in virtual antibiotic screening and de novo peptide–MHC (pMHC) binder design, two
challenging problems that require balancing competing objectives. These applications test our hypothesis
that systematic optimization of models and workﬂows, guided by experience from previous tasks, can
improve discovery under these constraints. In antibiotic discovery, MolRSI draws on experience from 25
molecular property prediction tasks to develop a customized screening workﬂow balancing antibacterial
activity, cytotoxicity and structural novelty. Experimental testing against Staphylococcus aureus identiﬁes 17
of 107 compounds with minimum inhibitory concentrations of 32µg/mL or lower. The observed hit rate
of 15.9% is substantially higher than the 8.7% achieved by our previous expert-driven workﬂow using the
same training data and screening library [1]. In pMHC binder design, MolRSI draws on experience from 14
minibinder design tasks to develop customized workﬂows for pMHCs. Experimental screening identiﬁes

results highlight the value of accumulated experience and task-speciﬁc optimization for molecular discovery
under competing objectives.

Results
2.1
Overview of MolRSI
MolRSI combines workﬂow search with persistent memory to implement recursive self-improvement (RSI)
for molecular discovery (Figure 1c). Each task speciﬁes a biological problem, available data, an evaluation cri-
terion and a computational budget. An Explore Agent organizes task-relevant scientiﬁc papers and associated
code, tools, benchmarks and algorithms into validated, executable skills. These skills form the resource library
from which MolRSI constructs candidate workﬂows.
MolRSI develops these workﬂows through experience-guided Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS), starting
from conﬁgurations assembled from the resource library or retrieved from solution memory. Its search
policy determines which workﬂows to explore, how to allocate search eﬀort and how to reﬁne and execute
candidates.At each iteration, selection rules draw on workﬂows and scores in solution memory, combining
performance on the current and related tasks with an exploration bonus [26] for less-visited alternatives.
Reﬁnement memory supplies previous modiﬁcations and their outcomes to guide changes to the selected
workﬂow. The agent then executes and evaluates the revised candidate, using execution memory to allocate
resources and resolve errors. Search returns the workﬂow with the best development score within the task’s
computational budget.
Persistent memory carries the outcomes of these searches forward to guide later decisions. Each attempt
updates the stored workﬂows, performance scores, modiﬁcation outcomes and execution records. These
updates change subsequent search decisions within the task and remain available for later tasks. Search
experience can also lead to revised or additional skills. Searches on new tasks therefore use a policy informed
by earlier outcomes and generate further experience that revises the policy applied to subsequent tasks,
completing the RSI cycle (Methods and Sections S1.1 to S1.3).
We assess the contribution of this cross-task policy adaptation by comparing MolRSI with and without RSI.
Both settings use experiences obtained during the current task to guide search, whereas only MolRSI with RSI
also draws on experience from previous tasks. Zero-search transfer tests direct reuse of previously developed
workﬂow conﬁgurations without new search. Model ﬁtting on target training data and evaluation on target
validation data are retained.

enabled the discovery of a new structural class of antibiotics. Developing such workﬂows requires navigating
interdependent choices in molecular representation, model selection and training, screening criteria and can-

MolRSI outperforms existing agents in antibacterial activity prediction benchmarks. Accurate
prediction of antibacterial activity is central to prioritizing compounds for experimental testing. We therefore
benchmarked MolRSI and existing agents on six antibacterial activity prediction tasks, each corresponding to a
diﬀerent bacterial species (Figure 2a). We compared MolRSI with the scientiﬁc research agents Biomni, Claude
Science and STELLA and general-purpose coding agents Codex and Claude Code [28]. For this comparison,
we ﬁrst evaluated MolRSI without RSI, starting each task without experience from previous tasks. This
establishes a baseline for assessing the contribution of RSI. MolRSI develops workﬂows by selecting and
reﬁning combinations of models and methods from the full resource library of 110 reusable skills, comprising
55 models, 48 methods and seven tools or resources (Figure 2b). All agents used the same ﬁve similarity-based
data splits. Mean validation area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC) guides workﬂow selection, and
mean held-out test AUPRC measures predictive performance (Methods and Section S3.1 and supplementary
information S2). MolRSI achieves the highest mean test AUPRC among the evaluated agents, ranking ﬁrst on
four tasks and second on the remaining two.
MolRSI’s performance and search eﬃciency improves as it accumulates experience from more
tasks. We next tested whether experience from other molecular prediction tasks improves the search policy
used to develop antibacterial workﬂows. MolRSI ﬁrst searched independently on 25 source tasks, comprising
22 Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC) [29] tasks and three cytotoxicity tasks for HepG2, HSkMC and IMR-90
cells[1] (Supplementary Table S3). The six antibacterial benchmark tasks were excluded from this experience
pool (Figure 2c). MolRSI used workﬂow performance, reﬁnement outcomes and execution feedback from
these searches to guide workﬂow selection, reﬁnement and execution on the antibacterial tasks (Methods
and Section S3.2). Under the same search budget, MolRSI generally achieves higher mean test AUPRC than
the baseline agents across the search trajectories (Figure 2d). On E. coli and N. gonorrhoeae, its ﬁrst evaluated
workﬂow already outperforms the ﬁnal workﬂows selected by every baseline agent after 20 search iterations.
To assess the contribution of RSI, we compared searches with and without experience from previous tasks
using the same initial resource library, backbone model and search budget (Figure 2e). RSI improves mean
test AUPRC on all six antibacterial tasks. We also included zero-search transfer to test whether previously
developed workﬂows can be applied without further workﬂow optimization. This setting retains model ﬁtting
and validation on target data (Section S3.3). Zero-search transfer outperforms search without RSI on S. aureus,
A. baumannii and K. pneumoniae, achieving the highest mean test AUPRC among the three conditions on K.
pneumoniae. Experience therefore improves subsequent search and, on several targets, provides workﬂows
that outperform search from scratch without further optimization.
We further examined whether the beneﬁts of accumulated experience increase as more tasks contribute to
it (Figure 2f). We increased the number of source tasks contributing experience from 2 to 25 and assessed
workﬂow transfer to the six antibacterial targets. We averaged performance across all combinations containing
the same number of source tasks to ensure that the observed trend is not driven by a particular task order.
The mean test AUPRC of the transferred workﬂows increases from approximately 0.32 with two source
tasks to approximately 0.37 with all 25. With experience from all 25 tasks, this performance exceeds the mean
AUPRC of 0.361 achieved by search without RSI after 20 iterations. These results show that accumulating
experience across more tasks makes increasingly eﬀective workﬂows available for subsequent tasks, extending

MolRSI identiﬁes compounds with potent in vitro activity against MRSA. We next evaluated whether
the screening workﬂow developed by MolRSI can identify antibacterial compounds while balancing activity,
cytotoxicity and structural novelty. We used the same training datasets and screening library of approximately
12.1 million compounds as in our previous expert-driven study [1]. The workﬂow integrates optimized antibac-
terial activity and cytotoxicity predictors with structural ﬁltering and diversity-based selection. Structural
ﬁlters exclude speciﬁed antibiotic cores and compounds similar to known antibiotics, while cytotoxicity pre-
dictions for HepG2, HSkMC and IMR-90 cells inform candidate prioritization. Antibacterial activity ranking
followed by selection across structural clusters yields 107 compounds for experimental testing (Figure 3a;
Methods; Section S3.4).
Experimental validation identiﬁes 17 compounds with minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of 32µg/mL
or lower against methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA). The hits occupy multiple regions of the chemical
space (Figure 3b). Among these hits, C3 and C32 also exhibit potent activity against methicillin-resistant
S. aureus (MRSA), with MICs of 1 and 8µg/mL, respectively. C3 and C32 are ranked at 28th and 5th by
predicted antibacterial activity among the tested compounds (Figure 3c). Both compounds inhibit MRSA at
concentrations that do not cause cytotoxicity in each of the three types of human cells. Their half-maximal
cytotoxic concentrations (CC50) range from 6.4 to 17.5µg/mL for C3 and from 24.2 to 54.7µg/mL for C32.
These measurements establish a separation between antibacterial potency and mammalian cytotoxicity under
the tested conditions (Figure 3d,g; Section S3.6).
MolRSI improves experimental hit rate and antibacterial activity prediction over the previous
expert-driven workﬂow. The 17 hits among 107 tested compounds correspond to an experimental hit rate
of 15.9%, compared with 8.7% (21 of 241 compounds) in the previous expert-driven study at the same MSSA
MIC threshold [1]. Thus, using the same training datasets and screening library, the workﬂow developed by
MolRSI achieves an approximately 82% relative increase in observed hit rate (Figure 3e; Section S3.6.3).
To assess predictive performance independently of diﬀerences in compound selection, we compared the
antibacterial activity predictors on a shared held-out set of 271 compounds experimentally evaluated in the
previous study (Section S3.1). MolRSI achieves an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve
(AUROC) of 0.828, compared with 0.590 for the previous predictor, and an area under the precision–recall
curve (AUPRC) of 0.300, compared with 0.090 (Figure 3f). The improved predictive discrimination on a
common test set complements the higher prospective hit rate, demonstrating gains in both activity prediction
and the yield of experimentally active compounds.

3
Discussion
MolRSI improves molecular-discovery workﬂows through recursive self-improvement of the search policy
used to develop them. Performance on related tasks changes the selection scores that determine where the
agent directs further exploration. Current-task evaluations revise these priorities, while earlier modiﬁcations
and execution outcomes guide reﬁnement and repair. The policy therefore changes throughout workﬂow
development and carries the eﬀects of earlier searches into subsequent tasks. Those searches produce new
results that revise later decisions, completing the recursive process. This links two levels of improvement. At
the task level, the agent selects and reﬁnes computational methods to produce an eﬀective workﬂow. Across
tasks, the results of these attempts change how it approaches the next search.
The computational comparisons show that adapting this policy helps the agent make better use of a ﬁxed
search budget. Under matched conditions, incorporating prior-task results improves the workﬂows selected
for antibacterial prediction and pMHC ranking. The antibacterial search trajectories also show that eﬀective
workﬂows can be found early, sometimes exceeding the ﬁnal performance of baseline searches at the ﬁrst
evaluation. The resulting workﬂows can also remain useful beyond their original tasks. Direct transfer
outperforms search from scratch on several antibacterial targets, providing an additional beneﬁt of earlier
workﬂow development. The retrospective pool analysis further identiﬁes better available workﬂows as
experience covers more source tasks. Together, these ﬁndings show how earlier searches can supply eﬀective
conﬁgurations and improve the policy used to develop new ones.
The experimental studies demonstrate why improved workﬂow development matters for molecular discovery.
In antibiotic discovery, the MolRSI workﬂow achieves a higher observed hit rate than the previous expert-
driven workﬂow using the same training data and screening library [1]. Evaluation on a shared held-out
compound set supports the predictive value of the workﬂow alongside its ability to prioritize active com-
pounds for testing. The pMHC application extends the framework to design, where selecting candidates
requires considering both target binding and discrimination against related oﬀ-targets. The ranking workﬂow
developed with prior experience guides subsequent generation and selection, yielding binders with preferential
binding to their intended complexes over tested oﬀ-targets. The workﬂow developed by Claude Science yields
no hits under the same screening conditions. These experiments assess complete workﬂows that coordinate
several computational methods. Their outcomes show the practical value of improving how such methods are
selected, combined and reﬁned before candidates advance to experimental testing.
These results also suggest how scientiﬁc agents that improve through use should be assessed. A successful ﬁnal
workﬂow establishes performance on a particular biological problem. Policy improvement concerns how the
agent conducts subsequent searches, including which alternatives it explores and how eﬀectively it uses the
available budget. Comparisons between search with prior experience, search from scratch and direct transfer
help distinguish the value of inherited conﬁgurations from the beneﬁt of further workﬂow development. This
makes the decisions involved in developing workﬂows open to systematic evaluation. Which methods are
combined, which modiﬁcations are pursued and which attempts fail all provide evidence that can change later
12


search priorities. Such evidence complements the scientiﬁc knowledge embodied in existing models and tools.
Future benchmarks could assess how eﬀectively an agent adapts its search policy over successive tasks.
Further progress depends on whether policy updates remain useful as biological targets and evaluation criteria
change. The variation among experience pools highlights the importance of the contributing tasks, while
current-task evaluation provides a way to reassess priorities inherited from earlier searches. Biological
validation remains necessary to establish whether better computational scores lead to more eﬀective molecular
candidates. Future systems could incorporate experimental outcomes into policy updates, allowing laboratory
successes and failures to inﬂuence which workﬂows receive further exploration. This would connect the
improvement of search strategies more closely to the biological goals of discovery. More broadly, MolRSI
shows that scientiﬁc experience can be transferred between related tasks to improve the policy that guides
workﬂow development. This provides a basis for discovery systems that reﬁne their search strategies through
use, with each project contributing to how future problems are approached.
4
Methods
4.1
MolRSI framework
4.1.1
Resource library construction
MolRSI uses an Explore Agent to build the resource library. The agent searches for scientiﬁc papers and
their associated code repositories, tools, benchmarks and algorithms relevant to the task. It uses the task
description and data characteristics to formulate queries about data representations, model architectures,
training objectives and domain constraints. For each retrieved resource, the Explore Agent creates a reusable
skill describing how to use it. Each skill speciﬁes the resource’s purpose, software dependencies, input and
output requirements and execution steps, with references to available code, documentation and usage examples.
The agent checks each skill’s syntax, dependencies and interfaces during library construction. When a newly
constructed skill is ﬁrst used for a task, the agent runs a usage example in an isolated environment. If execution
fails, it uses the error messages and repository information to revise the code, dependencies or environment
settings, then reruns the example. Skills that pass execution validation are tagged as validated and retained for
reuse. Skills that still fail after repair are removed, so that only validated skills are available for subsequent
tasks. Full implementation details are given in Section S1.1.
4.1.2
Persistent memory
MolRSI retains experience from workﬂow development and evaluation in solution, reﬁnement and execution
memory. These memories are updated during each task and retained for use in later tasks. MolRSI uses the
accumulated experience to guide later iterations of the same search and searches on new tasks. Experience
from previous tasks could also be used to select an existing workﬂow without a new search, a mode referred
to as zero-search transfer.
Solution memory. Solution memory stores each workﬂow’s executable code, conﬁguration, execution status
and any available development scores. Development scores are calculated using the task-speciﬁc evaluation
criterion to compare workﬂows during search. The conﬁguration speciﬁes the component methods, data
processing, model architecture, parameter settings and training or generation procedures. Each score is
stored with the task, dataset, evaluation metric, and data split and random seed identiﬁers where applicable.
MolRSI uses the stored workﬂows and scores to select workﬂows for further reﬁnement on the current task
or transfer to a new task.
13


Reﬁnement memory. Reﬁnement memory stores the original and revised workﬂows, the changes made and
the reasons for proposing them. For revisions that complete evaluation, it also stores the development scores
before and after the change, measured under the same task-speciﬁc evaluation protocol, and their diﬀerence.
Modiﬁcations are retained whether performance improves, declines or remains unchanged. Revisions that
fail during execution are stored with their failure status. MolRSI uses these previous modiﬁcations and their
outcomes when proposing further changes on the current task or related tasks.
Execution memory. Execution memory stores the computational requirements of workﬂows and the skills
available in the resource library. Runtime, GPU time, processing speed and GPU memory use are stored
together with the dataset, dataset size, hardware conﬁguration and software environment. MolRSI uses these
measurements and their associated execution conditions to plan computing resources for later runs. When
execution fails, execution memory stores the stage at which the failure occurs, the error messages and logs,
and the cause when identiﬁed. It also stores each attempted repair, including changes to code, dependencies,
environment settings or resource allocation, and whether the repair succeeds. When similar errors occur in
the same or later tasks, MolRSI retrieves the previous repairs and their outcomes to guide further debugging.
Details of memory storage and updates are given in Section S1.2.
4.1.3
Recursive self-improvement of the search policy
MolRSI uses experience from its own workﬂow searches to improve its search policy. Evaluation results
from the current task and experience retained in persistent memory guide which workﬂows to explore, which
modiﬁcations to pursue and how to resolve execution failures. Each attempt provides further evidence about
workﬂow performance, the eﬀects of modiﬁcations and execution requirements. This experience informs
subsequent search decisions within and across tasks, completing the feedback loop through which recursive
self-improvement occurs.
Workﬂow search uses experience-guided Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) [26]. Initial workﬂows form the
roots of separate trees, with each node representing a workﬂow. A workﬂow selected for modiﬁcation is the
parent, and a revised version is its child. Workﬂows based on a common model or computational approach
are grouped into families. Each search iteration comprises the following ﬁve steps:
Step 1: Selecting workﬂow families. MolRSI selects a workﬂow family using current task performance,
an exploration bonus and a historical transfer score. For families already evaluated on the current task, the
performance term uses the best development score obtained in earlier iterations. The exploration bonus [26]
is larger for less visited families. The historical transfer score summarizes the family’s performance on related
tasks using solution memory. Historical experience guides initial exploration, and current task scores are
incorporated as evaluations become available. The family with the highest combined score is selected. The
selection rule and historical transfer score calculations are given in Section S1.3.
Step 2: Selecting parent workﬂows. MolRSI samples a parent workﬂow from those already evaluated on
the current task. Sampling favours better development performance, penalizes workﬂows that have already
produced more candidates, and incorporates historical transfer scores from solution memory. The sampling
rule and eligibility criteria are given in Section S1.3.
Step 3: Proposing workﬂow modiﬁcations. MolRSI retrieves previous modiﬁcations and their outcomes
on related tasks from reﬁnement memory. It uses these modiﬁcations and outcomes to propose changes to the
selected parent workﬂow, such as replacing a method, changing how components are combined or adjusting
parameter settings. Each proposal speciﬁes the changes and their rationale. Before execution, MolRSI checks
that the proposed workﬂow follows the required format, does not duplicate an existing candidate and is
feasible to run.
14


Step 4: Executing and evaluating workﬂows. Using the resource requirements stored in execution
memory, MolRSI conﬁgures the runtime environment and allocates computing resources. It then runs the
revised workﬂow and assesses its performance using the task-speciﬁc evaluation procedure. If execution
fails, MolRSI ﬁrst searches execution memory for similar errors and the repairs that have resolved them.
When relevant experience is available, it uses the previous repair to guide changes to the code, dependencies
or environment settings. Otherwise, it diagnoses the problem from the current error messages and makes
the necessary changes. The workﬂow is rerun after each repair. If execution still fails, MolRSI uses the new
error messages to guide further changes, up to a predeﬁned limit on repair attempts and within the task’s
computational limits. Successful evaluations provide development scores for subsequent search iterations.
Step 5: Updating search policy to guide subsequent searches. After each execution attempt, MolRSI
stores the workﬂow, its execution status and any available development score in solution memory. It adds
the modiﬁcation and its outcome to reﬁnement memory and stores runtime, resource use, errors and repair
outcomes in execution memory. MolRSI then uses the updated memories to select families and parents,
propose modiﬁcations, allocate resources and repair execution failures in later iterations (Steps 1–4).
We denote the eﬀective policy governing the decisions in Steps 1–4 by πt(a | s), the distribution over search
actions a for the current task and search state s. Let Mt denote persistent memory after t updates and Et the
new execution and evaluation experience. The policy before and after a memory update is expressed as
πt(a | s) = π(a | s; Mt),
Mt+1 = Update(Mt, Et),
πt+1(a | s) = π(a | s; Mt+1).
(1)
The updated policy guides further searches, whose results supply experience for the next memory update
(Figure 1). This feedback loop implements recursive self-improvement of the eﬀective search policy. MolRSI
uses the outcomes of its own searches to focus exploration on promising workﬂows, reduce repeated unsuc-
cessful modiﬁcations and reuse eﬀective repairs. The same process operates within a task and across tasks as
experience accumulates. Search stops when the task-speciﬁc limit on evaluation attempts or computation
time is reached. MolRSI returns the successfully evaluated workﬂow with the best development score on the
current task.
MolRSI uses experience from previous tasks to select a workﬂow for direct transfer or as the starting point
for a new search. It ﬁrst identiﬁes the best matching previously completed task, referred to as the source
task, using task type, evaluation metric, dataset size and task description similarity. It then retrieves the
workﬂow with the best development score on that source task from solution memory. In zero-search mode,
the retrieved conﬁguration is applied to the new task without searching over alternative workﬂows. Model
parameters are ﬁtted using the new task’s training data where required. Alternatively, the retrieved workﬂow
serves as the starting point for further search using Steps 1–5, within the task-speciﬁc limits on evaluation
attempts or computation time.
4.2
MolRSI for antibiotic discovery
4.2.1
Datasets and benchmark evaluation
Source tasks for experience accumulation. We used 25 source tasks to accumulate experience for antibiotic
workﬂow development: 22 absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity tasks from the
Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC) [29], together with three cytotoxicity tasks for HepG2, HSkMC and
15


IMR-90 cells from our previous study[1]. The TDC tasks used the oﬃcial benchmark partitions and task-
speciﬁc evaluation metrics. The cytotoxicity datasets were obtained from our previous study, with the same
compound structures and experimental measurements. Each cytotoxicity dataset was divided into training,
validation and test sets at proportions of 8:1:1 using molecular similarity, with a Tanimoto threshold of 0.40.
Five splits were generated using diﬀerent random seeds.
Antibacterial target tasks. We evaluated MolRSI on six antibacterial activity prediction tasks for Staphylo-
coccus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae and
Neisseria gonorrhoeae. These tasks were excluded from the source experience pool. The datasets were obtained
from our previous study, with the same compound structures and experimental measurements. Each dataset
used ﬁve similarity-based training, validation and test splits with a Tanimoto threshold of 0.40, proportions
of 8:1:1 and the same ﬁve random seeds used for the cytotoxicity tasks. All methods used identical splits. Each
task involved a single workﬂow search, during which every candidate was ﬁtted and evaluated across all ﬁve
splits. Mean validation AUPRC guided the search, and the workﬂow with the highest value was selected. Test
AUPRC is reported as the mean and sample standard deviation across the ﬁve splits. Test outcomes were not
used to guide workﬂow search.
Activity S. aureus held-out test set. For the predictive comparison in Figure 3f, we used a held-out set of 271
compounds from our previous study [1]. This set comprised 241 candidate compounds and 30 low predicted
activity controls; the 12 known antibiotic class positive controls were excluded. MolRSI and the published
antibacterial activity model were evaluated on the same compounds using the same experimental activity
labels. AUROC and AUPRC are reported for both models.
Dataset sizes and evaluation metrics for all datasets are provided in Section S3.1, together with the split sizes
and random seeds used for workﬂow development.
We benchmarked MolRSI with RSI and MolRSI without RSI against Biomni, Claude Science, STELLA, Codex
and Claude Code on the six antibacterial target tasks (Figure 2). All agents received the same task prompt,
datasets and development objective, with up to 20 attempted workﬂow evaluations per task. Each agent
retained its native tools and workﬂow development procedures. MolRSI and all agent baselines except Codex
used Claude Sonnet 5; Codex used GPT-5.5. MolRSI accessed its backbone model through the Anthropic API,
whereas Biomni ran on its own platform. Complete task prompts, model identiﬁers, API settings and baseline
conﬁgurations are provided in Section S2.2.
4.2.2
Evaluation of recursive self-improvement
MolRSI searched for workﬂows independently on the 25 source tasks described in Section 4.2.1. Experience
from these searches was pooled in persistent memory before evaluation on the antibacterial target tasks.
We compared MolRSI with and without RSI. MolRSI with RSI began with the accumulated source memory,
whereas MolRSI without RSI began without experience from previous tasks. Both conditions used the same
resource library and backbone model, together with the data partitions, evaluation procedure and workﬂow
evaluation budget speciﬁed in Section 4.2.1. Memory was updated during each search to guide subsequent
iterations. Experience generated on one antibacterial task was not passed to the other antibacterial tasks.
For the search trajectories in Figure 2d, we identiﬁed the workﬂow with the highest mean validation AUPRC
obtained so far at each iteration. Its test performance was calculated after the search to construct the plotted
trajectory.
For zero-search transfer (Figure 2e), MolRSI ﬁrst identiﬁed the best matching source task using task type,
evaluation metric, dataset size and task description similarity. It then selected the workﬂow with the best
validation performance on that source task. Both selections were made before evaluating the workﬂow on the
16


antibacterial task. Model parameters were ﬁtted using the target task’s training data where required, and the
transferred workﬂow was evaluated without further workﬂow search.
We compared the transfer performance available from diﬀerent numbers of source tasks with the performance
of MolRSI without RSI after workﬂow search (Figure 2f). One workﬂow from each of the 25 source tasks was
transferred to each antibacterial task, yielding 150 source target evaluations. Experience pools contained 2–25

and these values were averaged across the six tasks. The mean was calculated over all source task combinations
of each size, with variation among combinations estimated from 2,000 random orderings of the source tasks.
The transfer evaluations and calculation of means and percentile ranges are described in Section S3.2.
4.2.3
Virtual screening and compound selection
MolRSI uses the experience accumulated across source tasks to guide the development of a virtual screening
workﬂow for identifying candidate antibiotics against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). The
workﬂow combines antibacterial activity prediction with cytotoxicity prediction for HepG2, HSkMC and
IMR-90 cells. The activity models were trained on methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA; strain RN4220).
All activity and cytotoxicity models used the same training datasets as our previous study [1] and both studies
used the same screening library.
Screening began with structural ﬁltering. Compounds with a maximum Tanimoto similarity of 0.40 or greater
to the antibacterial reference set were excluded. Structural ﬁltering also removed compounds matching PAINS
[42] or Brenk alerts [43] and speciﬁed antibiotic cores. Cytotoxicity was then predicted separately for the
three cell types. The HepG2 and IMR-90 predictors combined MiniMol embeddings [44] with Morgan ﬁnger-
prints [45] and RDKit descriptors [46], whereas the HSkMC predictor used MiniMol embeddings. Cytotoxicity
ﬁltering used a score threshold of 0.40 for each cell type. Model architectures, reference compounds and ﬁlter
settings are described in Section S3.4.
The remaining compounds were ranked by a weighted combination of predictions from two MiniMol-
based variants and a LANTERN–RADR ensemble, with weights of 0.55, 0.30 and 0.15, respectively. Each
component prediction was averaged across ﬁve ﬁtted models. Butina clustering [47] grouped structurally
similar compounds, and candidates were selected across clusters to retain chemical diversity.
We selected and experimentally tested 107 compounds. Activity against MRSA was assessed during experimen-
tal validation. Compound identities, prediction scores and sourcing details, including supplier substitutions
and exceptions to the cytotoxicity criterion, are provided in Section S3.4.
4.2.4
Experimental validation
Antibacterial activity assays. Antibacterial activity was measured against methicillin-susceptible Staphylo-
coccus aureus RN4220 (MSSA; DSM 26309) and methicillin-resistant S. aureus USA300 FPR3757 (MRSA; ATCC
BAA-1556). Compounds were ﬁrst screened at a single dose of 128 µg mL−1, and those that inhibited growth
were advanced to two-fold dose–response testing from 128 µg mL−1 (8–9 concentrations, to 1 or 0.5 µg mL−1).
Overnight LB cultures grown from single colonies were diluted to approximately 105 CFU mL−1 in fresh
LB and added to plates containing compound in DMSO (ﬁnal DMSO ≤1%). Plates were covered with a lid
and incubated for 18–24 h at 37 ◦C with 5% CO2. Growth was measured as OD600. The minimum inhibitory
concentration (MIC) was deﬁned as the lowest concentration giving ≥90% growth inhibition relative to
untreated growth controls, and compounds with an MSSA MIC ≤32 µg mL−1 were deﬁned as hits. DMSO
vehicle, untreated growth and Triton X-100 kill controls were included. MICs were determined in technical
replicate (n = 2-3) in a single experiment, and the higher replicate value was reported.
17


Cytotoxicity assays. Cytotoxicity was measured in human hepatocellular carcinoma (HepG2; ATCC HB-
8065), primary human skeletal muscle (HSkMC; ATCC PCS-950-010) and human fetal lung ﬁbroblast (IMR-90;
ATCC CCL-186) cells. Cells were seeded at 5–10 × 103 cells per well in 96-well clear ﬂat-bottom black
tissue-culture-treated plates (100 µL per well) and incubated for 24 h at 37 ◦C and 5% CO2 before dosing.
Compounds were added as a two-fold dilution series from 128 to 0.5 µg mL−1, with 1% DMSO at the highest
concentration diluted in step with compound, and cells were incubated for 48–72 h. Viability was quantiﬁed
with PrestoBlue HS (10 µL per well, 4 h at 37 ◦C) by ﬂuorescence (excitation/emission, 550/590 nm). Untreated
cells deﬁned 100% viability, with a matched DMSO series and Triton X-100 (0.1%) lysis wells as vehicle
and ﬂoor controls and media-only wells as background. The half-maximal cytotoxic concentration (CC50)
was calculated by log-linear interpolation between the two concentrations bracketing 50% viability. Each
compound and concentration was tested in two technical wells in a single experiment.
4.3
MolRSI for peptide–MHC binder design
4.3.1
Datasets and benchmark evaluation
The protein study instantiated the cross-task update framework in three stages. First, ranking workﬂow
development on 14 miniprotein-binder tasks supplied protein-domain source experience. Second, MolRSI
used the resulting experience memory to develop workﬂows for one pMHC binder design task. Third,
the selected pMHC workﬂow guided prospective binder design workﬂow optimization for MART-1 and
SARS-CoV-1 pMHC complexes.
Miniprotein-binder tasks. We assembled experimentally annotated de novo miniprotein-binder datasets for
14 protein targets from three published studies [37, 38, 3]. Each target deﬁned a task using a leave-one-target-out
partition: workﬂow search used the other 13 targets, and the selected workﬂow was then evaluated on the
held-out target. We retained all 1,565 positive designs and sampled up to ten negatives per positive within each
target, yielding 17,215 designs. Target identities, sample counts and label deﬁnitions are reported in Section S4.1.
pMHC task. The pMHC benchmark comprised three target datasets: two for gp100 and one for MART-1.
Both gp100 datasets were used directly for workﬂow search, while MART-1 was reserved for testing. Each
candidate was represented as a four-chain complex comprising the HLA heavy chain, β2-microglobulin, the
presented peptide and the designed binder. The datasets were generated using the experimental screening
procedure described by Liu et al. [2]. Peptide identities, dataset sizes and label deﬁnitions are reported in
Section S4.1.
Ranking metrics. We evaluated candidate rankings using the area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC),
the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and the Enrichment Factor (EF). EF
measures the positive fraction among top-ranked candidates relative to the positive fraction in the full target
dataset. For a target with N candidates and N+ experimentally positive binders, let nf be the number retained
at the top-ranked fraction f and n+,f the number of positives among them. We calculated
EFf = n+,f/nf

We denote these metrics as EF@1%, EF@2%, EF@5% and EF@10%, respectively. Random ranking has an
expected EF of 1. At each cutoﬀ, macro EF is the unweighted average across the 14 miniprotein tasks (Figure 4a).
For the pMHC benchmark, EF is reported separately for the held-out MART-1 target (Figure 4c). Full EF results
at all four cutoﬀs and AUPRC results are reported in Section S4.3. Comparisons with ﬁxed expert-deﬁned
scoring criteria are provided in Section S4.2.

Design targets. We designed binders for HLA-A*02:01 complexes presenting MART-1 (ELAGIGILTV) and
SARS-CoV1 (GLMWLSYFV). Each intended pMHC was paired with an oﬀ-target complex presenting a diﬀerent
peptide while retaining the same HLA heavy chain and β2-microglobulin. Target sequences and exposed
peptide hotspots were derived from source structures. The source coordinates were not supplied as design
inputs. Oﬀ-target peptide sequences, source structures and hotspot deﬁnitions are given in Supplementary
Table S14.
4.3.2
Evaluation of recursive self-improvement
Resource library and workﬂow search. MolRSI selected and conﬁgured components from the protein
resource library, which contained 65 executable skills comprising 28 models and 37 registered scoring terms
(Figure 4b). For ranking workﬂow development, these components covered input and feature preparation,
complex-structure prediction, sequence and interface scoring, and score aggregation. Each candidate workﬂow
mapped a target–binder pair to a scalar ranking score. AUPRC on development data from the 13 non-held-out
targets guided workﬂow search and selection within each miniprotein-binder task, whereas AUROC guided
search and selection for the single pMHC task.
Benchmark baselines and budget. We compared MolRSI with Biomni [21], Claude Science [34], STELLA [35]
and Codex [36]. For each benchmark task, all agents received the same task inputs, data partitions, and
evaluation objective, with a budget of 300 A100 GPU-hours and 20 attempted workﬂow evaluations. Each
agent retained its native tools and workﬂow-development procedures. Folded structures and the scores
produced by each model were cached and reused across workﬂow evaluations to avoid repeated computation.
Task instructions and baseline conﬁgurations are provided in Supplementary Information S2 and section S2.3.
Memory ablation. We compared pMHC workﬂow search initialized with pooled experience from the 14
miniprotein-binder tasks against search without this prior experience. The initial memory contained no
pMHC or prospective design outcomes. Both conditions used identical resource libraries, candidate data,
search controllers, evaluation objectives and procedures, and the per-task budget speciﬁed above. Memory
was updated during search in both conditions, so the comparison isolated the contribution of prior cross-task
experience.
4.3.3
Binder generation and candidate selection
Generation-workﬂow optimization. The prospective pipeline combined candidate generation, hotspot-
contact ﬁltering, inverse folding and ranking. MolRSI optimized generation methods and conﬁgurations
separately for each target, using RFDiﬀusion3 [48], Proteina-Complexa [49], PXDesign [50], BoltzGen [51]
and the MOSAIC [52] simplex accelerated proximal gradient method (APGM). Each target-speciﬁc search
received up to 30 attempted rounds and 1,000 A100 GPU-hours. The searches ran in parallel without sharing
evaluations or search records.
For each evaluated conﬁguration, we generated 20 backbones with binder lengths of 80–120 residues. Sol-
ubleMPNN [53] generated eight sequences per retained backbone, with cysteine excluded from the sequence
alphabet. The ranking procedure selected in the memory-guided pMHC search remained ﬁxed throughout
prospective search and candidate selection. The mean score among the top-ranked 5% of generated candidates
served as the conﬁguration-selection objective.
Final candidate generation and selection. After each target-speciﬁc search, the three highest-scoring
workﬂows each generated 8,000 additional designs. Candidates from the search and expansion stages were
pooled and deduplicated. Final MolRSI candidates were required to satisfy on_target_ipsae_min ≥0.50,
monomer_plddt ≥0.80, at least 15 HLA contacts (hla_res_contact ≥15), and son −soff > 0. Passing
19


designs were ranked by the on-target frozen-veriﬁer score son. Experimental outcomes are reported separately
over evaluable designs, as deﬁned below. Detailed generation and selection settings are provided in Section S4.4.
The Claude Science protocol is described in Sections S2.3 and S4.5.
4.3.4
Experimental validation
Biotinylated pMHC monomers were purchased from KACTUS Bio and comprised HLA-A*02:01 (Gly25–
Thr305), β2-microglobulin (Ile21–Met119) and the MART-1 (ELAGIGILTV) and SARS-CoV-1 (GLMWLSYFV)
peptide. Binder designs were synthesized by Twist Biosciences and cloned into engineered M13 bacteriophage
lacking gIII as previously described [54]. Phage libraries were then selected for speciﬁc binding activity
using host E. coli cells encoding positive selection circuits for on-target pMHC binding activity and negative
selection circuits for oﬀ-target pMHC binding using an adaptation of previously described methods [55, 56].
Next generation sequencing of phage-encoded designs was used to assess enrichment of functional designs
throughout selection.

Figure S1: Contents and uses of persistent memory. Solution memory links a workﬂow’s code, conﬁguration and
development performance to its task and evaluation conditions. This information supports workﬂow-family selection,
parent selection and workﬂow transfer. Reﬁnement memory links original and revised workﬂows to the proposed
changes, their rationale and observed outcomes. Previous modiﬁcations, including unsuccessful ones, inform subsequent
proposals. Execution memory links runtime measurements and failures to the conditions under which they occurred,
the repairs attempted and the results of rerunning the workﬂow. It supports resource allocation and reuse of successful
repairs. Examples illustrate the types of information stored, rather than new performance measurements. The same
memories inform later iterations within a task and searches on subsequent tasks, subject to the evaluation protocol. DILI,
drug-induced liver injury; AUROC, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve; MLP, multilayer perceptron.
IA3 denotes a parameter-eﬃcient ﬁne-tuning method [1].

Resource library construction
Resource retrieval and synthesis. The Explore Agent generates task-speciﬁc queries about scientiﬁc

relevance to the task. The retrieved material is summarized to identify models, methods, tools, benchmarks
and associated implementations for skill construction.
Resource analysis and skill construction. The Explore Agent examines the retrieved resources and their
available code, documentation and usage examples. It prioritizes resources for skill construction according to
task relevance and the requested skill categories. When repository code is available, static analysis of Python
abstract syntax trees recovers public interfaces and their arguments. These interfaces, together with model
deﬁnitions and training or inference examples, inform the skill instructions. Additional searches supply
supporting repositories or documentation when the initial material is incomplete.
Library organization and skill contents. A shared index lists each skill’s name, category and purpose. Each
skill describes how to use the resource and links these instructions to available implementations, dependencies,
usage examples and source references. Where relevant, it also speciﬁes feature representations and whether
computed features could be reused. The contents are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.
Checks during construction. The Explore Agent checks package identity and examines Python usage
examples for syntax and import-name inconsistencies. When an execution environment is available, it also
checks dependency availability and selected interfaces. Detected problems prompt revisions to installation
instructions, dependencies or usage examples. These checks precede execution validation when a skill is ﬁrst
used.
Execution validation and reuse. When a newly constructed skill is ﬁrst used, MolRSI runs a usage example
in an isolated environment. Execution errors guide repairs to the code, dependencies or environment settings.
The example is rerun until it completes successfully or reaches the repair-attempt limit. Skills that pass are
tagged as validated and retained in the resource library for reuse. Skills that remain unsuccessful are removed.
Incremental expansion. The Explore Agent could expand an existing resource library by retrieving addi-
tional resources and identifying skills not already present. Existing skills are retained. New skills undergo the
same construction checks and ﬁrst-use execution validation, and the shared index is updated to include them.

execution. MolRSI retains this experience in solution, reﬁnement and execution memory, together with the
tasks and conditions under which it was obtained. These memories guide later search decisions within and

data processing, training objective and training or generation procedure. Development scores are retained
with their evaluation metric and, where applicable, data split and random seed. A workﬂow that failed before
evaluation retains its failure status without receiving a development score. Scores from completed workﬂows
provide evidence about which families performed well on related tasks and which individual workﬂows are

S1.3
Recursive self-improvement of the search policy
Search representation. MolRSI uses experience-guided Monte Carlo tree search over a forest of executable
workﬂows. Initial workﬂows form the roots of separate trees, and each node represents a concrete workﬂow
with its speciﬁcation, execution status, development scores, resource use, logs and generated artifacts. A
workﬂow selected for modiﬁcation is the parent, and the revised workﬂow is its child. An edge represents the
proposed changes, such as a diﬀerent representation, architecture, objective, data-processing procedure or
composition of methods. Workﬂows based on a common model or computational approach are grouped into
families. The ﬁve steps below specify how current-task observations and historical experience guide search
decisions.

Step 3: Proposing workﬂow modiﬁcations. The agent retrieves previous modiﬁcations, their rationales
and their outcomes from reﬁnement memory to guide changes to the selected parent workﬂow. Each
proposal speciﬁes the changes and their rationale. The proposed workﬂow is checked for the required format,
duplication of an existing candidate and feasibility of execution. Malformed, duplicate or infeasible proposals
do not become completed workﬂow nodes. The proposed modiﬁcation and its observed outcome are added
to reﬁnement memory after execution, including changes that reduced performance or failed to execute.
Step 4: Executing and evaluating workﬂows. The proposed child workﬂow is implemented and run in an
isolated environment using the task-speciﬁc evaluation procedure. Execution memory informs environment
settings and resource allocation. If setup or execution fails, the agent ﬁrst searches execution memory for
similar errors and successful repairs. Relevant experience guides changes to the code, dependencies or
environment settings. If no suitable repair is available or the problem persists, the agent diagnoses the current
failure and proposes further changes. The workﬂow is rerun after each repair, within the repair-attempt
limit and computational budget. Successful evaluations supply development scores; all attempts retain their
execution status, logs, resource measurements and repair outcomes. Failed evaluations count toward the
workﬂow-evaluation allowance, but repairs of the same candidate do not consume additional evaluations.
Step 5: Updating search policy to guide subsequent searches. After each attempt, MolRSI adds the
workﬂow and its development score or failure status to solution memory. It stores the proposed modiﬁcation
and its outcome in reﬁnement memory, and runtime, resource use, errors and repair outcomes in execution
memory. The corresponding node and edge in the search forest are also updated. The updated memories
guide subsequent family selection, parent sampling, modiﬁcation proposals and execution. These updates
change the eﬀective search policy πt, whose subsequent evaluations generate experience for the next memory
update. This feedback implements the RSI loop expressed by Mt, Et and πt in Equation (1).
Historical and current task updates. Current task executions update development scores, visit counts and
the experience used for subsequent reﬁnement and execution. When the source memory was ﬁxed for an
evaluation, its historical transfer scores remained unchanged during that target-task search. Under continued
use across tasks, experience from newly completed tasks could contribute to transfer estimates for later
searches. The bounded transfer scores serve as empirical selection priors; their boundedness alone does not
establish a standard target-task or node-level regret guarantee.
Search termination and returned workﬂow. Search stops when the task-speciﬁc limit on evaluation
attempts or computation time is reached. MolRSI returns the successfully evaluated workﬂow with the best
current-task development score. This is the best evaluated workﬂow under the stated criterion and budget,
rather than a global optimum. The evaluation unit and accounting for failed attempts and repairs are speciﬁed
in Supplementary Information S2.
Workﬂow transfer with and without further search. MolRSI could use experience from previous tasks to
retrieve a workﬂow for direct transfer or initialize a new search. It ﬁrst identiﬁes the best matching previously
completed task using task type, evaluation metric, dataset size and task description similarity. It then retrieves
that source task’s workﬂow with the best development score from solution memory. This source task routing
is distinct from the family- and workﬂow-level transfer scores used in Steps 1 and 2. In zero-search mode, the
retrieved conﬁguration is applied to the new task without proposing or comparing alternative workﬂows.
Model parameters are ﬁtted using the new task’s training data where required, and ﬁtting and execution still
incur computational cost. Alternatively, the retrieved workﬂow becomes a starting point for Steps 1–5 under
the new task’s limits on evaluation attempts and computation time.

S3.2
Evaluation of recursive self-improvement
Source experience. MolRSI developed workﬂows independently on each of the 25 source tasks and pooled
the resulting experience in solution, reﬁnement and execution memory. Each antibacterial target began with
the same pooled source experience. Experience generated during a target search guided later iterations of that
search and was not shared with the other targets.
Resource library and RSI conditions. The small-molecule resource library contained 110 reusable skills:
55 models, 48 methods and seven tools or resources. These covered molecular standardization, feature
construction, predictive models, class-imbalance handling, calibration, stacking and ensembling. MolRSI
with RSI and MolRSI without RSI used the same resource library, backbone model, data partitions, evaluation
procedure and 20-attempt search allowance. MolRSI with RSI began with the pooled source experience;
MolRSI without RSI began without experience from previous tasks. In both conditions, memory was updated
during the current task to guide later iterations.
Search trajectories. At each iteration, we identiﬁed the completed workﬂow with the highest mean validation
AUPRC obtained so far. Its test performance was calculated after workﬂow search to construct the trajectory
in Figure 2d. Test scores were not returned to the agent as feedback and were excluded from memory updates.
Zero-search transfer. For Figure 2e, MolRSI identiﬁed the best-matching source task using task type,
evaluation metric, dataset size and task-description similarity, and then selected the workﬂow with the
validation performance on that source task. Both selections preceded evaluation on the antibacterial target.
Model parameters were ﬁtted on the target training data where required, but no alternative target-workﬂow
conﬁgurations were searched.
Resource accounting. One workﬂow-search iteration comprised construction, execution and validation
evaluation of a candidate under the accounting rules in Section S2.2. Zero-search transfer omitted target-
workﬂow search but retained any required model ﬁtting and inference. Skill construction, experience
accumulation on source tasks, target search, ﬁnal ﬁtting and screening-library inference were separate
computational stages.
Experience-pool-size analysis. We transferred one workﬂow from each source task to each of the six
51


antibacterial targets, obtaining a matrix of 150 source–target evaluations. For each subset of 2–25 source tasks,
we retrospectively selected the transfer test AUPRC available within that subset separately for each target,
then averaged these six task performances. The mean curve in Figure 2f is the analytical expectation of this
quantity over all equally sized source-task subsets.
To estimate the percentile bands, we generated 2,000 random permutations of the 25 source tasks using
numpy.random.default_rng with seed 20260901. For pool size Z, the ﬁrst Z tasks in each permutation
deﬁned a sampled pool, producing nested pools across sizes. We calculated the interquartile and 5th–95th
percentile ranges of the six-target mean across these pools. The seed aﬀected these sampled bands, not the
analytical mean or the underlying source–target scores. The bands describe variation in pool composition, not
independent search replicates or conﬁdence intervals for the routing method. At size 25, only the complete
pool was available.

supported workﬂow selection for A. baumannii. Solution memory retained an equal-weight ensemble of

urations. MiniMol used IA3-based adaptation [1] of the prediction head, a gated multilayer perceptron and
class-balanced sampling. MapLight GNN used a Set2Set readout [14] with three processing steps. The four
components achieved AMES AUROCs of 0.8480–0.8633, and their ensemble achieved 0.8731 (Supplementary
Table S6). These conﬁgurations provided previously evaluated alternatives for the antibacterial task.
We transferred the components and their ensemble without changing their architectures or training settings.
Model parameters were ﬁtted on the A. baumannii training data, and target validation performance selected
among the ﬁve inherited conﬁgurations before test evaluation. This selected LANTERN–RADR, which
achieved a mean test AUPRC of 0.5356. The unchanged four-component ensemble achieved 0.4742, and
the other individual components ranged from 0.3235 to 0.3863. Thus, the ensemble that performed best on
AMES did not provide the best transfer performance. Retaining its component conﬁgurations allowed target
validation to identify a more eﬀective predictor without generating or reﬁning a new workﬂow.
Search without experience from previous tasks evaluated 20 workﬂows on A. baumannii, comprising ﬁve
initial model evaluations, six reﬁnements and nine ensemble evaluations (Supplementary Table S7). The
reﬁnements changed molecular features, prediction heads, class-imbalance handling and training procedures.
For example, ADMETTBoost–XGBoost [15] incorporated Morgan-ﬁngerprint features, focal loss [16] and
bagging. Euclia-ML variants used gradient-boosting heads, class-balanced focal loss and feature selection.
Ensemble evaluations combined conﬁgurations evaluated earlier in the search.
The highest observed test AUPRC increased from 0.3716 at step 1 to 0.4134 after the ADMETTBoost–XGBoost
reﬁnement at step 3. It reached 0.4335 after the Euclia-ML reﬁnement at step 5. Ensembles then achieved
0.4572 at step 6 and 0.4988 at step 7. The remaining 13 evaluations explored additional predictors, training
modiﬁcations and ensembles but did not exceed 0.4988. These evaluations included gated fusion of molecular
descriptors, cosine warm restarts [17], stochastic weight averaging [18] and label smoothing. Additional
ensemble conﬁgurations achieved test AUPRCs of 0.4005–0.4684, showing that further combinations of the
evaluated models did not improve the best observed result in this run.
The validation-selected LANTERN–RADR conﬁguration exceeded the best observed test AUPRC in the
20-step search by 0.0368, corresponding to a relative improvement of 7.4%. This advantage was obtained
without further workﬂow reﬁnement, while retaining target-task model ﬁtting and validation-based selection.

S3.4
Virtual screening and compound selection
Training data and screening library. The prospective activity and cytotoxicity models used the same
training datasets as our previous study [10]. The activity models were trained on methicillin-susceptible S.
aureus (MSSA; strain RN4220), and cytotoxicity was predicted for HepG2, HSkMC and IMR-90 cells. Both
studies screened the same library of 12,076,365 compounds, comprising 11,277,225 entries from the Mcule
purchasable database and 799,140 entries from the Broad Institute collection.
53


Selected screening models. The MolRSI workﬂow used separately optimized predictors for activity and
cytotoxicity (Supplementary Figure S2). HepG2 and IMR-90 cytotoxicity predictors combined MiniMol
embeddings with Morgan ﬁngerprints and RDKit descriptors and used residual multilayer-perceptron heads.
The HSkMC predictor used MiniMol embeddings with gated multilayer-perceptron heads. Activity ranking
combined two MiniMol-based variants and a LANTERN–RADR ensemble with weights of 0.55, 0.30 and 0.15,
respectively. These weights combined the outputs of the three component-speciﬁc ensembles.
Filtering, ranking and diversity selection. Screening proceeded through structural novelty ﬁltering,
cytotoxicity ﬁltering, activity ranking, Butina clustering and diverse picking (Supplementary Figure S2).
Structural ﬁltering removed compounds matching PAINS or Brenk alerts [19, 20] or established antibiotic
cores. It also required a maximum Tanimoto similarity below 0.40 to known antibiotics. The excluded core
families were β-lactams, quinolones, oxazolidinones, nitrofurans and sulfonamides. Cytotoxicity ﬁltering
required predicted scores of ≤0.40 for each of HepG2, HSkMC and IMR-90. The retained compounds
were ranked by the weighted antibacterial-activity ensemble described above. Butina clustering [21] grouped
structurally similar candidates, and diverse picking selected compounds across clusters to retain chemical
diversity. We selected and experimentally tested 107 compounds; the ﬁnal selection included documented
exceptions to the nominal cytotoxicity criterion.
S3.5
Comparison with the previous expert-driven workﬂow
Workﬂow diﬀerences. The workﬂows diﬀered in their predictive models, ﬁlter order and thresholds, and
compound-selection procedures (Supplementary Figure S2). The expert-driven workﬂow applied activity
prediction before cytotoxicity and structural ﬁltering. MolRSI applied structural ﬁltering before cytotoxicity
prediction and activity ranking. Training data and library composition are speciﬁed in Section S3.4.
Expert-developed predictors and ﬁltering. The previous study used separate 20-member Chemprop
ensembles [22] with RDKit descriptors for S. aureus activity and for each of the three cytotoxicity tasks [10].
Activity-score thresholds of > 0.4 for Mcule and > 0.2 for the Broad Institute collection retained 10,310
compounds. Requiring all three cytotoxicity scores to be < 0.2 reduced this set to 3,646. PAINS/Brenk
ﬁltering retained 2,209 compounds, and similarity and antibiotic-core ﬁltering retained 1,261. The similarity
ﬁlter used a maximum Tanimoto similarity below 0.5 to training actives, and the excluded core families were
β-lactams and quinolones.
Candidate selection. In the expert-driven workﬂow, Monte Carlo tree search identiﬁed substructures
supporting antibacterial predictions and informed selection of compounds with or without such rationales.
Compounds were sourced according to availability and purity greater than 90%, and 241 ﬁltered candidates
were tested. The MolRSI workﬂow instead ranked candidates with its weighted activity ensemble and selected
structurally diverse compounds across Butina clusters, leading to 107 selected and tested compounds. The
substructure search in the previous study served molecular interpretation and candidate selection; it was a
diﬀerent application of tree search from the workﬂow-development search performed by MolRSI.
Comparison endpoints. Predictive discrimination was evaluated against shared labels, whereas experimental
hit proportions were calculated within each campaign’s selected candidates. These analyses assess prediction
and complete-workﬂow yield, respectively (Sections S3.1 and S3.6.3).

S3.6
Experimental validation
S3.6.1
Antibacterial activity assays
Bacterial strains and culture. S. aureus RN4220 (MSSA) was obtained from DSMZ (DSM 26309) and S.
aureus FPR3757/USA300 (MRSA) from ATCC (BAA-1556). Strains were authenticated by the suppliers by
next-generation or Sanger sequencing. Single colonies were picked from LB agar (1.5% Difco agar; BD 244520)
and grown aerobically in liquid LB (BD 244620; 2–3 mL working volume in 14 mL Falcon tubes) at 37 ◦C with
shaking at 300 rpm, without antibiotic selection.
Compounds. Compounds were purchased from ChemBridge (C3; ID 7359936) and Vitas-M Laboratories
(C32; STL235206) and dissolved in DMSO (MilliporeSigma D5879) at 12.8 mg mL−1.
Inoculum. A suspension of approximately 105 CFU mL−1 was prepared either by a 1:10,000 dilution of an
overnight culture picked from a single colony or by a 1:500 dilution of an OD600 0.08 suspension in fresh LB.
MSSA screen and dose–response plates. Compounds were screened against MSSA at a single dose of
128 µg mL−1 with a PrestoBlue readout. Compounds that inhibited growth were advanced to an eight-point
two-fold dose–response series (128 to 1 µg mL−1) in duplicate 384-well plates. Compound was dispensed from
DMSO stock by acoustic dispensing (Echo; maximum DMSO 1.0%) before addition of inoculated LB to a ﬁnal
volume of 64 µL. Each plate carried sterility (medium only), untreated growth, Triton X-100 kill and DMSO
vehicle (1.000% to 0.008%) controls.
MRSA dose–response plate. The MRSA MIC for C32 was determined in a 96-well plate with a nine-point
two-fold series (128 to 0.5 µg mL−1) in three replicate rows, a DMSO vehicle series, a Triton X-100 dilution
series, untreated growth wells and medium-only outer wells.
Incubation and readout. Plates were covered with a lid (not sealed) and incubated for 18–24 h at 37 ◦C with
5% CO2. OD600 was read on a SpectraMax M3 multi-mode microplate reader (Molecular Devices). MIC was
the lowest concentration giving ≥90% growth inhibition relative to the untreated growth control.

S3.6.3
Predictive performance and experimental hit rates
Predictive performance was evaluated as described in Section S3.1. Experimental hit rates used the 107 MolRSI-

S4.2
Fixed expert-deﬁned ranking objectives
Fixed objectives from established expert-deﬁned design pipelines. BoltzGen [28], BindCraft [29], and
PXDesign [30] are complete binder design pipelines. For the retrospective ranking benchmarks, we isolated a
ﬁxed loss or ranking criterion used internally by each pipeline and evaluated it retrospectively on the same
target–binder sequence pairs. Thus, these references compare the criteria used to prioritize designs.
BoltzGen used its worst-rank quality criterion on Boltz-2 [31] predictions. If rij is the within-target percentile
rank of candidate i under metric j, with smaller ranks better, the reported higher-is-better score was qi =
−maxj rij. The primary three-metric reference used equal rank weights for design_iiptm (which restricts
the design side of the pTM calculation to residues within 8 Å of the target), design_ptm, and the negative
minimum design-to-target PAE. This is the conﬁdence-only form of the BoltzGen criterion. The complete
pipeline additionally ranks PLIP [32] hydrogen bonds, PLIP salt bridges, and ∆SASA. We evaluated both
the three-conﬁdence-score and six-score forms and report the former as the stronger, and therefore more
conservative, ﬁxed comparator.
For BindCraft, we reproduced the default eight-term default_4stage_multimer design loss,
LBindCraft = 0.1 ℓpLDDT + 0.4 ℓPAE + 0.1 ℓiPAE + 1.0 ℓcontact + 1.0 ℓinterface contact
+ 0.3 ℓRg + 0.05 ℓipTM −0.3 ℓhelix,
with the termini-distance loss disabled as in the default conﬁguration. Terms were evaluated from AlphaFold2-
Multimer-v3 [33] predictions with target-chain MSAs. The benchmark score was −LBindCraft, so that larger
values were better.
For PXDesign, we reproduced the seven-term PXDesign-h hallucination loss on Protenix-Mini [34] predictions,
LPXDesign = 0.15 ℓpLDDT + 0.4 ℓPAE + 0.1 ℓiPAE + 1.0 ℓcontact + 1.0 ℓinterface contact
−0.3 ℓhelix + 0.3 ℓRg,
and again used its negative as the higher-is-better benchmark score.
Because the retrospective candidate collection did not contain pipeline-native starting backbones, this com-
parison did not reproduce generator-speciﬁc initial-guess or design-to-refold RMSD ﬁlters, iterative sequence
optimization, or ﬁnal diversity selection. It should therefore be interpreted as a comparison of ﬁxed internal
objectives from the three design pipelines rather than an end-to-end comparison of the pipelines themselves.

S4.4
Binder generation and candidate selection
Design targets. Each prospective design task was speciﬁed by an HLA allele, an on-target and oﬀ-target
peptide pair, source structures for sequence and hotspot provenance, chain assignments, a binder-length
range, and exposed peptide hotspots (Supplementary Table S14). Within each target pair, the HLA heavy-chain
and β2-microglobulin sequences were held ﬁxed and only the peptide sequence was changed. The source PDB
coordinates were not supplied as design inputs. They were used only to establish the target-chain sequences
and select peptide hotspots. Following previous work [27], hotspots were selected from solvent-exposed,
upward-facing peptide residues with relative solvent-accessible surface area (relSASA) of at least 20% in the
binder-free pMHC complex. Individual binder lengths were sampled within the target-speciﬁc allowed range.
Generation workﬂow search conﬁguration. The available generation methods were RFDiﬀusion3 [36],
Proteina-Complexa [37], PXDesign [30], BoltzGen [28], and the MOSAIC [38] simplex accelerated proximal
gradient method (APGM). The methods diﬀered at the generation stage and entered the same inverse-folding
and ranking procedure.
The target-speciﬁc searches ran in parallel without exchanging evaluations or search records. Each search
was allocated 30 attempted rounds and 1,000 A100 GPU-hours. At each node, MolRSI evaluated a generator
conﬁguration by generating 20 backbones and applying SolubleMPNN [39] to generate eight sequences for
each retained backbone, followed by the remaining scoring steps. Cysteine was excluded from the inverse-
folding sequence alphabet in all workﬂows. The ranking score among the top-ranked 5% of designs deﬁned
the conﬁguration’s development reward.
After the 30-round search for each target, the three highest-scoring workﬂows were each used to gen-
erate 8,000 additional designs. The ﬁnal candidate pool comprised all designs from the search and ex-
pansion stages and was then subjected to deduplication, ﬁltering, and ranking. We denote the exported
ﬁelds on_target_ranking_score and off_target_ranking_score by son and soff, respectively. For ﬁnal
MolRSI selection, designs were retained when on_ipsae_min ≥0.50, monomer_plddt ≥0.80, at least 15
HLA contacts (hla_res ≥15), and s8_on −s8_off > 0. Passing designs were ranked by s8_on.

S4.5
Comparison with reference design workﬂows
Prospective agent comparison. We selected Claude Science as the prospective baseline because it achieved
the highest performance on the miniprotein-binder benchmark and had previously demonstrated autonomous
design of experimentally validated protein binders [8]. The completed comparison comprised independent
target-speciﬁc campaigns for MART-1 and SARS-CoV-1. The target and peptide-swapped oﬀ-target speciﬁ-
cations, binder-length range, design objectives and total computational budget were held ﬁxed, while each
system retained its native generation, reﬁnement and ranking procedure. Both systems received 1,000 A100
GPU-hours per target. The 30-round allowance applied to MolRSI’s workﬂow search; Claude Science instead
controlled job scheduling within the shared GPU-hour budget.
Claude Science run conﬁguration for prospective binder design. We initialized one independent Claude
Science session per target, using Claude Opus 5 and an adaptation of Anthropic’s multi-target binder-design
protocol [8]. The prompt source is provided in Section S2.3; this section summarizes the study-speciﬁc
adaptations and run conﬁguration.
We adapted the instructions to peptide-speciﬁc pMHC design and the available computing platform. Inputs
comprised HLA heavy-chain and β2-microglobulin sequences and MSAs, on- and oﬀ-target peptide sequences,
an 80–120-residue binder range, epitope hints and novelty references. Every scoring prediction was required
to include the HLA heavy chain, β2-microglobulin, peptide and binder. Epitope positions were hints, not a
mandatory hotspot-contact ﬁlter.
The campaign was given a computational budget of 1,000 A100 GPU-hours per target, with job state recorded
in ﬁles on a shared volume. SolubleMPNN [39] and SolubleCaliby [40] were named for designs selected for the
ﬁnal computational output, with their base variants permitted during backbone search. The speciﬁed pipeline
placed checks for sequence and structural novelty, liabilities, monomer foldability, structural plausibility and
redundancy before any complex prediction.
The instructions required retained candidates to be scored against the on- and oﬀ-target complexes using
ESMFold2-Fast, ESMFold2-Full [35] and Protenix v2 [41]. For each scoring model, the ﬁxed criterion combined
minimum ipSAE [42], design-to-prediction scDockQ [43] and the on-target-minus-oﬀ-target minimum-ipSAE
diﬀerence. Scores were standardized within the candidate cohort and combined with relative weights of 4:1:4.
The minimum scDockQ across the scoring models used also entered the ranking key at a prespeciﬁed pose
threshold of 0.23, ordering designs that met it above those that did not. At least two rounds of partial diﬀusion,
prediction followed by redesign, or sequence resampling were required. The requested output was a ranked
set of 500 designs per target, excluding exact and near duplicates and limiting representation from individual
design lineages and structural clusters. At least three structure generators had to be represented, with no
more than 50% of designs from one generator or two-thirds from one sequence-design method.
The authors speciﬁed the target inputs, scoring criterion, output format, diversity limits and computational
budget. Claude Science chose the generation strategy and parameters, sampling allocation, use of epitope hints,
monomer and structural-plausibility thresholds, parent selection and reﬁnement operations. The monomer
and structural-plausibility thresholds were ﬁxed after selection. The comparison therefore used its native
binder-design protocol with pMHC- and platform-speciﬁc adaptations.
