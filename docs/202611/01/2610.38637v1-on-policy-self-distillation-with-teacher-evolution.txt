Abstract 

On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD), which aligns a student model’s token-level distribu-tion with that of its self-teacher prompted by privileged context, has emerged as a promis-ing direction to mitigate reward sparsity in sequence-level post-training paradigms. How-ever, OPSD often fails to achieve continuous performance gains during training. In this paper, we find that the model’s limited self-teaching capability underlies this phenomenon by generating unreliable guidance on certain samples. To address this problem, we propose Teacher-Evolution Self-Distillation (TESD), which enables the self-teacher to generate roll-outs and receive environmental feedback based on task correctness and context independence. First, using these rewards as training objectives, we perform teacher-oriented optimization to continuously enhance the model’s self-teaching capability during distillation. Second, by esti-mating teacher guidance reliability from these rewards, we apply adaptive student-oriented op-timization to down-weight samples with unre-liable guidance. Extensive experiments across multiple datasets and model scales show that TESD consistently outperforms representative token-level and sequence-level methods, high-lighting the necessity of improving supervision quality in OPSD.

This sparse sequence-level scheme overlooks the varying significance of individual tokens, hinder-ing the model’s ability to focus on important tokens during optimization (Li et al., 2026). To address this limitation, On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD) offers dense supervision by in-troducing a self-teacher as shown in Figure 1 (Zhao et al., 2026b; Shenfeld et al., 2026). The self-teacher is constructed by prompting the student with a self-generated privileged context, which usually consists of a correct solution to the input query. By computing the per-token logits of the self-teacher on the student’s rollouts, OPSD deliv-ers token-level supervision while preserving the on-1arXiv:2610.38637v1 [cs.LG] 28 Oct 2026 policy property. Therefore, it can maintain align-ment between the dense supervision signal and the model’s current behavior (Hübotter et al., 2026). However, existing OPSD methods fail to contin-uously improve performance during training (Yang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026), leading to suboptimal results as shown in Figure 1(c). This phenomenon is correlated with their reliance on the assumption of the model’s strong self-teaching ability, i.e. , the self-teacher can always correctly guide diverse stu-dent rollouts based on the limited privileged con-text. First , this assumption deviates from practi-cal implementations. The self-teacher only has the additional privileged context compared to the student, without any improvements in model capa-bility. Thus, the self-teacher may fail to associate the limited privileged context with diverse student rollouts (Yu et al., 2026), leading to unreliable guid-ance. This insufficient self-teaching capability also struggles to improve during training, as the cur-rent OPSD paradigm optimizes the model solely as a student. Second , relying on this unrealistic as-sumption, current methods overlook the teacher’s potential to deliver unreliable guidance on certain samples. Consequently, the student is forced to follow the teacher’s guidance across all samples indiscriminately (Hübotter et al., 2026), thereby injecting misleading signals into its optimization process. These two problems limit the quality of supervision received by the student, making it diffi-cult for OPSD to achieve continuous performance improvement during the distillation process. In this paper, we propose the Teacher-Evolution Self-Distillation (TESD) framework to enhance the supervision quality of OPSD. As shown in Figure 1(b), rather than generating rollouts only through the student, TESD enables the self-teacher to generate rollouts and receive rewards. Based on these rewards, TESD introduces two complemen-tary mechanisms to address the aforementioned problems: teacher-oriented optimization to im-prove the model’s self-teaching ability, and adap-tive student-oriented optimization to mitigate the impact of unreliable teacher guidance. Specifically, for the reward function, we design it with both task-correctness and context-independence to esti-mate the model’s self-teaching ability. For teacher-oriented optimization, these rewards serve directly as training objectives for the self-teacher. This ob-jective encourages the self-teacher to generate cor-rect and distinct solutions from privileged contexts, thereby improving its ability to associate the priv-ileged context with diverse student rollouts. For adaptive student-oriented optimization, these re-wards reflect the reliability of teacher guidance across different samples and serve as sample-wise weights that adaptively scale the student’s loss val-ues. By suppressing gradient updates from samples with unreliable guidance, their negative impact on student optimization can be mitigated. In experiments, we evaluate the effectiveness of TESD on pre-trained LLMs (Yang et al., 2025; Olmo et al., 2025) with different parameter sizes and multiple downstream tasks (Feng et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2023; Jain et al., 2025). The evaluation results show that TESD consistently outperforms current representative OPSD (Hübotter et al., 2026) and sequence-level RLVR (Shao et al., 2024) meth-ods. The superior performance of TESD demon-strates the significance of improving supervision quality during the self-distillation process. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We introduce TESD, an OPSD framework that allows the self-teacher to generate rollouts and receive rewards regarding task correctness and context independence. • With the rewards from teacher rollouts, we enhance OPSD’s supervision quality through teacher-oriented and adaptive student-oriented optimization, explicitly improving the model across its dual roles as teacher and student. • Extensive experiments demonstrate the supe-rior performance of TESD, which reflects the importance of enhancing the supervision qual-ity in the OPSD paradigm. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 RL with Verifiable Rewards 

RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has been widely used to improve the reasoning ability of pre-trained LLMs (Guo et al., 2025). As a rep-resentative RLVR method, GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) estimates group-relative advantages from sampled responses and removes the need for a sep-arate critic model. Subsequent studies further im-prove the GRPO by modifying the objective (Chu et al., 2025), normalization (Yu et al., 2025a), and clipping (Zhao et al., 2026b). Despite differ-ences in training strategies and loss formulations, these methods uniformly utilize sequence-level re-wards to optimize the model as a student, which 2is prompted solely by input queries. In contrast, we design a reward function incorporating task-correctness and context-independence terms to op-timize the model as a self-teacher, which receives both the input query and privileged context. By optimizing the self-teacher with this reward, we leverage sequence-level feedback to enhance the supervision quality of the token-level OPSD. 

2.2 On-Policy Self-Distillation 

Unlike classical knowledge distillation, which forces the student to strictly fit the teacher’s out-put trajectory (Gu et al., 2024), on-policy distil-lation (OPD) enables the teacher to provide guid-ance based on the student’s trajectory (Agarwal et al., 2024; Lu and Lab, 2025). This approach can transfer the teacher’s token-level knowledge while preserving the distribution of the student, mak-ing it widely adopted in LLM post-training (Yang et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2026). Building upon this, the on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) paradigm guides the student using a self-teacher, which is built by prompting the student with privileged con-texts, thereby achieving OPD without the exter-nal teacher (Hübotter et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026a; Shenfeld et al., 2026). However, the OPSD paradigm fails to achieve performance improve-ment continuously during training. To tackle this problem, current studies suggest incorporating more privileged context (Yu et al., 2026), degrad-ing OPSD as a weighted module of GRPO (Yang et al., 2026), and implementing an entropy-based routing update strategy (Jin et al., 2026). Differ-ent from these studies relying solely on student-generated rollouts, we empower the self-teacher to generate rollouts and obtain rewards from the environment. Using these rewards, we optimize the self-teacher and adaptively adjust the intensity of its sample-wise supervision, thereby achieving more consistent performance gains over existing methods through enhanced supervision quality.

4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup Models and Datasets . Qwen3-1.7B/4B/8B (Yang et al., 2025) and Olmo3-7B-Instruct (Olmo et al., 2025) are selected as the base models. For datasets, we train models separately on six datasets follow-ing the prior work (Hübotter et al., 2026). The first four datasets are question-answering bench-marks from the reasoning subset (L3) of SciKnow-Eval (Feng et al., 2024), which cover scientific reasoning in Chemistry, Physics, Biology, and Materials Science. Moreover, we use a tool-use dataset from ToolAlpaca (Tang et al., 2023), where the model is required to map a tool-API spec-ification and a user request to the correct tool call. Additionally, we adopt the v6 subset of Live-CodeBench (Jain et al., 2025), a set of coding problems ranging from simple to competition-level. Each dataset is split into a training set and a test set. Furthermore, we evaluate the model across several holdout tasks to measure the ability of TESD to mitigate catastrophic forgetting (Zhou et al., 2023; Verschueren et al., 2018; Lin et al., 2022; Cobbe et al., 2021; Hendrycks et al., 2021; Clark et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023).

Second, we uti-lize SDPO (Hübotter et al., 2026), a representative OPSD method that functions in the same verifier-only RLVR setting as our method. 4.2 Performance on Downstream Tasks Learning without rich feedback . In Table 1, we evaluate the effectiveness of TESD on tasks without rich feedback, i.e. , only one correct student rollout is selected as the privileged context. First, we ob-serve that although SDPO is claimed to achieve faster convergence through the token-level super-vision (Hübotter et al., 2026), a substantial per-formance gap remains compared to the sequence-level GRPO upon full convergence ( e.g. , 69.66% vs. 73.40% on Qwen3-4B). Second, when SDPO and GRPO are directly combined, the resulting per-formance lies between that of the two individual methods ( e.g. , 72.27% vs. 74.94% on Qwen3-8B). This phenomenon indicates that a naive summation at the loss level fails to promote mutual enhance-ment. Finally, TESD outperforms all aforemen-tioned baselines across different base models. 

Learning with rich feedback . In Table 2, we present the performance of TESD and other base-lines on the task with rich feedback, i.e. , the priv-ileged context including a correct student rollout and other code execution results such as runtime errors and failed unit tests. First, SDPO can out-perform GRPO with rich feedback, reflecting the importance of the information density within the privileged context for OPSD. Second, combin-ing SDPO and GRPO achieves the best perfor-mance on Qwen3-1.7B, yet lags behind TESD on larger model scales (Qwen3-4B/8B). This discrep-ancy stems from the limited capability of Qwen3-1.7B, where direct supervision of the student effec-tively accelerates its convergence. In contrast, for stronger models, integrating the self-teacher into the optimization framework improves the quality of supervision signals and then achieves better per-formance. Finally, TESD consistently outperforms SDPO and GRPO across different base models, indicating that the effectiveness of TESD is com-patible with the expansion of privileged context. 

Performance on Holdout Tasks . We evaluate the catastrophic forgetting of the model on other tasks after being trained with different methods on a specific task. As Table 3 shows, TESD learns the new task while mitigating the degradation of initial capabilities, achieving a better performance-forgetting tradeoff than SDPO and GRPO.

4.4 Analysis Model Uncertainty . We explore the impact of TESD on the model’s uncertainty through the accu-racy standard deviation and response length of the model. Experiments are conducted on the Physics subset of SciKnowEval using Qwen3-8B. First, as Figure 4(a) shows, both GRPO and TESD maintain a higher accuracy standard deviation compared to SDPO. Second, for response length in Figure 4(b), GRPO exhibits an increasing trend as training pro-gresses, while SDPO shows a rapid decline in the early training stages before stabilizing. TESD falls between these two trajectories, presenting a grad-ual decrease in both metrics. These results indicate that, compared to SDPO, TESD tends to maintain more uncertainty in model outputs and prevents the over-confidence phenomenon during training. Meanwhile, TESD preserves a key characteristic of the OPSD paradigm: it attempts to generate shorter responses rather than relying on extended reasoning paths to boost performance like GRPO.

5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we propose a method called Teacher-Evolution Self-Distillation (TESD) to achieve on-policy self-distillation with continuous perfor-mance improvements by enhancing the quality of the self-teacher’s supervision. First, we design a reward function considering both correctness and independence to evaluate the self-teacher’s ability to guide diverse student rollouts. Then, using this reward, we perform teacher-oriented optimization to improve the model’s self-teaching capability dur-ing training. Finally, we utilize this reward for adaptive student-oriented optimization, which sup-presses updates on queries with potentially unre-liable teacher supervision. Extensive experiments indicate the stronger performance of TESD com-pared to representative OPSD methods, indicating the importance of enhancing the supervision qual-ity. For in-stance, the optimal λedit is 1.0 for the biology sub-set and 0.7 for the materials subset. Second, set-ting λedit = 1 .0 achieves the highest average per-formance across all three subsets. Therefore, to avoid dataset-specific hyperparameter tuning, we fix λedit = 1 .0 for all main experiments.

A.2.4 Training Efficiency 

In Figure 12, we compare the time cost of different methods on the LCBv6 benchmark. We observe that as training progresses, TESD requires increas-ingly less time compared to GRPO and SDPO. This efficiency gain arises because a substantial portion of the training time in coding tasks is con-sumed by code execution and sandbox evaluation. Since TESD better enhances the model’s capabil-ities throughout the training process, it generates programs that are more structured and efficient to execute, thereby accelerating training speed. 

A.3 Discussion A.3.1 Relation with Methods improving Privileged Context 

Recently, several concurrent studies have improved OPSD from the perspective of privileged context by increasing its volume (Yu et al., 2026) or strength-ening it through contrastive exemplars (Pan et al., 2026). In this section, we discuss the similarities and differences between them. • Similarity . Both TESD and these concurrent methods identify the quality of teacher guid-ance as the primary bottleneck that constrains the performance of the OPSD paradigm, and actively seek to improve it. • Difference . While these concurrent methods focus heavily on the quality of the context pro-vided to the self-teacher, TESD focuses on the self-teacher’s inherent capability to leverage this context to effectively guide diverse stu-dent rollouts. These two paradigms stabilize the performance gains of OPSD from com-plementary angles, suggesting a promising potential for integration. 15 A.3.2 Relation with Methods integrating GRPO and SDPO 

Several concurrent studies have explored com-bining token-level OPSD with sequence-level GRPO (Yang et al., 2026; Pan et al., 2026). In this section, we discuss both the similarities and differences between TESD and these approaches. • Similarity . Both TESD and these methods in-tegrate supervision at both the sequence level and token level. • Difference . (i) For the motivation, these concurrent studies degrade OPSD to an advantage-weighting component within GRPO. Conversely, TESD leverages GRPO to empower the self-teacher for improving OPSD’s supervision quality. (ii) For the loss formulation, these studies adopt the standard GRPO objective, while TESD preserves the divergence-based loss of OPSD for the student model. (iii) For the optimization framework, these studies apply the GRPO and OPSD to a single student model. In contrast, TESD differentiates the model’s dual roles, utilizing GRPO with specifically designed rewards for the self-teacher and OPSD with proposed adaptive weighting mechanism for the student, offering a principled framework rather than a naive objective blend. 

A.4 Ethical Considerations 

This work aims to improve the reasoning capabil-ities of large language models. We acknowledge that enhanced reasoning capabilities could also be misused for harmful purposes. Therefore, we em-phasize the importance of deploying such models with robust safety alignment mechanisms to miti-gate potential risks. All datasets used in our experi-ments are publicly available open-source resources. We adhere to their respective licenses and use them in accordance with their intended purposes. 

A.4.1 Limitations 

Despite outperforming representative token-level and sequence-level methods, TESD introduces ad-ditional hyperparameters, such as λedit , due to the inclusion of extra training objectives and rewards. Furthermore, TESD remains constrained by the teacher updating mechanism of the current OPSD paradigm (Hübotter et al., 2026), which maintains a separate set of weights for the teacher and updates it via an exponential moving average to ensure train-ing stability. This mechanism inherently smooths out the improvement of the teacher’s capabilities, thereby limiting the speed at which the teacher evolves. In the future, to address these limitations, we will attempt to develop an adaptive indepen-dence term for the teacher reward and investigate the internal mechanism of the relationship between teacher updates and training stability. Moreover, investigating whether TESD exhibits sparse update patterns (Shen et al., 2024, 2026c) offers an in-teresting direction for further understanding the student–teacher co-evolution framework. 16
