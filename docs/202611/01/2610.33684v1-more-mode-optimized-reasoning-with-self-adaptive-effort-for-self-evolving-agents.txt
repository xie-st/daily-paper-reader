## ABSTRACT 

Self-evolving training improves the performance of agents on difficult tasks through verifiable feedback, but it often applies costly deliberation uniformly across tasks. To address this problem, we propose Mode-Optimized Reasoning with Self-Adaptive Effort (MORE), a self-evolving training framework that adap-tively selects both reasoning form and computational effort for each task. Specif-ically, MORE follows a two-stage training paradigm: (i) Stage 1 establishes a compact prior over direct answering, short and long chain-of-thought, and code-based reasoning, and (ii) Stage 2 self-evolves this prior with Mode-Adaptive and Test-time Effort PPO (MATE-PPO) on generated, verifiable task streams without curated data. By learning mode selection and token expenditure jointly, MORE al-locates concise reasoning to tasks that permit it while retaining extended delibera-tion where it is useful. Across coding, mathematics, and general-reasoning bench-marks, MORE achieves consistent performance gains on Base and Coder back-bones at model scales from 3B to 14B. During inference, MORE demonstrates higher accuracy and better efficiency, with markedly shorter response lengths and reduced inference time. Coding General Reasoning

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Self-evolving training, especially for coding and mathematics reasoning, is a post-training approach that aims to strengthen agents’ reasoning ability without human-supervision. The prevailing existing methods designed for coding and mathematics reasoning use Proposer-Solver paradigm for reason-ing models in which the model simultaneously learns to define tasks that maximize learnability and to solve them effectively, enabling self-evolution through self-play without relying on external data. In practice, people find that reasoning-oriented language models improve difficult problem solving by making intermediate computation explicit, showcasing superior performance.

Chain-of-thought prompting and self-consistency establish that generated reasoning can improve answer quality (Wei et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023), while test-time scaling shows that additional sampling or search can convert more inference compute into higher accuracy (Brown et al., 2024; Snell et al., 2024). Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards extends this principle into training: execution, answer extraction, and formal checks provide scalable feedback for coding and mathematical reasoning (DeepSeek-AI, 2025; Zhao et al., 2025). Despite their successes, these objectives do not specify how much computation is appropriate for each task. To this end, the absence of accuracy-computation calibration becomes consequential in self-evolving agents. Generated tasks, sampled solutions, and verifier outcomes continually reshape both capa-bility and effective task difficulty. Repeated exposure to difficult verifiable tasks can make long reasoning a default hedge, even when a direct answer would suffice, increasing response length and completion time. Autonomous evolution can also introduce capability degradation and safety drift across coding, mathematical, and safety evaluations (Shi et al., 2026). Long reasoning is valuable when needed, but self-evolving agents must allocate it where its accuracy gains justify its cost. Difficulty-aware compute allocation addresses only part of this problem. CODA, for example, ar-gues that simple instances often gain little from additional tokens whereas hard instances may still require deliberate computation (Wu et al., 2026). A scalar budget alone, however, can not decide whether the computation should be a direct response, a long derivation, or executable code. Con-versely, choosing a discrete reasoning format without controlling its length can still produce verbose traces. Self-evolution adds another complication: a static reward schedule is calibrated to one policy state, even though the solver’s success profile and useful budget change during training. Given the above, we propose Mode-Optimized Reasoning with Self-Adaptive Effort (MORE), a self-evolving training framework that learns four unique reasoning forms and computation amount as coupled policy decisions. Specifically, for task x and response y, MORE factorizes generation as 

πθ (amode , y | x) = πθ (amode | x)πθ (y | x, a mode ), where amode is one of direct , short cot ,

long cot , or code . This factorization separates the form of computation from its amount while allowing both decisions to be optimized by the same training signal. Before producing the response, MORE emits a structured <mode> control token as its first internal decision. Thereafter, the token conditions the subsequent response and supports training and inference statistics. MORE learns this behavior in two stages. Mode-format supervision first makes the action vocabu-lary identifiable and gives each mode a stable response contract. Mode-Adaptive & Test-time Effort PPO (MATE-PPO) is our proposed Stage-2 optimization method for coupled mode–budget learn-ing (Schulman et al., 2017). It integrates verifier-grounded correctness, online difficulty estimation, mode fit, and dynamic budget regulation into a single trajectory objective, so clipped policy updates jointly shape reasoning form and computation amount as a learned model capability. 

Our core contributions are as follows: i) We introduce MORE, a self-evolving training frame-work that jointly adapts reasoning mode and computation budget to online task difficulty. Across coding, mathematical, and general-reasoning benchmarks, MORE delivers strong and consistent performance. ii) Our two-stage learning design first establishes four mode-specific response con-tracts and then applies MATE-PPO to jointly optimize mode choice, response generation, and com-putational effort through a unified verifier-grounded trajectory objective. iii) We design an online difficulty–effort controller that converts evolving rollout clusters into difficulty tiers and updates mode-conditioned budget bands throughout training, enabling the policy to calibrate its reasoning al-location as its capabilities change. MORE also reaches its strongest observed out-of-distribution per-formance earlier during self-evolution than another framework in the same self-evolving paradigm.

## 3 METHODOLOGY 

3.1 MORE F RAMEWORK 

Our proposed Mode-Optimized Reasoning with Self-Adaptive Effort (MORE) is a two-stage self-evolving training framework that enables models to learn both the form and the amount of com-putation used for each task. The model trained under MORE framework first emits one of four internal modes: direct , short cot , long cot , or code , and then generates a response under the corresponding reasoning contract and a difficulty-conditioned effort budget. Figure 2 summarizes the complete MORE framework. MORE’s Stage 1 constructs mode-format supervision to initialize a stable prior over the four reasoning contracts. MORE’s Stage 2 draws in-spiration from AZR’s proposer–solver self-evolving paradigm (Zhao et al., 2025) and it performs the overall reinforcement-learning procedure: it samples verifiable tasks, generates mode-conditioned rollouts, verifies their outcomes, updates online difficulty and budget state, constructs the MORE terminal return, and applies a Mode-Adaptive & Test-time Effort (MATE) PPO update. Telemetry from the same loop records capability, mode allocation, token economy, and system efficiency. Stage 2 operationalizes MORE through a self-evolving proposer–solver training loop, with MATE-PPO providing its policy-optimization mechanism. For each sampled task, MORE generates a mode token and response under a pre-update difficulty context and mode-conditioned budget band. The verifier then supplies correctness, which MATE-PPO combines with the retained controller state to form the terminal return for a clipped policy update. The controller then updates the associated clus-ter’s difficulty context and mode-conditioned budget band for subsequent rollouts. The generated mode token remains a likelihood-bearing part of the optimized trajectory, linking mode selection, response generation, and effort calibration. The following subsections provide further details. 3.4 ADAPTIVE MODE -B UDGET CONTROL 

Online difficulty. The controller represents each task by structural and behavioral features. Firstly, Structural features include code shape, prompt artifacts, task family, and verifier target; behavioral features include rolling success and response length. Thus, a global success threshold would become stale as a self-evolving policy improves, whereas independent per-task estimates are sparse and noisy. Then, Clustering pools evidence across structurally and semantically comparable rollouts; the EMA follows policy drift without reacting fully to one outcome; and quantile tiers preserve relative difficulty when absolute success rises. Finally, the resulting tier indexes both mode-fit utility and mode-conditioned budget bands, applying stronger pressure toward direct or short traces in currently easy regions while retaining wider long-reasoning or code allowances in hard regions. Subsequently, a stable task identifier is assigned to a family- and verifier-aware cluster c. After a verified rollout, its success estimate is updated by q(t)

Autonomous mode-token generation. For every rollout, MORE generates a <mode=m> control token before the response body, with m ∈ { direct , short-CoT , long-CoT , code }. This token is the re-alized amode : it selects the response contract, indexes U (d, m ), and identifies the mode-conditioned budget band. As a likelihood-bearing policy action, it makes mode allocation an endogenous model behavior under the task distribution.

The same four-mode vocabulary is used in supervision, rollout generation, reward routing, infer-ence, and evaluation. MORE emits the mode token before the response body and then follows the corresponding response contract. The inference harness parses the generated token for telemetry and scores the resulting answer. The telemetry interface records mode ratios, per-mode success, difficulty tiers, budget bands, reward components, response-length distributions, throughput, and wall-clock components, providing a unified view of adaptive reasoning behavior.

Table 3 shows that expanding the maximum response length from 8,096 to 10,240 tokens raises AVG by 0.3 for both the Base and AZR controls, but produces mixed movements on individual benchmarks. This limited gain suggests that simply extending the generation ceiling approaches a capacity bottleneck at the 7B scale. Also, Stage 1 is not a capability-only warm-up: its mode SFT deliberately reshapes the backbone distribution to fit four tagged response contracts. Immediately after this reshaping, CAvg falls from 56.6 to 52.2 and MAvg from 23.9 to 19.9, reducing AVG from 48.8 to 45.3. Learning the prescribed mode syntax and response forms therefore trades part of the backbone’s original unconditioned coding and mathematical behavior for a stable, controllable mode prior. The same shift is visible when this prior precedes AZR, whose AVG changes from 54.4 to 52.4. Conversely, Stage 2 without Stage 1 reaches 47.8 AVG: it retains substantial reasoning ability, but has not learned the four-format prior needed for autonomous mode allocation. MATE-PPO aligns the Stage-1 prior with verifier-grounded self-evolution. The complete two-stage configuration combines stable mode-contract learning with adaptive optimization and attains the highest AVG (57.3), CAvg, MAvg, GSM8K, and MMLU-Pro. 

## 6 LIMITATIONS 

Our study does not include fine-grained ablations of MATE-PPO’s internal components, since iso-lating individual terms while preserving its coupled mode–difficulty–budget dynamics did not yield a meaningful controlled comparison. MORE uses four predefined response contracts and online difficulty estimates from task and rollout statistics. Its efficiency metrics are too coarse to resolve relative changes among finer-grained time components across training and evaluation. 

## 7 CONCLUSION 

We introduced MORE, a two-stage self-evolving training framework that learns reasoning mode and computational effort as coupled policy decisions. Stage 1 establishes compact response con-tracts for direct answering, short and long chain-of-thought, and code-based reasoning. Stage 2 self-evolves this prior on generated, verifiable task streams without curated data, with MATE-PPO optimizing mode selection, response generation, and effort calibration within one trajectory objec-tive. Across coding, mathematical, and general-reasoning benchmarks, MORE achieves strong and consistent performance on Base and Coder backbones and model scales from 3B to 14B. MORE reaches strong capability earlier during self-evolution while sustaining lower rollout token demand, shorter evaluation responses, and lower inference latency. Taken together, these research results 9establish MORE as a framework for task-adaptive reasoning form and effort, advancing efficient self-evolving training and its models’ reasoning through joint mode–budget learning.

PPO base for joint mode–budget control. MATE-PPO uses a PPO-style clipped actor update as its optimization base and augments it with a controller-conditioned terminal return. Unlike Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024), which forms advantages by ranking mul-tiple completions of a common prompt within a local group, MORE must optimize heterogeneous proposer and solver behaviors under one shared policy. Proposer samples construct different ex-ecutable tasks, whereas several solver modes can be correct and compliant with their own (d, m )

budget bands on the same task. Group-relative normalization would make such valid task construc-tions or mode contracts compete within a sampled group and remove return differences across task groups from the advantage estimator. The trajectory-level PPO objective instead preserves direct, shared-scale credit for task construction, mode selection, solution accuracy, and effort calibration across the six-stream update. It also maintains broad task coverage for the online controller without requiring repeated long rollouts from every frozen controller state. ## E ROLLOUT CLUSTERING AND DIFFICULTY ASSIGNMENT 

This section specifies the full clustering path used by the longitudinal realization. It replaces a global notion of difficulty with policy-relative groups: rollouts with comparable task structure and verifier semantics share a success history, and their relative success determines the reward-routing tier. The online feature-bucket procedure continuously adapts these groups as the policy evolves. 

Observation and task envelope. For rollout i, the reward manager constructs oi =(ui, a i, L i, x i, z i) from a stable task identifier ui, verifier correctness ai, attended response length 

Li, problem text xi, and metadata zi. The task role defaults to solver. Explicit family and sub-type metadata take precedence. Otherwise, the runtime uses the rollout prefix to distinguish pro-poser from solver and the verifier suffix to assign reasoning semantics: input recovery to abduction, output prediction to deduction, and function synthesis to induction. Prompt cues complete these assignments; tasks outside the recognized semantics enter a dedicated general family/subtype that preserves them for online clustering. 

Rollout representation. The representation ϕi concatenates the feature groups in Table 7. Ex-tracted structural and semantic fields provide the task representation, while a per-UID window of the latest 200 verified observations contributes mean success, success variance, and mean response length. After each rollout is verified, its correctness and length update this state for subsequent mode and budget decisions.

RQ2: Does adaptive mode selection achieve a stronger test-time accuracy–efficiency trade-off than a fixed reasoning mode? 

To isolate the value of routing, Table 11 compares the adaptive MORE-Coder-7B policy with four counterparts that apply one response contract to every evaluation instance. Each benchmark pair reports accuracy and Response Length Avg (RLA), making the capability–effort profile of a globally fixed mode directly comparable with adaptive routing. The fixed policies expose a clear capability–effort ladder: Direct yields the shortest responses, while Long CoT achieves the strongest fixed-policy accuracy at substantially greater token cost. Adaptive routing attains the highest Code Avg of all policies while using 52.8% fewer Code tokens than fixed Long CoT. On Math Avg, GSM8K, and MMLU-Pro, it remains within 0.3, 1.3, and 0.9 points of the strongest fixed policy, respectively, while reducing RLA by 36.7%, 14.1%, and 30.4%. The learned policy thus draws on the strengths of long reasoning when it is useful without committing every instance to its cost, yielding the strongest observed accuracy–efficiency balance across the evaluated suites. The benchmark-specific allocations in Figure 10 align with the compact test-time profiles in Table 2. Across both Base and Coder model families, MORE couples these varied mode mixtures with lower Response Length Avg and Wall Time than matched AZR models. The resulting test-time behavior therefore combines task-specific response-form selection with lower overall generation demand and elapsed evaluation time.
