## ABSTRACT 

Generalist robot policies and language-model agents provide complementary ca-pabilities, but completing a task also requires adapting their use to the conse-quences of physical interaction. We study execution as a locus of adaptation: feedback guides the current action and supplies evidence for revising subsequent decisions under fixed model weights. We introduce RoboDoctor , a training-free framework that connects diagnosis, grounded action, and persistent experience across three revision targets. The harness allocates control between policy and agent; intervention determines when to assist while preserving useful policy be-havior; and operation refines physical methods through observed effects. Linked experience records retain the reason for a decision, the attempted method, its out-come, and conditions for reuse. On a curated archive of 54 RoboDojo-Sim task entries, RoboDoctor achieves a five-dimension mean score of 57.10, with a native success rate of 49.44%. Recorded interactions illustrate strategy changes, selec-tive correction, and grasp revision. These findings characterize execution-time adaptation in the evaluated system; controlled trials remain necessary to isolate the contribution of cross-episode experience. ## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Vision–language–action (VLA) models and world action models (WAM) have expanded the range of manipulation behaviors available from a single model (Kim et al., 2024; Black et al., 2025a; Ye et al., 2026). Language-model agents add complementary capabilities in task interpretation, planning, and tool use (Ahn et al., 2022; Liang et al., 2022). Translating these capabilities into complete task execution also requires managing their consequences: each interaction changes the conditions under which later actions must succeed. A grasp can be locally successful yet leave an object poorly positioned for the next step. Robot capability therefore depends on how available behaviors are coordinated, evaluated, and revised through physical interaction. This raises a system-level question: how can a robot use execution feedback to adapt the way it deploys existing model capabilities? 

Robot agents already incorporate environmental feedback into planning, detect execution failures, and use reflection to refine action selection (Huang et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2025). Building on this progress, we study the decisions that arise when an agent and a continuous robot policy share responsibility for execution. A deviation may require a local correction, a different contact method, or a change in the strategy that organizes the task. These responses operate at different levels and can interfere with one another: correcting alignment may preserve a useful grasp, whereas repeatedly restoring that grasp may perpetuate an ineffective strategy. The challenge is to identify what needs revision while retaining behavior that remains useful. Recent embodied recursive self-improvement (RSI) systems use physical feedback to revise exe-cution harnesses and accumulate reusable skills (MMLab & PhysicalRSI contributors, 2026; Song et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026). Within this direction, our perspective is that execution is itself a locus of adaptation . Physical interaction serves two roles: it advances the task and provides evidence about how the available capabilities should be used. That evidence can regulate the next action and become external experience for subsequent attempts. With both policy and agent weights fixed, the adaptive state lies in control organization, intervention decisions, and the accumulated methods that inform future behavior. Preserving successful behavior is central to this process: a failure should prompt revision at the level implicated by the feedback, without discarding competence that the same interaction has demonstrated. Making this perspective operational requires connecting diagnosis to physical evidence. Recogniz-ing a slipped grasp does not specify a stable contact, and reaching a commanded pose does not establish that the object moved as intended. Likewise, a successful correction supports a reusable lesson only under conditions justified by the observation. An alignment principle may apply in an-other scene even when its original displacement does not. We therefore link the reason for a decision to the attempted physical method, its observed effect, and its conditions of applicability. Reuse calls for checking those conditions and grounding the method again in the current scene. We introduce RoboDoctor , a framework that implements this perspective through three coupled revision targets (Figure 1). Harness RSI allocates control responsibility between policy and agent. 

Intervention RSI determines when assistance is warranted and which policy behavior to preserve. 

Operation RSI refines how an intended physical change is achieved. A H YBRID controller lets the policy propose primary motion while the Doctor reviews and selectively corrects it; a D IRECT 

controller assigns planning and action to the Doctor through the same grounded tools (Su et al., 2026). Observed outcomes guide selection between these controllers, and linked experience records support revision within and across episodes. Recursive self-improvement here refers to updating and reusing this external state under frozen model weights; the evaluated harness selects between predefined controllers. We evaluate RoboDoctor through a curated archive of 54 RoboDojo-Sim task entries (Chen et al., 2026). The selected Refinement outcomes achieve an Overall score of 57.10, compared with 49.74 during Warmup. The capability profile in Figure 1 shows that the largest gains occur in General-ization and Open instruction following, while Long-Horizon performance decreases. The accom-panying examples illustrate alignment correction and grasp revision; Section 5 examines these in-teractions alongside a change in task strategy. The archive supports analysis of these mechanisms and their associated performance; its retrospective selection and unequal coverage do not isolate the causal benefit of cross-episode experience (Section 4.1). 2RoboDoctor Technical Report Our contributions are: (i) a training-free execution framework that connects control allocation, selec-tive intervention, and physical operation as coupled targets of adaptation; (ii) an experience mecha-nism that links diagnosis, method, outcome, and applicability to guide subsequent use of fixed model capabilities; and (iii) a 54-task empirical analysis combining aggregate outcomes with recorded ev-idence of how execution decisions change. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

VLA and WAM based robot policies. Learned robot policies provide the manipulation capabil-ities on which execution-time adaptation can operate. Diffusion Policy models multimodal action distributions for visuomotor control (Chi et al., 2023). Octo, OpenVLA, and π0 develop generalist robot policies (Ghosh et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024; Black et al., 2025b), while π0.5 uses hetero-geneous co-training to extend generalization to new environments (Black et al., 2025a). World action models connect action generation to prediction of physical evolution: DreamZero jointly models future video and actions (Ye et al., 2026), and PrismWAM separates semantic context from depth-based physical state (Xin et al., 2026). We study adaptation in how a trained policy is used, evaluating frozen PrismWAM; transfer to other policy families remains unmeasured. 

Robot agents and execution-time reasoning. Robot agents organize model capabilities into task-directed behavior. SayCan, Code as Policies, and VoxPoser connect language reasoning to robotic affordances, programs, and spatial action representations (Ahn et al., 2022; Liang et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2023). Inner Monologue incorporates environmental feedback into planning (Huang et al., 2022); REFLECT explains failures from multisensory experience summaries (Liu et al., 2023); and DoReMi monitors plan–execution misalignment to support recovery (Guo et al., 2024). Reflective Planning uses imagined future states and reflection to refine VLM action selection at test time (Feng et al., 2025). We build on feedback-driven execution to study assistance to an existing continuous policy: which behavior to preserve, when to replace a proposal, and whether the correction addresses the task’s limitation. 

Recursive self-improvement for robots. Embodied RSI extends execution-time reasoning by re-vising the system that generates subsequent behavior. Its adaptive state can take several forms. PhysicalRSI and RegenHarness revise execution harnesses and the rules connecting context, rout-ing, and recovery (MMLab & PhysicalRSI contributors, 2026; Wang et al., 2026). EmbodiedRSI combines hypothesis-guided experimentation with code–skill co-evolution (Song et al., 2026); Em-bodied Turing Machines develops a shared stateful code library that executes without a VLM or VLA in the loop (Hu et al., 2026). EmbodiRSI instead uses failure feedback to target corrective data collection and successive policy training (Lang et al., 2026). RoboDoctor places adaptation in control allocation and external experience while keeping both model weights fixed. The evaluated harness makes a discrete controller selection; within that controller, accumulated evidence informs intervention decisions and physical methods. This mechanism implements a bounded form of robot RSI. 

Experience and in-context adaptation. For fixed-weight agents, external experience lets an ear-lier interaction inform later behavior. ReAct interleaves reasoning and action, Reflexion retains verbal feedback, and Voyager accumulates executable skills (Yao et al., 2022; Shinn et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). In robotics, RoboICL studies control through in-context demonstrations and interaction memory (Liu et al., 2026), while GPT-Policy organizes demonstrations and execution feedback into context for VLM-based control (Cheng et al., 2026). EmbodiedEvo distills physical feedback into reusable code Skills and diagnostic Lessons, acquired in a task-specific scene and then frozen for execution in new scenes (Li et al., 2026). 3.1 EXECUTION -T IME ADAPTATION WITH FROZEN MODELS 

RoboDoctor adapts the use of fixed models through physical feedback. Figure 2 shows how control allocation, selective intervention, and grounded operation connect current observations to experience retrieval and update. Control allocation determines who directs execution, intervention determines what policy behavior to preserve, and operation determines how to achieve and verify a physical effect. Experience links these decisions to their observed consequences and conditions for reuse. We consider language-specified, dual-arm manipulation. At step t, the Doctor receives head and wrist RGB views, metric depth, measured joint state, and end-effector poses, without privileged object poses or object-state labels. When the policy supplies primary motion, adaptation requires deciding which behavior to preserve and when assistance is warranted. We separate policy proposal from physical execution: a roll-out returns At and a robot-only forward-kinematics (FK) preview without moving the robot. The Doctor relates this candidate motion to current RGB-D observations, measured poses, and experi-ence. It seeks an action-relevant reason to assist, such as a changed object location or an impending alignment mismatch, while retaining useful aspects of the interaction. For each proposal, the Doctor selects one of two execution branches:

Both branches discard the unused proposal suffix and return a fresh observation. Multi-call recovery is possible, but each correction requires a new observation and policy proposal. Every executed frame, including agent corrections, enters the policy history so that subsequent proposals respond to the achieved state. The resulting observation tests the intervention decision. Releasing a proposal may expose a missed failure; a correction may restore alignment or disturb a stable grasp. These events can be recorded as evidence for revising later assistance, while autonomous successes identify behavior worth pre-serving (Section 3.5). Thus the decision to intervene depends on both the current proposal and ex-perience from earlier outcomes. The FK preview supports reasoning about intended robot motion; the physical effect of either branch must be established through execution. 3.4 OPERATION RSI: G ROUND , T EST , AND REVISE PHYSICAL METHODS 

An intervention decision identifies a change to make; operation determines how to realize and verify that change. The same requirement applies when D IRECT plans a task action. The Operation RSI module in Figure 2 organizes both types of agent-issued action into grounding, action, and verifica-tion. The pouring example distinguishes an alignment correction from the subsequent observation of liquid transfer. The Doctor grounds visible surfaces from image pixels and depth, then uses mea-sured end-effector poses and current head or wrist views to construct a contact method. A grounded surface point is a localization reference, not necessarily a valid finger contact or end-effector target. The method specifies approach, contact region, wrist orientation, gripper timing, motion sequence, and the role of the other arm. Execution provides evidence for retaining or revising this method. The Doctor compares the com-mand, achieved robot motion, and observed object interaction. Failure to reach a target directs attention to motion or configuration; reaching it while the object slips suggests revisiting the contact method. It can revise the relevant choice, for example by changing the gripper closing axis and checking retention with a short lift before transport. End-effector and joint-space tools support these 5RoboDoctor Technical Report revisions. H YBRID retains the five-step per-call limit; D IRECT can allocate longer budgets to clear travel and shorter ones to contact-sensitive motion. Task progress remains subject to the same verification. In D IRECT , a live plan tracks subtasks and observed progress, while separate notes preserve detailed evidence. Later observations can reopen a subtask if its result is disturbed. In both modes, an operation is retained when its observed effect supports the intended interaction and reconsidered when later evidence contradicts that assessment. Persistent experience carries the method and its supporting evidence into subsequent decisions. 3.5 EXPERIENCE : T URN PHYSICAL FEEDBACK INTO REUSABLE METHODS 

A correction changes the current physical state; retaining its evidence can change how a later action is chosen. Experience provides this link between execution and adaptation. A lesson connects the reason for a decision, the behavior worth preserving, the attempted method, and its observed effect. Its applicability is limited by that evidence: achieving local alignment, for example, does not establish that the subsequent pour succeeded. In H YBRID , records contain an event type, triggering evidence, policy state, attempted method when applicable, and outcome. Policy state distinguishes observations, proposed or executed actions, and measured or previewed FK. Operation fields describe timing, tools, contact technique, and a stop-ping condition. Linked diagnosis and operation entries associate the evidence for intervention with the attempted physical method. The experience panel in Figure 2 links a mouth–rim alignment cue to a lateral correction that preserves the grasp, with local alignment and subsequent pouring verified separately. The schema distinguishes autonomous policy success, policy failure, and intervention. A policy-success record specifies the observed object interaction, success evidence, and applicabil-ity; other records require the attempted operation and stopping condition. Failed attempts remain evidence about when a method should be revised.

## 4 EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION 

4.1 BENCHMARK AND EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

We evaluate performance on 54 task entries from RoboDojo-Sim across five capability dimensions: Generalization, Precision, Long-Horizon, Memory, and Open instruction following. Generalization comprises 12 standard and 12 randomized entries, while the remaining dimensions comprise 8, 8, 6, and 8 tasks, respectively. Our evaluation uses the category mapping and reference leaderboard archived on 2026-10-20. We use PrismWAM as the underlying policy (Xin et al., 2026). The local archive combines multiple source runs for which a uniform agent version, checkpoint, and inference budget are not established. 

Evaluation stages and outcome selection. We divide the evaluation into two stages: Warmup, covering iterations 0–2, and Refinement, covering iterations 3–8. Warmup already incorporates Doctor assistance and experience updates and therefore characterizes the initial stage of the system rather than an unassisted policy or a baseline without memory. An outcome is considered valid if it is complete, explicitly marked as valid for evaluation, and associated with a finite native score in [0 , 1] . Valid outcomes with zero scores are retained. The archive includes score-based selection among candidate outcomes, retaining the highest valid score with deterministic tie-breaking. Because selected outcomes may originate from different source runs and memory histories, the stage comparison characterizes the archived results rather than a prospectively controlled treatment effect.

## 6 DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

Evidence determines what can be reused. The three revision targets distinguish failures that require different responses: reallocate control, change an intervention decision, or revise the contact method. Their connection through experience is useful only if the retained lesson accurately reflects execution. A plausible command or achieved robot pose does not certify stable contact, and visible progress need not satisfy the native task predicate. Schema validation makes records inspectable but does not establish their truth. Perceptual errors, incorrect diagnoses, and overgeneralized lessons can therefore persist across decisions. 

Mechanism evidence and causal evidence. The cases demonstrate strategy changes, selective correction, and feedback-driven operation revision. The aggregate results characterize the selected runs, including regressions. Neither alone establishes the causal benefit of cross-episode experi-ence. Retrospective selection, unequal coverage, and differing source histories limit attribution. A prospective study should compare persistent and reset experience under matched controllers, con-10 RoboDoctor Technical Report figurations, seeds, and interaction budgets. The same design should compare fixed H YBRID , fixed DIRECT , and Warmup-based routing. 

Cost and generality. Training-free adaptation still consumes agent inference, policy calls, and en-vironment interaction. Short execution horizons can improve observability while increasing latency; the present archive lacks a consolidated cost analysis. All results are in simulation, and transfer to other policies or physical robots is unmeasured. Bounded actions limit motion before renewed ob-servation, but do not provide collision avoidance or a formal safety guarantee. 

## 7 CONCLUSION 

RoboDoctor studies how existing model capabilities can be adapted to a task through physical in-teraction. With policy and agent weights fixed, it connects control allocation, selective intervention, and operation revision through experience that retains both a method and its supporting evidence. This makes execution a source of adaptation: observed effects inform what to preserve, what to revise, and what can guide a later attempt. The RoboDojo-Sim archive shows a higher aggregate Refinement score alongside concrete strategy changes and local corrections, with regressions that expose remaining limitations. The next experimental priority is to measure when revised experience improves subsequent execution under matched configurations and interaction budgets.

## A RUNTIME CONTRACTS AND EXTERNAL EXPERIENCE 

The implementation separates policy proposal from physical execution. policy_rollout vali-dates a finite H × 14 array and gripper values in [0 , 1] , requests a robot-only FK preview, and returns with zero physical steps. policy_continue requires the current proposal identifier and exe-cutes an unmodified prefix, capped at the controller’s execution limit. Direct-tool interventions in HYBRID require an existing proposal and a step budget from one to five. A new observation clears the old proposal. These host-side checks enforce the protocol independently of whether the agent’s diagnosis is correct. The current RSI controller caps released prefixes at 16 steps, even if a backend advertises a longer execution horizon. The bundled PrismWAM preview configuration advertises 32-action predictions and a 24-step backend horizon; the host’s 16-step cap determines what a single H YBRID release can execute. This distinction prevents backend defaults from being mistaken for the applied controller limit. The current preview backend uses four past RGB frames at stride four plus the current frame; early missing frames repeat the earliest observation.
