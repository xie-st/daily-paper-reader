Abstract 

Improving an agent through successive revisions requires balancing exploration and evaluation under a limited budget. A narrow search can miss useful sequences of edits, while fully evaluating every intermediate version can exhaust the budget before those sequences yield a verified improvement. We introduce Spec ulative Recursive Self-Improvement (SpecRSI ), which drafts executable successor agents before their predecessors are fully evaluated, while requiring complete verification before adoption. Verification-Aware Tree Search (VATS) allocates the budget between further edits and out-standing checks using the predicted verified return of a continuation. Group Verification Optimization (GVO) learns the improver from completed searches while keeping the real evaluator fixed. In the main scientific comparison, terminal gains exceed Janus by 33.3%–40.0%. Additional evaluations show that SpecRSI outperforms Janus in all 50 scientific backbone–task pairs and exceeds MetaRSI-v1 by 1.85%–2.38% in macro-average system score across twenty backbones. Coding-validation success reaches 69.4% versus HGM’s 64.3% at a 20.8% lower target-cost index.

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Agent systems can be improved through successive revisions to their prompts, tools, and workflows. Each round proposes executable changes and then evaluates whether they improve task performance, and both consume time and computation. This raises a central question: how should an agent allocate its limited budget between exploring new revisions and verifying their benefits? Successful revisions can make later ones easier, but the evaluations that identify them are often far more expensive than proposing another edit (Zhang et al., 2026a; Wang et al., 2026b; Liu et al., 2026). The difficulty is not a shortage of candidates. It is turning exploration into verified progress before time or computation runs out. Two limiting strategies expose the tradeoff. Following one fully evaluated lineage gives every decision fresh feedback, but it narrows the alternatives and inserts an evaluation barrier between dependent edits. Maintaining many lineages explores more, but fully evaluating them all buys evidence for branches that may never be used. Extra workers cut elapsed time without making the work free, and under a fixed budget a wider search need not leave enough computation to finish the most useful branch. The barrier, not the shortage of ideas, is what stalls progress: can we draft successors ahead of their parents’ full evaluation, yet still spend verification only where it pays? For example, one 1arXiv:2610.16491v1 [cs.LG] 12 Oct 2026 coding-agent revision adds a file–test map; a successor uses it for focused intermediate retesting while retaining the final full suite. Prior systems relax part of this by separating expansion from evaluation or validating in parallel (Wang et al., 2026b; Liu et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2026b). Here we focus on jointly selecting further revisions and bundles of outstanding checks under the remaining budget. Once successors can be drafted ahead, where should we spend the budget next? A candidate that looks strong may still need several checks before it can be adopted, while a child that repairs an unhelpful parent may need only one remaining check. A scorer that only asks How good does this version look?” therefore misses the cost of making it usable. What governs the budget is not a candidate but a continuation , a sequence of further edits and checks from the current state, and the operative question becomes What verified improvement can this continuation still deliver with the remaining budget?” This quantity must be estimated before the checks that would reveal it are paid, and a wrong estimate wastes irreversible budget; this pre-verification estimate under a hard budget, not the drafting of successors, is what makes the problem hard. It shifts the scoring target from candidate quality to the marginal verified return of further editing and checking. Root

However, the drafting–confirmation idea cannot be transferred unchanged from tokens to agent revisions. (1) Revisions are revisable programs, not accepted prefixes. A successor may repair or build on a parent that is not yet admissible. (2) Verification consists of paid, complementary checks. External evaluations are not supplied by one target-model pass: checks have heterogeneous costs and dependencies, and several may be jointly required for adoption. (3) Progress is verified improvement under a budget. Search outcomes depend on realized gains and all incurred costs, including failed and unfinished work, rather than proposal or acceptance counts. We propose SpecRSI with the following contributions: • A Two-Level Framework for Speculative Self-Improvement. To address (1), inner loop searches extend incompletely evaluated versions but adopt only fully evaluated, admissible ones. The improver remains frozen within each search and learns from complete-search feedback between rounds. • Verification-Aware Tree Search (VATS). To address (2), the Solver preserves affordable com-pletion bundles, including individually low-value checks; the Scorer predicts verified gains from further edits and checks, net of subsequent costs; and the Policy selects the next draft or verification plan under the remaining budget. • Group Verification Optimization (GVO). To address (3), GVO uses independent complete searches from the same start as its unit of credit. Verified gains minus all search costs provide delayed credit to sampled edits and work choices, training the Drafter and Policy. Realized suffix returns and paid same-state comparisons train the Scorer. 22 PRELIMINARIES AND RELATED WORK

Accurate benefit evaluation is central to guiding RSI toward better agents. It informs which revisions and checks deserve further work, guiding the direction of improvement. Inaccurate evaluation can overlook useful revisions or waste resources on candidates that cannot be adopted. Therefore, evaluation should reflect the verified improvement that further work can deliver. However, accurate benefit evaluation is non-trivial: a candidate’s current quality does not determine the value of continuing from it. This difficulty stems from two key challenges. 1 Benefit evaluation should reflect downstream verified improvement. Intermediate revisions may enable later gains, but establishing them requires subsequent edits and real checks. 2 Benefit evaluation should account for completion costs. Complementary checks, planning, and failed attempts consume the same limited budget; even promising gains may be unaffordable to verify. Together, these 3challenges create a performance–cost trade-off: a useful continuation must promise sufficient verified improvement while remaining affordable to complete. 4.1 EVALUATION SETUP .

Tasks, models, and baselines. We evaluate five scientific domains (PyBaMM, AQM, FDTD, Reactor, and IRP), four system tasks (Terminal-Bench 2.1 (TB2.1), SWE-Pro, GPQA-hard100, and AIME), and separate coding, prompt, and workflow profiles. Qwen3.5-35B-A3B serves as the reference backbone for mechanism studies and the primary matched system comparison. For cross-backbone evaluation on the scientific domains, we additionally test GPT-6-Astra, Claude-Opus-5, Gemini-3.5-Flash, DeepSeek-V4-Pro, GLM-5.3, Kimi-K3, MiniMax-M3, GPT-5.6-Sol, Claude-Sonnet-5-1M, and GPT-5.5, covering 50 backbone–task pairs in total. We further evaluate the four system tasks across twenty model backbones to test whether the observed gains persist across different starting models. Baselines include Janus, HGM, MetaRSI, FlashEvolve, and task-specific optimizers (Liu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b; Tan et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2026b). D/H/M permits data, harness, and model edits, whereas D+H excludes model edits. Comparisons remain within matched permission bands; SpecRSI uses direct checks. 

Measurements. Scientific gain and call-axis AUBC retain native units; task scores use percentages, absolute gains percentage points (pp). Target-cost, training-cost, and lifecycle indices are not pooled. Validation normally uses five training seeds with three nested searches each. 

Reproducibility. The reference reproduction configuration uses official BF16 Qwen3.5-35B-A3B with a frozen text backbone and rank-16 Drafter LoRA. Training uses 2,048-token sequences and one H20 per scientific domain; a separate H20 serves 32,768-token inference. Other evaluated backbones use provider APIs. 4.5 RESOURCE TRADE -OFFS AND LIMITS 

RQ4: When are more planning and parallel work worth their cost? Planning and parallelism. Doubling plans from 32 to 64 raises gain from 6.3 to 6.5 pp but lowers net return from 4.7 to 4.5 (Table 3(a)). Increasing workers from one to eight reduces elapsed time from 1,287 to 192 s, raising worker time from 1,287 to 1,403 s and gain from 6.3 to 6.5 pp (Table S17). Thus, planning and parallelism expose different resource trade-offs: the former has a finite useful range, while the latter primarily exchanges additional total work for substantially lower latency. 

Verification cost. Batching lowers verification cost in three regimes and raises it in three (Table S16). The separate lifecycle ledger gives SpecRSI (ours) a 5.7% lower total-cost index: 106.3 versus MetaRSI-v1’s 112.7 (Table 3(b)). This lifecycle result complements Figure 2’s ten-backbone target-cost analysis, which measures a different cost endpoint. ## 5 CONCLUSION 

SpecRSI’s two-level loop extends incompletely evaluated parents but adopts only complete, admis-sible versions. VATS allocates edits and complementary checks by predicted net verified return; GVO updates the improver from completed searches under a fixed verification contract. SpecRSI outperforms Janus in all 50 scientific backbone–task pairs and MetaRSI-v1 by 1.85–2.38 pp in four-task macro-average score across twenty backbones. Full planning achieves the highest mean verified gain and lowest total-cost index among tested planning conditions at all eight budget caps. Starting-artifact controls support gains from improver updates beyond retaining stronger task agents. ## A EXTENDED RELATED WORK 

Recursive Self-improvement. Unlike within-task reasoning search or adaptive inference compute (Yao et al., 2023; Snell et al., 2025), agent improvement persistently revises task solvers and their improvers. DGM, SICA, ADAS, AFlow and GEPA iteratively revise and evaluate code, workflows, or prompts (Zhang et al., 2026a; Robeyns et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b; Agrawal et al., 2026); MemEvolve co-evolves experience and memory programs, while Hyperagents and MetaRSI adapt improvement generation or scheduling (Zhang et al., 2025a; 2026b; Tan et al., 2026). HGM separates lineage-metaproductivity-guided expansion from agent–task evaluation (Wang et al., 2026b); Janus co-evolves programs and proxies but retains real promotion checks (Liu et al., 2026). SpecRSI uses VATS to connect exploration to evaluation completion, jointly selecting branches and outstanding-check bundles by attainable verified improvement under the remaining budget. 

Speculative Decoding. Speculative decoding decouples cheap drafting from target verification without changing the target distribution (Leviathan et al., 2023). SpecInfer and EAGLE-2 use token trees; Sequoia adapts their size and depth to hardware costs (Miao et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024). Multi-token prediction learns future-token heads for speculative inference (Gloeckle 16 et al., 2024); Speculative Verification adapts verification length to predicted draft–target alignment (Kim et al., 2026). Dynamic Speculative Agent Planning and Speculative Actions speculate on planning or executing actions within a task (Guan et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026); FlashEvolve asyn-chronously overlaps evolution stages, optionally starting downstream work from partially evaluated artifacts (Hu et al., 2026b). SpecRSI separates executable-successor proposals from residual-check confirmation: incomplete parents may expand, but adoption requires complete, admissible outcomes for the selected version, not every ancestor. The analogy concerns verification expenditure, not exact token sampling or one-forward-pass agent evaluation. 

Learning for Agent Improvement. Process supervision learns step evaluators; hierarchical agent RL learns long-horizon values and policies (Lightman et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024). GRPO estimates relative advantages for same-prompt responses (Shao et al., 2024); DVI trains token drafters from verifier feedback; GTO targets expected tree acceptance length (Bhansali & Heck, 2025; Hu et al., 2026a); MAS 2 trains meta-agents with value-weighted preferences from cost-sensitive terminal outcomes (Wang et al., 2026a). GVO instead credits independent complete successor searches from identical starting conditions, not responses or shared-tree leaves. Only complete admissible versions earn improvement; computation, elapsed time, failed and unfinished work remain charged. Established leave-one-out baselines and clipped updates train the drafter and work-selection policy (Ahmadian et al., 2024; Schulman et al., 2017); observed-return regression trains the scorer, with paid alternative continuations supplying scorer-only labels. These updates improve later searches while the evaluator stays fixed. The contribution is this verification-grounded, cost-sensitive learning unit integrated with successor search, not a new policy-gradient estimator. ## C WHY VERIFICATION -A WARE SEARCH HELPS 

This appendix expands Section 4.3. It separates three explanations that would otherwise be conflated: seeing more information, offering completion-aware work, and choosing how much work to execute before observing feedback. The latter’s resource regimes are examined separately in Appendix E.2. C.1 INFORMATION AND WORK SCOPE 

The information–scope study crosses two information levels (summary or full state) with two work scopes (one check or a bundle). The relevant comparison is between rows that hold one factor fixed, not just the weakest and strongest rows. With full state, gain rises from 4.8 to 6.3 pp and total cost falls from 63.1 to 59.2 when bundles are permitted (Table S9). Failure falls from 9.8% to 6.5%, while hit rate rises from 84.0% to 92.0%. These fixed-information compar-isons support completion-aware work scope as a contributor to VATS’s performance beyond richer inputs alone. The larger gain contrast under full state is consistent with complementarity between information and work scope; the 0.6 pp difference is descriptive rather than a significance-tested interaction. Speculative correspondence. A token proposal becomes an executable edit; a draft continuation becomes a path of actual revisions; a verification batch becomes a set of required checks; acceptance becomes selection of a complete admissible agent. Search progress. A draft descriptor names existing parents and a bounded path shape. Each dependent edit waits for its actual parent, while independent branches may run in isolation. Invalid edits are charged and end their path; valid prefixes remain searchable. Parents may be internal nodes, incomplete nodes, or previously failed but editable nodes. A verification descriptor names jobs on existing nodes. It adds no version edge. Formally, DRAFT : C+ = C ∪ ∆C, A+ = A ∪ ∆A,

VERIFY : T + = T , E+ = E ∪ { e ∈ ∆E : Valid Γ(e) = 1 }.

Here a superscript + denotes the post-execution object and ∆ denotes newly produced versions, edges or records; Valid Γ(e) ∈ { 0, 1} checks a record’s identity, required fields and provenance, not whether the task passed. Both operations update history and budgets. The next decision can return to another branch; there is no requirement to expand the most recent leaf. Dependencies between checks belong to a typed job graph, not the parent–child version tree. No visit-count or UCT backup is assumed.

Direct execution. Requested checks run the frozen version under Γ or read an already admitted identical record. A valid record is keyed to its candidate, item, fields, executor/checker version, randomness semantics, and relevant environment state. A record with a different key does not satisfy the requested check; fresh evaluation is required. A timeout or partial result leaves missing fields missing. A hard failure is a valid observation; it can disqualify the current version while still informing a repairable descendant. Predictions choose which verification work to buy, not which results to admit. 

Execution safeguards. Draft and verification branches execute in isolated snapshots. Changes to shared files, irreversible messages, financial actions, or external services cannot be rolled back merely because a branch was speculative. Such effects require a separately enforced execution contract. A copied state must include the relevant tool and environment state, not only a prompt string. A work cap bounds attempts and expense, not success. Every failed edit, failed check, setup, cancellation, copy, and preparation call has an explicit account. Within a synchronous reference block, feedback changes the next decision only after the block ends. An asynchronous adapter must log launches, in-flight commitments, cancellation fees, stale results, and completion times. The fastest observed configuration cannot be declared the cheapest when it also increases total worker time. Matched asynchronous comparisons cross the allocation rule with the execution scheduler, instead of attributing all speedup to the policy. F.5 TWO -S TAGE TRAINING

Proposition 4. This is a policy-class comparison, not a claim that a trained singleton learner finds its optimum. It excludes real parallel latency, nonzero model calls, setup sharing and function-approximation difficulty. An executable two-stage adapter supplies a minimal example. An old tool emits an unstructured string consumed by an old parser. At a fixed state and continuation policy, each prospectively forced arm has an ordinary expected return. Independence and fixed sample sizes make its sample mean unbiased. Their difference therefore identifies the conditional contrast in Equation (12). Minimizing population squared difference error identifies that contrast, but is invariant to a common offset added to both predictions. The ordinary return loss in Equation (11) is consequently required to anchor mean calibration. Neither identity establishes data efficiency, actor improvement, or causality of an individual edit. These are separately measured claims.
