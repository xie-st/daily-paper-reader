Abstract 

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) aims to transform interaction experience into per-sistent improvements, yet external feedback is costly and world-model predictions may become unreliable as policies evolve. We introduce RSI-Anything , a domain-general framework built on JEPA-Anything that uses predictive world models to guide action and learning-data selection. Observed feedback drives policy or work-flow improvement and conditional world-model adaptation, while component and compatibility checks govern the retention of updated policy–model pairs. Across diverse computational and scientific domains, RSI-Anything demonstrates substantial gains. In code repair, with the coder fixed, success increases from 26 .3% with a static verifier to 29 .2% through predictive selection and 31 .2% after world-model adaptation, at comparable test usage. Graphical user interface (GUI) evaluations showed higher task completion, while average actions decrease by 39 .4%. Across five successive policy versions, laboratory-planning scores more than double from 21 .80% to 46 .01%. In reaction optimization, RSI-Anything achieves 96 .15% mean best observed yield versus 82 .16% for Bayesian optimization under the same 40-experiment budget. An end-to-end immune-science application further connects workflow evolution and predictive modeling with therapeutic hypothesis generation. These results demonstrate how predictive world models and retained system updates enable recursive improvement across diverse environments. 

## 1. Introduction 

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) turns interaction experience into lasting improve-ments in a system’s ability to act and learn. Within tasks, feedback refines solutions or guides actions [1, 2]. Across rounds, self-training and preference optimization support

persistent policy or procedural updates [3–5]. Recursiveness requires these updates to be retained and shape subsequent interaction and learning. The central question is how experience from one round improves the system’s ability to act and learn in the next. Learning from external feedback requires executing or evaluating candidates before their consequences can guide improvement, limiting exploration under constrained interaction budgets. World models predict the effects of candidate actions on subsequent states or outcomes [6–8], enabling prospective evaluation and planning. Within RSI, these predictions can prioritize feedback collection and guide learning-data selection or construction. World models thus provide a predictive foundation for improvement under limited external feedback. JEPA-Anything provides a shared framework for world-model construction across hetero-geneous domains [9]. Building on joint-embedding predictive architectures [10–12], it predicts target representations from observed context. Its orthogonal predictive factor-ization (OPF) decomposes latent targets into complementary factors, learns dedicated predictors, and recombines their outputs into a complete latent state [9]. This provides a predictive foundation across domains (Figure 1). However, prediction alone does not establish recursive improvement : predicted outcomes must guide interaction, while external feedback must produce updates that persist across subsequent rounds. 

Closing this loop introduces a coupled-update challenge. Policy updates change the candi-date actions and experience distributions on which the world model operates, potentially undermining prediction reliability. Conversely, world-model updates alter candidate rankings, imagined trajectories, and the learning signals guiding policy improvement. Consequently, improving either component independently does not guarantee better joint performance . Reliable RSI therefore requires determining when to update each compo-nent, assessing prediction reliability under changing policies, and ensuring that retained policy–model pairs improve subsequent interaction and learning. 

> 2RSI-Anything

To address these challenges, we introduce RSI-Anything , a domain-general framework that integrates predictive world models with feedback-driven recursive improvement (Figure 2). World-model predictions guide action selection, prioritize external evalu-ations, and support learning-data selection or construction. External feedback drives policy learning or workflow revision and triggers world-model adaptation when re-liability checks warrant it. The policy and world model can evolve asynchronously ,with component-level checks evaluating individual updates and compatibility checks determining which policy–model pair is retained . This retained pair shapes subsequent candidates and learning experience, closing the recursive improvement loop. Our evaluations examine whether introducing world-model predictions into RSI improves candidate selection, whether retained policy or model updates yield further improve-ments, and whether these gains can be achieved under limited external feedback. We investigate these questions across general computational tasks (code repair and GUI interaction), physical-domain planning, and experimental science. We further present an end-to-end application of RSI-Anything in immune science, spanning scientific-agent workflow evolution, immune world-model construction, analysis with self-correction, and prospective therapeutic hypothesis generation [13]. Our contributions are threefold: • World models for recursive self-improvement. We introduce RSI-Anything, adomain-general framework that integrates predictive world models into recursive self-improvement to anticipate action outcomes and guide experience collection. We formulate persistent improvement at the level of the policy–world-model system, making prediction reliability under evolving policies a central design requirement. • A recursive update-and-retention mechanism. We connect prediction-guided action and learning-data selection with feedback-driven policy learning or workflow revision and conditional world-model adaptation. Component and compatibility checks determine which policy–model pair is retained, allowing asynchronous updates. The retained pair shapes subsequent candidates and learning experience, linking each accepted system revision to the next round of improvement. • Empirical evidence across domains. With the coder fixed, we demonstrate successive gains from introducing world-model-guided selection and adapting the predictor at similar online test usage. Further evaluations show higher GUI task completion, improvement across five successive laboratory-planning policy versions, and higher reaction yields than Bayesian optimization at matched experiment counts. An end-to-end immune-science application illustrates how RSI-Anything connects retained workflows and predictive models with evidence-guided analysis and hypothesis generation.

## 3. Results 

We examined how world-model predictions improve action selection, how retained updates support further improvement, and how prediction and feedback guide deci-sions under limited interaction budgets. Code repair and GUI interaction cover general computational tasks, alongside physical-domain laboratory planning and experimental reaction optimization. Within these settings, we evaluate predictive selection, model adaptation, task completion, successive policy versions, and search at matched experi-ment counts. We then examine an end-to-end application of RSI-Anything in immune science, connecting scientific-agent workflow evolution, world-model construction, and scientific analysis. 

3.1. World-model-guided selection and adaptation improve code repair 

We evaluated two contributions of RSI-Anything in code repair: introducing world-model predictions for candidate selection and obtaining further gains by updating the predictor with execution feedback. The ReasonFlux-Coder-7B policy was held fixed [14]. We compared RSI-Anything with a fixed world model (CURE-WM-Lite) and RSI-Anything with world-model adaptation. All selection methods used the same coder and candidate budget: four revisions were proposed before one was selected for execution. Evaluation covered 200 CodeContests tasks [15] across three seeds and up to three feedback rounds, with online tests supplying feedback and hidden tests measuring final success. Introducing world-model-guided selection increased final hidden-test success to 29 .2% (175 /600), compared with 26 .3% for a capacity-matched static verifier and 26 .0% for random selection (Figure 3; Table 2). The paired gain over the static verifier was +2.83 percentage points (95% interval [+ 0.17 , +5.50 ]). In this fixed-world-model versus static-verifier comparison, candidate-ranking accuracy increased from 66 .5% to 72 .8%, and the five-class Brier score [16] decreased from 0 .488 to 0 .358. Online test-suite usage was similar: 3 .71 calls per task for the fixed-world-model configuration and 3 .72 for the static verifier. These results connect improved prediction and ranking with higher repair success under the same candidate budget. Feedback-driven world-model adaptation produced an additional gain over fixed-model selection. With the coder and candidate budget retained, RSI-Anything with world-model adaptation reached 31 .2% hidden-test success, exceeding the fixed-world-model configuration by 2 .0 percentage points. Online test-suite usage was 3 .72 calls per task. Execution feedback informed program repair and revised the predictor used for subsequent candidate selection. The two comparisons show successive gains from introducing predictive selection and then adapting the world model, with similar online test usage. 3.4. Prediction- and feedback-guided search improves reaction yields at matched experiment counts 

We examined the use of limited external feedback in experimental science by comparing reaction-condition search at matched experiment counts. RSI-Anything used world-model yield predictions to rank candidate conditions for experimental evaluation. Each measured yield extended the observation history used to guide subsequent condition choices, linking prediction, experiment, and the next selection. We evaluated this process in two Suzuki–Miyaura screens, comparing RSI-Anything with Bayesian optimization [28] within each screening protocol. Figure 6 shows yield progression with cumulative experiments (b, e) and endpoint com-parisons at matched experiment counts (c, f). After 20 experiments in the GC-analyzed Pd–NHC–DMS screen, the best observed yield reached 93 .0% with RSI-Anything and 87 .5% with Bayesian optimization, a gain of 5 .5 percentage points. In the flow LC–MS screen using Pd(OAc) 2, the mean best observed yield after 40 experiments was 96 .15%, compared with 82 .16% for Bayesian optimization, a gain of 13 .99 percentage points. At the matched endpoints within each screen, prediction- and feedback-guided search achieved higher best-yield metrics from the same number of experiments. These re-sults support using world-model forecasts together with measured outcomes to guide condition selection under a limited experimental budget. 

3.5. An end-to-end application of RSI-Anything in immune science 

We applied RSI-Anything to an end-to-end immune-science workflow that connects persistent workflow development, predictive world-model construction, and evidence-guided scientific analysis. During construction, Agent Genesis searched scientific-agent workflows and model configurations and selected a model-building agent workflow [13]. The retained workflow produced an action-conditioned immune world model. During downstream analysis, an analysis agent used this model with fixed parame-ters to coordinate simulation, evaluation, tool use, analysis revision, and hypothesis prioritization. The model represents cellular, tissue, and individual immune states and predicts the consequences of explicit interventions (Figure 7). Measured perturbation evidence supports model development, and withheld interventions and biological contexts assess forecasting and action composition. In the analysis stage, model forecasts and exper-imental evidence inform intervention ranking and self-correction. The construction stage retains the selected model-building workflow and resulting predictor; subsequent analysis uses the constructed predictor to revise candidate interventions and hypotheses. In therapeutic hypothesis generation, self-correction removed the screened cytokine-pair candidates, and the integrated evidence led to a prospective nomination combining SIRP 𝛼 inhibition with IL-36 𝛾 -axis modulation [13]. This case connects a retained scientific-agent workflow, a predictive world model, and evidence-guided analysis within one scientific workflow, culminating in a concrete hypothesis for experimental testing.

## 4. Related Work 

4.1. Recursive self-improvement 

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) has evolved from improving reasoning and code to adapting interactive agents and their underlying systems. In coding, CURE co-evolves code generation and unit testing through execution feedback [14], while Self-Improving Coding Agent and Darwin Godel Machine iteratively modify agent implementations and retain empirically validated improvements [29, 30]. AIDE 2 extends this approach to self-improving AI research agents [31]. Beyond code, Voyager accumulates reusable executable skills [32], while GenEnv and Agent0 adapt task curricula to evolving agent capabilities [33, 34]. RLAnything jointly optimizes the policy, reward model, and environment [21], and OpenClaw-RL enables online policy improvement from user interactions and tool feedback [35]. Long-horizon RSI further requires improvements to persist across extended interactions. Recuris couples Experiential and Working Memory with validation-gated memory evolution [36], while ScienceBuddy introduces nested self-improvement through harness refinement and model reinforcement learning [37]. These approaches improve agent code, policies, memories, harnesses, or training environments. RSI-Anything extends this line by explicitly incorporating predictive world models into the recursive loop: predictions guide candidate selection, observations assess model 

18 RSI-Anything 

reliability, and policy–model compatibility determines which updated components are retained for subsequent interactions. 

4.2. Verification and policy self-improvement 

Verifiers, self-training, preference optimization, and verbal feedback turn evaluated trajectories into signals for selection and learning [1–5]. CURE [14] co-evolves a coder and unit tester, providing the ReasonFlux-Coder policy used in our code evaluation. RLAnything jointly optimizes the policy, reward model, and environment through feed-back [21]. These approaches motivate the feedback-to-policy part of RSI-Anything. Our framework additionally makes world-model reliability and policy–model compatibility explicit in update acceptance: changing the policy changes the candidate distribution on which its predictor must be evaluated. 

4.3. Predictive world models 

World Models [6], MuZero [8], Dreamer [7], and Genie [38] establish predictive envi-ronments as a basis for control, planning, and interactive generation. Joint-embedding predictive architectures learn latent targets without reconstructing every observation detail [10–12], and JEPA-Anything extends this line through OPF across heterogeneous data types [9]. Building on these predictive capabilities, RSI-Anything organizes how pre-dictions and observed outcomes guide action selection and the acceptance of subsequent policy or world-model versions. 

4.4. Scientific and laboratory agents 

Vision-language-action and cross-embodiment datasets motivate action-conditioned models for physical systems [39–41]. LabHorizon measures visual next-action prediction and protocol-aligned planning [22]. In chemistry, language-model tools, yield predictors, Bayesian optimization, and robotic laboratories provide complementary components for selecting and testing reactions [28, 42–46]. ReactionT5 [47] is a reaction-pretrained transformer that supports product, retrosynthesis, and yield prediction; our chemistry adapter combines this reaction-prediction backbone with the predictive design of JEPA-Anything [9] for action-conditioned yield forecasting. Single-cell foundation models and perturbation predictors support cellular-response forecasting [48–51]. RSI-Anything connects these domain capabilities through a common transition and feedback contract, so that the outcome of a program, experiment, or intervention can inform candidate selection and subsequent system updates. 

## 5. Discussion 

The results support two roles for world models in recursive improvement: informing which actions receive external feedback and providing a predictor that can itself improve from that feedback. Predictive selection and model adaptation yielded successive gains in code repair, while retained policy versions improved laboratory planning. In GUI tasks, RSI-Anything updated both the policy and the world model and achieved higher task completion, with fewer repeated clicks and incorrect-submission episodes on the 

> 19 RSI-Anything

checkbox task. Together with measured-feedback search and the end-to-end immune-science workflow, these findings connect prediction-guided decisions to learning and retained system changes. The common contribution is a procedure for using prediction– observation pairs to guide current actions and assess changes to the system that will make subsequent decisions. The design links decisions about actions to decisions about system versions. Pairing predictions with observations allows the same experience to inform candidate utility and predictive reliability. Policies and world models can change on different schedules: a reliable predictor can support policy or workflow development, and new outcome evidence can motivate predictor adaptation while the policy remains fixed. Reliability checks govern when model adaptation is attempted, and component and compatibility checks govern which version is carried forward. Retaining accepted versions makes subsequent experience depend on the system’s accumulated improvements. Domain generality concerns the reuse of this improvement mechanism across domain-specific world models, policies, and evaluation procedures. The evaluations support predictive selection, predictor adaptation, successive policy improvement, and feedback-guided search in their respective settings. Joint multiround evaluation is needed to establish sustained mutual improvement of the policy and world model within a single task. The present laboratory results assess protocol-aligned planning; reliable physical execution requires evaluation on the relevant equipment and workflows. The chemistry results cover two Suzuki–Miyaura screens under the reported experiment budgets, and the therapeutic hypothesis generated in the immune-science application awaits experimental testing. 

## 6. Experimental Methods 

The domain settings instantiate the shared framework with different states, action spaces, prediction targets, and feedback sources. Our code execution predictor, W1, sup-plies execution-class probabilities for candidate–test pairs, ReactionT5–JEPA-Anything supplies a yield distribution, and the immune world model supplies immune-state and biological readouts. Table 4 summarizes the domain-specific models, prediction targets, feedback sources, and update pathways evaluated in each application. The following sections describe the evaluation settings and statistical reporting. The experiments instantiate distinct parts of the full protocol. Code repair holds the coder fixed while testing predictive selection and predictor adaptation; laboratory planning follows five successive policy versions. GUI evaluations measure task completion and action behavior as RSI-Anything updates both the policy and the world model. Chemistry tests feedback-guided condition search, and the immune-science application separates workflow evolution and model construction from downstream analysis with a fixed predictor.

6.2. Statistical reporting 

Success rates are reported over 600 CodeContests task-seed evaluations. We report the paired percentage-point difference between RSI-Anything with a fixed world model and the static verifier, together with its 95% interval. Tasks recur across seeds, so task-level resampling is the unit for clustered inference. APPS and LiveCodeBench report the arithmetic mean of final full-test success rates across three seeds, corresponding to 3,000 and 3,165 problem–seed evaluations, respectively. LabHorizon F200 reports mean scores over 200 tasks [22]. The 24 .21- and 0 .69-percentage-point gains are differences between these benchmark means. Chemistry reports the observed running best within each optimization sequence, with Bayesian optimization as the comparator. Evaluation settings for the immune-science application are described in [13].
