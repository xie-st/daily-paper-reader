## ABSTRACT 

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) enables agents to iteratively optimize their workflows via execution feedback. However, standard RSI typically operates as a first-order optimizer : it repeatedly patches surface-level parameters in response to immediate failure symptoms, often leading to trial-and-error thrashing without resolving underlying mechanisms. To address this limitation, we introduce SO-RSI, a framework that elevates workflow optimization to a second-order diagnostic inquiry —investigating why failures occur before committing to structural interven-tions. SO-RSI passively monitors execution traces for three structural anomalies (recurrence, opposing edits, and expectation mismatch) to trigger targeted mecha-nism investigations. By executing lightweight diagnostic probes and maintaining persistent inquiry memory across RSI rounds, SO-RSI accumulates causal evi-dence to guide systematic workflow edits rather than parameter patches. Across Lean 4 proof generation and Verus-based verifiable code generation, SO-RSI im-proves final held-out pass rates over Naive RSI by 21.8 and 25.8 percentage points under matched 24-hour search budgets. Behavioral analyses further confirm that SO-RSI substantially suppresses failure recurrence and eliminates unproductive zero-progress optimization loops. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) progressively improves an executable agent through repeated cycles of observing problems, exploring their causes, and attempting solutions (Robeyns et al., 2025). While such quick fixes can efficiently restore progress, remaining at this level of first-order problem solving can leave the underlying causes of those problems unexplored (Tucker & Edmondson, 2003). Consider a RSI trajectory for a Lean 4 proof agentic workflow that retrieves lemmas before generating a proof (Figure 1). When a proof attempt fails, first-order RSI relies on immediate quick fixes. Observing a failure due to missing lemmas, RSI directly increases the retrieval count to k = 20 —a quick fix that temporarily restores progress. However, a subsequent attempt fails as the excessive lemma text exceeds the context limit, prompting RSI to apply the opposite quick fix by decreasing the count to k = 15 . In a later round, as the reduced window omits critical lemmas again, RSI fine-tunes the parameter back up to k = 18 , only to encounter the original context-overflow error in the next iteration, forcing yet another reduction. This oscillation reveals a fundamental blind spot of first-order problem solving: misinterpreting a structural failure—low retrieval relevance—as a mere hyperparameter tuning task of search-testing for the "optimal lemma count," thereby failing to investigate the core mechanism of re-ranking and filtering. This observation reveals a central risk for RSI. First-order problem solving is necessary to overcome immediate obstacles, but stopping at this level leaves the causes of recurring problems unresolved. RSI may then repeatedly confront similar failures and face conflicting prescriptions, without a clear basis for deciding which to follow. What restores progress in one attempt can therefore leave the agent caught between opposing changes in the next. Appendix B.1 provides further examples involving failure history and tactic fallbacks.

Recent work on RSI and agent improvement already supports reflection, failure diagnosis, and workflow modification (Robeyns et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026). The remaining question is how to direct this research when immediate fixes leave conflicting outcomes unexplained. Second-order problem solving provides a concrete target: investigate how the workflow produces the problem, obtain evidence that distinguishes explanations, and use it to guide a change to that process (Tucker & Edmondson, 2003). The depth of inquiry is determined by this target, rather than by the length of reflection or the number of follow-up questions. Our work addresses three fundamental questions. First, first-order RSI is limited by an explanation gap : LLMs easily propose plausible failure explanations, but without explicit verification, they resort to blind parameter patching that fails to resolve structural flaws. Second, elevating to second-order inquiry is necessary because stopping recurring failures requires diagnosing the underlying mechanism rather than patching surface symptoms, replacing conflicting edits with durable workflow improvements. Third, we operationalize second-order inquiry through a persistent diagnostic architecture that detects anomalies, formulates testable hypotheses, validates them via executable probes, and executes evidence-guided workflow edits. We introduce SO-RSI, which monitors three signals: recurrence, opposing edits, and expectation mismatch. Triggered observations initiate mechanism investigation (I): gathering evidence, forming explanations, and testing predictions. Evidence accumulates across RSI rounds and guides workflow improvements (G), which return to ordinary RSI for evaluation and further optimization. Across Lean proving and Verus-based verifiable code generation (VCG), SO-RSI improves final pass rate over Naive RSI by 21.8 and 25.8 percentage points under the same 24-hour budget. Our contributions are threefold: • Diagnostic analysis: We document reversals in recorded RSI decisions and audit the evidence supporting their causal explanations. • Empirical motivation: We quantify investigation depth, intervention gains, and repeated unproductive editing across 134 attempts. 2Preprint • The SO-RSI framework: We use second-order problem solving to specify the target and evidence requirements of inquiry, integrating selective detection and persistent investigation into continuing RSI, with ablations supporting the value of probes and memory. 

## 2 PROBLEM FORMULATION 

Two levels of iteration. Consider an agent attempting a formal proof or program verification task 

x. The inner loop repairs a specific candidate artifact (e.g., a Lean 4 proof script or code annotation) given compiler feedback, keeping the governing agent procedure fixed. The outer RSI loop —the primary focus of this work—improves the executable procedure itself, denoted as St at round t. This workflow St coordinates generation, lemma retrieval, verification, and retry policies; a workflow-level improvement updates these reusable control components rather than the underlying language model weights. Executing St on x produces a trace τx of model interactions, tool outputs, and compiler feedback. An improvement agent analyzes these traces to propose a workflow edit, which is adopted into St+1 if validated on dev benchmarks. 

Symptoms and mechanisms. A symptom is a failure visible in τx, such as an unknown identifier error. A mechanism explains how the workflow St produces that failure. For example, the model may have generated the required local definition, but the code extractor discarded it before compilation. The error suggests adding a definition; tracing the mechanism instead suggests repairing the extractor. This explanation is testable: compare the raw response with the submitted proof, restore the omitted definition, and recompile. We use mechanism hypothesis for such an explanation and 

diagnostic probe for a check of its predicted behavior. 

Objective. Our goal is to improve final task performance within a research budget:

## 3 FROM OBSERVED OUTCOMES TO SUPPORTED EXPLANATIONS 

RSI can often make progress through first-order problem solving: observe a failure, apply a plausible fix, and continue. But when is such a quick fix enough? A local intervention may resolve the current failure while leaving unexplained why it occurred, allowing the same underlying problem to reappear or invite a later, opposing fix. This motivates a second-order question: does the agent investigate the workflow mechanism that generates the failure before continuing to optimize it? Inspired by the distinction between immediate workarounds and efforts to address underlying causes in Tucker & Edmondson (2003), we operationalize this transition for RSI as a progression in observable inquiry. F (direct repair) applies an immediate fix without a workflow-mechanism explanation. H (mechanism hypothesis) proposes such an explanation but does not test it. I (mechanism investigation) performs a diagnostic check of the proposed mechanism. G (evidence-guided intervention) uses the resulting evidence to determine the intervention. U (insufficient information) marks attempts whose inquiry process cannot be determined from the available record. For a conservative binary summary, only F is counted as first-order; H, I, and G represent progressively stronger observable second-order inquiry, while U is grouped on the second-order side only to avoid inflating the first-order count. Appendix A provides the full decision rules and worked examples. To examine the gap between reactive patching and structural diagnosis, we audit five independent Lean 4 proof RSI trajectories comprising 134 optimization attempts. We ask two research questions: • RQ1: How often does RSI move beyond direct patching? • RQ2: What gains does RSI achieve, and when does further patching become unproductive?

The revised workflow then returns to ordinary RSI for evaluation. Appendix D describes the controller and investigation interfaces. 4.1 LIGHTWEIGHT DETECTION OF IMPROVEMENT ANOMALIES 

After each RSI optimization attempt, the detector logs a structured record Xt = {vt, d t, r t}, where 

vt is the workflow version, dt specifies the modified component and textual edit, and rt captures the intended rationale. Rather than running additional benchmark evaluations, the detector filters prior records by component or failure type, then uses a short LLM call to compare Xt with the selected traces τx for three structural improvement anomalies : recurrence , where targeted failure signatures persist or re-emerge across consecutive rounds despite attempted edits; reversal , where edges or parameters are directly toggled or opposingly altered for similar symptoms; and expectation mismatch , where observed outcomes directly contradict the stated rationale rt or predicted behavior. When any anomaly is detected, Xt and its context are routed to initiate or update a mechanism investigation; absent any signal, ordinary RSI continues without investigation. Detection calls count toward the research budget. 4.2 MECHANISM INVESTIGATION (I) Given a flagged record Xt, the system investigates the root cause before committing to permanent workflow edits. Investigation operates via a two-stage diagnostic loop: hypothesis formation and 

hypothesis testing .

Hypothesis formation. The questioner first retrieves relevant historical attempts to contrast tra-jectories. For instance, observing a successful attempt with fewer lemmas directly challenges the naive assumption that retrieval count is the primary bottleneck. The questioner then analyzes work-flow interactions to propose non-mutually-exclusive causal hypotheses (e.g., failure to retrieve vs. suboptimal ranking). Each hypothesis specifies the implicated workflow operation, supporting or conflicting observations, a testable prediction, and a proposed check. When domain mechanics or diagnostic methods are uncertain, it consults external documentation or code specs on demand to ground its hypotheses. 

Hypothesis testing (Diagnostic Probing). The questioner generates a targeted diagnostic script or tool call, which the controller executes in an isolated workspace. It first inspects existing artifacts before requesting new generations. For example, saved retrieval lists distinguish missing lemmas from low-ranked ones; comparing original and reranked contexts under fixed context budgets and 5Preprint generator settings then tests the effect of ranking. The agent compares actual outputs with the recorded prediction. A completed check supporting an actionable workflow change permits handoff to G; otherwise the hypothesis is revised or retained for later investigation. 

Persistent investigation memory. Inspired by experience maintenance in AutoManual (Chen et al., 2024), we maintain an investigation record containing linked attempts, hypotheses, executed checks, their results, and the next open question. Related anomalies update the same investigation, allowing later RSI rounds to resume from the evidence already collected. 4.3 EVIDENCE -G UIDED IMPROVEMENT (G) G reuses the ordinary RSI editing agent, supplying the supported explanation, probe results, target code, and expected behavioral change. Its output is a workflow patch, which may adjust an existing component or introduce a new one. For the retrieval example, evidence of ranking failure motivates reranking and filtering before context construction. The candidate revision returns to the outer RSI loop for validation. Only accepted edits update St+1 ;all evaluation results feed back into investigation memory. Subsequent RSI can further optimize accepted changes under the shared budget in Equation 1.

## 6 ABLATION STUDY 

Two ablations test the central design choices. Without executable probes retains hypothesis formation and textual assessment but removes diagnostic execution, testing the value of checking explanations against actual behavior. Without persistent investigation memory clears investigation records between outer rounds while preserving accepted workflow edits and ordinary RSI logs, testing the value of accumulating inquiry across attempts. Each uses the same overall research budget as the full method. Table 4 reports final held-out pass rate for both domains. 

Both diagnostic execution and memory contribute. Removing executable probes lowers final pass rate by 7.8 points on Lean and 26.2 points on VCG; removing persistent investigation memory lowers it by 10.3 and 22.5 points (Table 4). Textual hypothesis formation alone does not recover the full benefit, and restarting investigations each round also weakens improvement. Both ablations still outperform Naive RSI on Lean, whereas the VCG probe ablation falls close to the baseline. These results support combining executable checks with evidence retained across rounds. 6.1 ANALYSIS OF IMPROVEMENT BEHAVIOR

## 7 RELATED WORK 

7.1 RECURSIVE SELF -I MPROVEMENT AND WORKFLOW DIAGNOSIS 

SICA improves a coding agent by letting it edit its own implementation and evaluate the resulting changes (Robeyns et al., 2025). HarnessFix more directly connects failed trajectories to harness mechanisms, consolidates diagnoses, and validates targeted repairs (Chen et al., 2026). These works establish self-modification and mechanism diagnosis as existing capabilities. SO-RSI studies when repeated improvement attempts call for further investigation: recurring failures, opposing edits, and unmet expectations trigger an inquiry that persists across RSI rounds and tests explanations before guiding another workflow edit. 7.2 SECOND -O RDER PROBLEM SOLVING 

Tucker & Edmondson (2003) distinguish immediate workarounds from efforts to address the processes that produce recurring failures. We adapt this distinction to outer RSI, using recorded hypotheses, diagnostic checks, and evidence-guided interventions to characterize inquiry. The distinction concerns what an agent investigates and tests, rather than the size of its edit. SO-RSI retains ordinary repairs while selectively investigating the workflow mechanisms behind unresolved patterns. A related distinction separates comprehension failures from faulty reasoning, motivating changes to how a model reads a question (Han et al., 2026a); our investigation concerns the reusable workflow that produces failures across attempts. 7.3 AGENTIC FORMAL VERIFICATION 

LeanSearch v2 improves Lean proving through global premise retrieval, while RAG-Verus retrieves repository context for program verification (Gao et al., 2026; Zhong et al., 2025). ExVerus generates and validates counterexamples to guide Verus proof repair (Yang et al., 2026). These methods provide concrete ways to improve the information available to a solver. In code security, behavior-aware extraction and demonstration selection likewise organize the context supplied to an LLM detector (Han et al., 2025). SO-RSI addresses the outer process that chooses such changes: it uses diagnostic evidence to determine which workflow mechanism to revise. Retrieval and counterexample reasoning are possible interventions, rather than components prescribed in advance by our method. 

Context-sensitive diagnosis and guidance. Xu et al. (2026) condition microservice anomaly detection on invocation chains, exposing path failures that appear normal when events are considered separately. SO-RSI similarly considers the context of an observed failure, but investigates patterns across workflow edits rather than predicting anomalous service events. Li et al. (2026b) compose lightweight adapters using educational state and calibrate answer options against multimodal evidence. Their task-specific adaptation is distinct from our outer-loop investigation, which determines which reusable workflow mechanism needs to change. Li et al. (2026a) find that more elaborate AI-generated theories do not necessarily predict empirical results better; SO-RSI consequently tests mechanism explanations rather than rewarding their complexity. Broader comparisons with reasoning supervision and task-grounded evaluation appear in Appendix H. 

## 8 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 

We studied second-order problem solving for outer RSI in Lean proof generation and Verus-based code verification. SO-RSI detects improvement anomalies, maintains investigations across rounds, and uses executable diagnostic evidence to guide workflow interventions. Under the same 24-hour outer budget, it improves final held-out pass rate over Naive RSI by 21.8 percentage points on Lean and 25.8 points on VCG. Ablations show that both executable probes and persistent investigation memory contribute to these gains. The behavioral analysis links these gains to more frequent mechanism testing, more evidence-driven revisions, and stronger intervention outcomes. Diagnosed failures recur less often, and zero-progress loops decline in both domains. The aim is selective escalation: direct repairs remain useful, while 9Preprint recurring or conflicting outcomes warrant investigation of the workflow mechanism that produces them. 

Limitations. Our evaluation covers two formal-verification domains and one outer improvement model. Behavioral findings concern the recorded trajectories and audited mechanisms; generalization to other models and workflows remains an open question.

Overall gains and retained progress. Each trajectory’s overall gain compares its initial workflow with the final workflow selected by that run’s acceptance rule on the same task set. The highest observed trial score is not substituted for the final accepted score; any best-trial statistic is reported separately. All 134 optimization attempts contribute to their trajectories’ recorded research costs, including investigations and rejected trials. Cost coverage includes improvement-agent and task-model calls where recorded; incomplete accounting is reported explicitly rather than treated as zero. Overall gains are not obtained by summing trial deltas and are not causally partitioned among inquiry labels, because accepted descendants inherit and interact with earlier edits. Retained progress is assessed on comparable descendant evaluations of an accepted intervention against its original parent, using both task success and model-call or token costs. The underlying record retains the original fine-grained labels. Group comparisons report eligible evaluation counts, and investigations without an evaluated workflow change are not counted as ties. Event-count comparisons are descriptive and must account for the unequal group sizes and available edit opportunities. ## C RESEARCH RECORDS AND EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

C.1 MECHANISM RECORD 

Each record contains the observed phenomenon, applicable task conditions, raw trace references, competing mechanism hypotheses, predicted outcomes, executed probe code and results, unresolved alternatives, a proposed workflow intervention, and a recurrence check. Hypotheses retain the status 

unresolved , supported under tested conditions , or contradicted ; no language-model confidence score alone changes a hypothesis to supported. An inconclusive probe and an unsuccessful implementation are distinct outcomes. C.2 PROBE AND INTERVENTION PROTOCOL 

Before executing a probe, the agent records the manipulated factor, held-fixed artifacts, observations expected under each live hypothesis, and its budget limit. Pure transformation replay is preferred 15 Preprint when it can answer the question without another model call. When generation is necessary, the model snapshot, decoding settings, prompt, and random seeds are logged; repeated calls still require empirical variability checks. A positive result authorizes only the conclusion supported by the manipulation. Broader causal claims require additional controls. Before implementing a workflow change, the agent specifies a mechanism-level prediction and a task-level acceptance criterion. For retrieval, the first may be retaining a needed lemma in context; the second is proof validity and task performance. An intervention can pass the first while failing the second because other proof errors remain. The record retains this distinction. New components expose their inputs, outputs, state, and tunable choices, but the framework does not prescribe the component type. Later edits are checked against the same task constraints and update the record when their behavior changes. C.3 DETAILED OUTCOME MEASUREMENTS

C.5 SECOND -O RDER BEHAVIOR ANALYSIS 

Units and annotation. An investigation episode begins when the agent explicitly examines a reusable workflow mechanism behind an observed pattern, and includes subsequent inquiry into that same question until closure or the run ends. An episode can span several RSI rounds; a reopen is linked to its original record. Direct artifact fixes without mechanism inquiry are not episodes. We reconstruct episodes from the full traces of every method, including Naive RSI and the memory ablation, without relying on method-specific record IDs. Annotation follows Appendix A; unknown cases remain explicit. Independent reviewers apply the same criteria to all variants and resolve disagreements with reference to recorded artifacts. 

Tested inquiry and decision revision. The tested-inquiry rate is the fraction of all episodes, including unfinished ones, containing a mechanism explanation, an explicit testable prediction, and an executed diagnostic check capable of discriminating that explanation from an alternative. Ordinary post-edit pass-rate evaluation alone does not qualify. The decision-revision rate uses the same denominator and counts tested episodes with a traceable probe-result-to-decision link that changes an explanation, redirects an intervention, or withdraws a proposal. A changed prompt or parameter without such a link does not count. A high revision rate is not inherently desirable, since an initially correct explanation may survive testing. 

G acceptance and gain. G edits are identified using the existing evidence-guided intervention definition, independently of whether the run invokes a module named G. Acceptance is accepted G edits divided by evaluated G edits. Gain is the arithmetic mean of development-pass-rate differences between each evaluated G candidate and its own parent, measured on the same problem set under matched task-solving limits; rejected candidates are included. Unevaluated edits are excluded and counted separately. This measures observed single-step gain, including interactions with inherited edits, rather than a causal allocation of the final trajectory improvement. 

Mechanism recurrence. For each audited accepted G edit, freeze a mechanism-identification check and an eligible case set before comparing its parent and child workflows. Using the recurrence definition above, compute 100 [ Rm(Schild ; Em) − Rm(Sparent ; Em)] in percentage points. Average over audited interventions within each run. Report audit coverage, eligible case counts, and exposure changes, retaining unresolved or unobservable outcomes rather than treating them as resolved failures. The paired comparison measures immediate recurrence reduction. Audit results from held-out cases never feed back into RSI. 

Aggregation. For the three behavioral rates, pool the reported numerator and denominator counts across runs and compute their ratio, separately for Lean and VCG. ## D IMPLEMENTATION SPECIFICATION 

This appendix describes the controller, investigation records, and tool interfaces for the method in Section 4. Task-level settings are reported in Appendix C.4. D.1 CONTROLLER AND MODEL CALLS 

A Python controller coordinates calls to one fixed base LLM with distinct contexts and instructions for detection, investigation, and editing. No additional training is required. The controller performs record retrieval, tool execution, persistence, and budget accounting. The outer and inner model configurations follow Table 11. After an ordinary RSI attempt finishes, the controller logs its edit and outcome, checks for anomalies, and updates the corresponding investigation. It advances at most one investigation step before resuming ordinary RSI. A step forms or revises hypotheses, runs a probe, or hands a supported finding to G; it need not complete an investigation. Eligible investigations are processed by the oldest pending update to avoid repeatedly favoring the newest signal. A G proposal enters the next ordinary edit–evaluate cycle. Investigation memory starts empty, without a hand-authored library of mechanisms or fixes. D.2 ATTEMPT RECORDS AND ANOMALY DETECTION 

The compact record Xt links to the following stored fields:               

> attempt_id parent_version, candidate_version component, dimension, before, after rationale, expected_outcome #expected_outcome is null if not explicitly stated observed_outcome, evaluation_result, trace_paths

18 Preprint Code diffs and logs supply available structured fields; a short extraction call summarizes natural-language edits when necessary. Observations retain links to their source artifacts. Each modified dimension has an entry linked to the same attempt, preserving the distinction between a multi-part edit and independent trials. The controller searches recent attempts for matching components or normalized failure types. If none are found, it skips historical semantic comparison, but still checks an explicit current prediction against the current outcome when both are available. Otherwise, it supplies the current record and a bounded set of related records to the detector prompt. The prompt requests an anomaly type, supporting attempt IDs, and the specific observations that justify the flag. Recurrence requires a targeted failure persisting or returning after a remedy; reversal requires opposing changes addressing a similar symptom; mismatch requires an explicit prediction contradicted by an observed outcome. Missing predictions or missing measurements cannot establish mismatch. Any detected signal enters memory without an additional root-cause investigation at this stage. D.3 INVESTIGATION MEMORY AND RETRIEVAL 

Each investigation is stored as a local JSON record with an ID, affected component, linked attempts, current question, hypotheses, completed probes and results, next check, and status. The statuses are 

open , deferred , ready_for_G , and monitoring . Hypothesis support is stored separately from investigation status. For a new anomaly, the controller first retrieves investigations associated with the component. An LLM compares their questions with the new observation and chooses an existing ID or requests a new record. Matching compiler messages alone is insufficient to merge investigations. Memory writes append evidence references and preserve previous results, including negative results. A deferred record specifies what is already known and what remains to be checked; an accepted intervention moves the investigation to monitoring, while new conflicting observations can reopen it. For hypothesis formation, historical attempts are filtered by component and ranked by embedding similarity between the current anomaly description and their edit rationales. The retrieved set includes both successes and failures when available; absent contrasts are not invented. The model receives these records, the investigation memory, and the implicated workflow code and prompts, with file-reading tools for additional inspection. D.4 HYPOTHESIS FORMATION AND EXTERNAL KNOWLEDGE 

The formation prompt asks for a workflow-level question and a small set of possible causes. Every hypothesis must supply:                         

> mechanism: workflow operation that may produce the failure observations: supporting and conflicting trace references prediction: observable consequence if the explanation holds check: executable way to inspect that consequence

A proposed parameter change without a mechanism and a prediction does not complete this step. The hypotheses need not be mutually exclusive. For example, missing retrieval coverage and poor ranking can affect different cases within the same investigation. A validator checks required fields and source references; the diagnostic probe, rather than schema compliance, tests the substantive explanation. External retrieval is optional. The model must identify a specific information need, such as the semantics of a retrieval API or a way to test whether a lemma applies to a goal. It searches project code and official documentation first, then research literature as needed. Queries, retrieved sources, and the hypothesis or check they inform are saved. External sources can suggest a cause or a test but cannot establish that the cause occurs in the current workflow. A model’s assessment of lemma relevance likewise remains a hypothesis until checked. D.5 PROBE GENERATION , S ELECTION , AND EXECUTION 

Available tools read and compare files, execute Python analysis scripts, call Lean or Verus, and replay a selected workflow stage. For each proposed probe, the LLM specifies the hypothesis, input cases or 19 Preprint artifacts, script or tool arguments, held-fixed conditions, predicted distinguishing observation, and execution cap. The controller checks paths and required fields and executes the probe in a temporary workspace or checkout, leaving the accepted workflow unchanged. Probe selection prefers existing-log inspection, followed by verifier checks or stage replay, and requests new generations only when needed. Among affordable checks, the prompt asks for one whose possible outcomes would change the proposed intervention or distinguish unresolved explanations. This is a qualitative selection rule, not a learned estimate of information gain. A probe that cannot fit within the remaining research allowance is deferred. The executor records stdout, stderr, exit status, timing, verifier outcomes, and any generated artifacts. A script failure is recorded as an execution problem, not evidence against the hypothesis. Directly measurable observations are extracted by code; an LLM compares them with the pre-recorded prediction and returns supported under tested conditions , contradicted , or inconclusive , citing actual outputs. A confidence score alone cannot establish support. At least one completed diagnostic check supporting an identifiable workflow intervention is required for handoff to G. This authorizes an edit proposal, not a claim that the root cause has been conclusively established. Contradictory or inconclusive results update the question and next check. When a meaningful comparison requires more repetitions than the remaining allowance, the result remains unresolved and the inquiry is deferred. D.6 INTERVENTION HANDOFF 

G receives the hypothesis and its scope, executed probe code and results, implicated workflow files, expected behavioral change, and task constraints. The ordinary RSI editing agent generates a patch and its expected effect. Parameter changes, modifications to existing components, and new components are all permitted when justified by the diagnosis. The original development evaluator and acceptance rule decide whether to retain the patch; diagnostic support alone never merges it. Both accepted and rejected outcomes are appended to investigation memory. Held-out tasks do not guide these decisions.

## H ADDITIONAL RELATED WORK 

Reasoning supervision and information quality. IPS uses auxiliary questions to provide process supervision for short-video moderation (Liu et al., 2026a), while reasoning-enhanced domain-adaptive pretraining combines captioning, question answering, and reasoning traces (Wang et al., 2025). Both improve task reasoning through model training; SO-RSI instead investigates the reusable workflow around a fixed model. Latent-space broadening targets informative annotation candidates, and audio-enhanced vision-language modeling improves available multimodal evidence (Sun et al., 2025). These are examples of changing information supply, a distinct intervention from rewriting instructions; our investigation seeks evidence for which kind of workflow change is needed. 

Task-grounded evaluation. WorldCoder-Bench checks runtime states and transitions in generated 3D programs through executable probes (Lu et al., 2026); SO-RSI uses probes to test explanations of workflow failures before constructing an intervention. Outside code, ReVA tests scene-centric and temporal reasoning in remote-sensing videos rather than relying on repetitive static-image ques-tions (Yao et al., 2026b). The survey by Yao et al. (2026a) organizes language-guided segmentation from semantic matching through referring expressions to reasoning-based guidance. These works dis-tinguish the capabilities a task actually requires; our first-/second-order distinction instead describes whether improvement attempts investigate the process producing recurring failures. RAGScope evaluates inexpensive evidence gates that route uncertain RAG outputs for further review (Liu et al., 2026b). Its routing objective relates to our lightweight detector, although our trigger concerns patterns across RSI attempts and initiates a persistent mechanism investigation. 

Representing prior experience. In-context Time Series Predictor packages historical forecasting tasks as lookback–future examples for contextual prediction (Lu et al., 2025). It illustrates an alterna-tive to supplying an undifferentiated history: prior experience can be organized into task-relevant units. SO-RSI uses a different unit—a mechanism hypothesis linked to its tests and outcomes—so that a later investigation can revisit what was supported or contradicted, rather than simply reuse an earlier response. 

Mechanism-specific interventions in other domains. Wu et al. (2026) address degraded underwa-ter imagery with geometry-constrained frequency enhancement and prediction-cued refinement. Their architecture is designed for a known source of interference; SO-RSI studies how an improvement agent can investigate a failure pattern before selecting a workflow intervention.

Model-internal analysis can recover interpretable activation directions using independent component analysis (Liu & Han, 2026), while inference-time interven-tion can modify initial-token attention without updating model weights (Han et al., 2026b).
