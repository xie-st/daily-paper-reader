## Abstract 

Pretrained vision-language-action models (VLAs) often fail midway through unseen long-horizon tasks. Recursive self-improvement (RSI) offers a remedy, but embodied RSI meth-ods either refine the harness around a frozen executor and so cannot add a missing motion, or train the executor on persistent failures without asking whether a failure lies in how the executor is called or in what it can do. We ask when a self-improving embodied system should change the weights of its executor, and propose AdaRSI, an on-demand RSI framework for embodied agents. A coding agent first

1arXiv:2610.20474v1 [cs.CL] 14 Oct 2026 1. Introduction 

Pretrained vision-language-action models (VLAs) [1, 11, 21] can perform a wide range of manipulation tasks in diverse environments. Recent models have also made progress on long-horizon tasks [23, 27]. Yet on unseen multi-step tasks they still often fail midway. A robot may approach the wrong target, misjudge whether a step has suc-ceeded, or fail to execute a required motion. Some of these failures lie in how the VLA, acting as the executor, is called and others in what it can do, and the failure itself does not tell which. Recursive self-improvement (RSI), in which a system turns its own experience into persistent changes that make it better [3, 4, 37], offers a way to repair such failures with-out collecting human demonstrations for every new task. An embodied system, made of a coding agent, its skills and harness, and a low-level executor such as a VLA, can improve itself at two levels. The harness, comprising the agent’s skills, its tools and the procedure that composes them, decides how the executor is called, while the execu-tor’s weights decide what it can do. Harness-level methods refine the agent’s skills and tools while keeping the executor frozen [6, 16–18, 25, 26, 30, 31, 33, 38]. Their changes are cheap, readable and easy to re-vert, but they stall when a task requires a motion the execu-tor cannot perform [29, 33]. Policy-level methods instead update the executor’s weights [5, 9, 10, 12, 15, 34], some of them directing training toward tasks with low recent suc-cess [5] or toward high-risk scenarios [9], and systems that act on both levels train on the data that a refined harness produces [7, 32]. None of these methods asks whether a persistent failure lies in how the executor is called or in a motion it lacks, although the two call for different remedies. Training on a failure that lies in how the executor is called is not only costly but also risky, as a fine-tuned policy can perform a sub-step worse than the executor it started from. This raises a question that current embodied RSI leaves open: when should a self-improving system change the weights of its executor? We argue that the answer is a mat-ter of attribution. The weights should change only for a sub-step that stays unreliable after the agent has tried other ways of calling the executor, such as other instructions, robot poses or approaches. Refining the harness first therefore does more than fix the failures that lie in how the executor is called. It also sets the states from which the executor is called, so that a policy trained afterwards learns the motion that is actually missing, from the states in which it will be used. We answer this question with AdaRSI , an on-demand recursive self-improvement framework for embod-ied agents. The agent first refines its harness from execution feedback, so that the harness absorbs the failures that better calling can fix without any training. It then attributes the

## 2. Related Work 

Vision-language-action models. VLAs map images and language instructions directly to robot actions [1, 11, 21]. Trained on large and diverse data, they follow instructions in new environments [8, 22], carry out long-horizon tasks with memory [23, 27], and can be paired with a high-level model that decomposes a task [24]. Yet they still generalize poorly to tasks outside their training distribution [20, 39]. We keep a pretrained VLA as the executor and let the em-bodied system built on it improve itself. 

Recursive self-improvement of embodied systems. Re-cursive self-improvement (RSI) refers to systems that turn their own experience into persistent changes that make them better [3, 4], as language-model agents have done by rewrit-ing their own code, harness and skills [13, 14, 36, 37]. Most embodied methods refine the agent’s skills and har-ness while the executor stays frozen [6, 16–18, 25, 26, 30, 31, 33, 38]. They change how and when the executor is called, but not what it can do [29]. Other methods improve the executor itself by training policies on self-collected data [5, 9, 10, 12, 15, 34, 35]. Some of them choose where to train, practicing tasks with low recent success [5] or col-lecting high-risk scenarios for a specialist that a gate calls in place of the nominal policy [9], and a few act on both lev-els [7, 32], for example fine-tuning the policy on data that an evolved harness collects [32]. These methods choose which failures to train on, but not whether a failure lies in how the executor is called, which a harness change can fix, or in a motion the executor lacks, which calls for new weights. We instead attribute each persistent failure to one of the two levels before training, and change the weights only for the sub-steps that remain unreliable after the harness has been refined. 

## 3. Problem Formulation 

Embodied agent system. We consider an embodied agent system S = ( A, H, Π) with three parts. The agent 

A is a language-model coding agent that interprets the task, runs the system and reads the feedback from execution. The harness H is what the agent writes and uses to act, includ-ing its skills, its tools, and the program that composes them into a procedure for the task. The executor library Π holds the low-level policies that perform manipulation. It starts as Π0 = {π0}, a single pretrained VLA, and grows when a policy fine-tuned for a specific sub-step is added. Tools may move the robot directly, for example to navigate to a fixture, but every manipulation sub-step is executed by a policy in 

Π, which the harness selects together with its instruction. 

Self-improvement on a new task. Given a new task, the system may change itself before it is deployed. A change acts on one of two levels, either the harness, H → H′, or the executor library, Π → Π ∪ { π′}, where π′ is fine-tuned from a policy already in Π. Changes are made in an im-provement phase in simulation, where the system can run the task repeatedly and privileged simulator state is acces-sible. No human demonstrations are available. At deploy-ment, the harness and the library are fixed, and the system acts from the robot’s regular observations only. Let J(S)

be the deployment success rate and C(S) = CH + CΠ the cost of improvement, where CH counts language-model to-kens and CΠ counts self-collected demonstrations and train-ing compute, including policies that were trained but not adopted. The goal is a system with high J at low C.

## 4. AdaRSI 

AdaRSI first refines the harness and then descends to the weights. In the harness rounds, the agent builds its tools and procedure, runs the system on held-out validation episodes, and revises the tools and procedure on this feedback. The 3Figure 3. weight round then lets the agent descend to the executor, but only for the sub-steps that remain unreliable after these re-visions. Each round starts from the system that the previous round produced. The harness is written by the agent for it-self: at deployment, a fresh instance of the same agent reads the procedure and calls tools and policies as it prescribes. 

4.1. Harness-Level Improvement 

The harness starts minimal. It exposes only the executor, which runs with a language instruction for a bounded num-ber of steps, the camera images and proprioception, a map of the fixtures in the scene, low-level commands for the base and the arm, and a basic tool that navigates the base to a fix-ture, as a planning and control module does on a real mobile manipulator. Everything else is written by the agent, such as tools for fine positioning in front of a fixture, resetting the arm or zooming a camera onto a fixture, among many others. Improving the harness means writing such tools and strengthening the procedure that calls them. The procedure tells the deployed agent which sub-steps to perform, which tool or policy to call for each, and how to verify progress. During development, the agent may reset the scene, save and restore simulator snapshots, and query the task’s suc-cess check. Snapshots enable counterfactual re-execution, in which a single sub-step is retried from the same state un-der a different instruction or approach, and this separates a poor instruction or a poor approach from a motion the ex-ecutor cannot make. After each round, the system is run as at deployment on held-out validation episodes, and the records of these runs are returned to the agent as feedback for the next round. The agent thus tunes its harness on one set of scenes and is judged on another, which exposes fail-ures that only appear in new scenes. 

4.2. Descending to the Weights 

Executor-limited sub-steps. Descending to the weights begins with failure attribution, which assigns each persistent failure either to the harness or to the executor. We call a sub-step executor-limited if no harness the agent can find lets π0

perform it reliably. At the end of the harness rounds, the agent records the sub-steps that still fail although the robot is in place and the instruction is one the executor knows, together with the success rate it measured for π0 from such states. These candidates, and the validation records behind them, guide the weight round. 

Self-collected demonstrations. For a sub-step it decides to train, the agent collects demonstrations in simulation it-self. It can apply rejection sampling to the base executor, rolling it out repeatedly from the failing state and keep-ing the segments that succeed, or write a scripted expert that reads privileged state such as object poses and articula-tion states. This follows learning with privileged informa-tion [2, 28], in which a privileged teacher labels data for a student that sees only the robot’s regular observations. Fine-tuning and validation. Each demonstration set fine-tunes a sub-step policy, starting from π0 or from one of the agent’s earlier policies, and the result is added to Π. The new policy is called through the same interface as the base executor. As in self-improving coding agents [37], every self-modification is validated empirically. The agent com-pares the new policy with π0 from development states and records the result in its procedure, which recommends the policy for the sub-steps where it performs better and keeps 

π0 as the default everywhere else. In the same round, the agent also works out how to call each new policy, for ex-ample how the robot is positioned before the call. Every trained policy stays in Π, so the deployed agent follows the recommendations of its procedure but can still choose an-other policy from the library, for instance after π0 fails re-peatedly. 

4.3. Recursion Across Rounds and Tasks 

Each round maps the current system and the experience gathered with it to an improved system, Sk+1 = g(Sk, Ek).The experience Ek contains the development runs, the val-idation records and, in the weight round, the training re-sults of every policy, adopted or not. Because the harness and the policies persist, later rounds build on what earlier rounds found, and a harness revision can make use of a newly trained policy as easily as of the base executor. Self-improvement also carries over across tasks. We se-lect pilot tasks to span high, moderate, and low base-VLA success rates on manipulation sub-steps, measured on de-velopment scenes during the harness rounds. This provides varied starting points for studying when harness refinement suffices and when weight adaptation is useful. The pilot tasks are improved without any limit on training. From their training logs, the agent writes an experience document that records which kinds of sub-steps benefited from training, how much data sufficed, and where effort was wasted. The same logs set a training budget by a fixed rule, scaled from the training that the adopted policies of the pilot tasks actu-ally used. Every later task is improved with this document and under this budget, which the environment enforces on training steps and self-collected demonstrations. The bud-get turns the choice of where to train into an explicit trade-off, and the experience tells the agent which sub-steps are likely to be executor-limited. In this sense the system im-proves not only itself but also the way it improves. At deployment, a fresh instance of the agent runs the fi-nal system on unseen scenes. It follows the procedure, calls tools and policies, and checks progress from images, with-out snapshots, success checks or privileged state.

5.2. Main Results 

Overall. AdaRSI achieves the highest suite-level success rate on all three suites, reaching 48.8% over the 50 tasks compared with 42.4% for the VLA alone and 46.7% for 

Agent + harness (Tab. 1). The largest gain is on Composite-Unseen, where success rises from 4.6% to 13.8%, about three times the base executor’s rate. The gains also extend to tasks present in the executor’s training data, with Atomic-Seen rising from 75.4% to 79.7% and Composite-Seen from 43.0% to 49.0%. The benefit is thus not confined to acquir-ing motions for unseen tasks, and improving how existing capabilities are used also raises task success. 

Harness level. Letting the agent call the VLA without any development does not reliably raise success. Agent + VLA 

falls from 75.4% to 72.2% on Atomic-Seen, and its overall success remains below that of the VLA alone. Harness de-velopment reverses this drop and improves all three suites, reaching 78.3% on Atomic-Seen and more than doubling success on Composite-Unseen to 9.9%. In CuttingToolSe-lection, for example, the agent writes a rule that maps each food to the right tool instead of leaving “the appropriate cut-ting tool” to the VLA, and adds checks that stop a failing attempt early, raising success from 30% to 50%. The bene-fit therefore comes from improving the tools and procedure through execution feedback, rather than simply allowing an agent to issue sub-instructions. 

Weight level. The weight round adds the most on Composite-Unseen, a further 3.9 percentage points against 1.4 on Atomic-Seen and 1.0 on Composite-Seen. Table 2 breaks this gain down by task. WaffleReheat shows the largest effect, rising from 0% with the harness alone to 38% with AdaRSI. Its harness reaches the microwave, but π0

cannot open the door and release the handle, and a policy fine-tuned for this sub-step resolves the bottleneck while π0

still performs every other step. RecycleBottlesByType im-proves from 0% to 18% and BreadSelection from 42% to 52%, so the weight round helps both tasks that rarely suc-ceed and tasks where the harness already does well. Not every task benefits, however. CuttingToolSelection falls from 50% to 36% after the weight round, and several tasks remain unsolved by every system although the agent fine-tunes policies for their sub-steps. 5.3. Ablations 

We ablate AdaRSI on the Composite-Unseen suite with five variants. (1) Agent + weights improves the system only by fine-tuning, without harness rounds, tools or a training bud-get. (2) Agent + harness improves only the harness and stops before the weight round. (3) Always train fine-tunes a policy for every manipulation sub-step and calls the fine-tuned policies by default. (4) Fixed procedure runs the pro-cedure and policies that AdaRSI builds as a fixed program, without the agent at deployment. (5) AdaRSI w/ π0 keeps the final system of AdaRSI but runs π0 wherever a fine-tuned policy would be called. 

Success against cost. Figure 4 places every system by its training cost and its success. The arrows trace the or-der in which AdaRSI improves the system. Harness rounds raise success from 5.9% to 9.9% without any training, and the weight round then adds 3.9 points for 966 GPU min-utes. Every other system that trains lands in the region that AdaRSI dominates, with at least the same training and lower success. Agent + weights trains more and reaches 7.2%, Always train spends 3.5 × the GPU time for 12.6%, and Fixed procedure uses exactly the same policies for 6.9%. The systems that train nothing are cheaper, but none of them exceeds 9.9%, so no system we compare is both cheaper and more successful than AdaRSI. 

Neither level suffices alone. Both levels help on their own, but neither reaches their combination. Fine-tuning without a harness lifts the VLA from 4.6% to 7.2%, and the harness alone reaches 9.9%, while AdaRSI reaches 13.8% on the same episodes. The harness is the larger lever, since most failures come from the procedure rather than from missing motions. It also does more than raise success, be-cause it tells the agent where training is needed. Choosing what to train. A simpler rule could decide where to train without attributing failures, by training the sub-steps on which π0 succeeds least. Lowest first applies this rule on the Composite-Unseen tasks that are not pi-lots. It starts from the same harness as AdaRSI, with the same budget, demonstration collection and adoption rule, measures π0 on every manipulation sub-step from the states that the procedure reaches, and trains as many sub-steps per task as AdaRSI does, lowest success first, without retrying any of them from other states or with other instructions. It gains nothing over the harness alone, reaching 11.8% against 12.0%, while AdaRSI reaches 16.6% at a similar training cost (Tab. 8). Where it trains, the rule mostly se-lects the same sub-steps as AdaRSI, 11 of the 13 it trains, yet it does not turn them into gains. In WaffleReheat, both systems train a policy for the microwave door, but only AdaRSI, which retries the failing sub-step before training, finds that its failure depends on how the hand approaches the handle and trains from an approach that works, so the task succeeds in 38% of episodes against 2%. Measuring every sub-step also takes time, and in four tasks it used up the development session before any policy was trained. At-tribution thus tells the agent not only which sub-steps to train but also from which states to learn them.

5.4. Cost 

On the Composite-Unseen suite, AdaRSI trains 2.4 policies per task on average, adopted or not, using 26 self-collected demonstrations, 1.9k updates and about one GPU-hour. Its improvement phase, including the harness rounds, valida-tion and the weight round, consumes 154M input tokens and 0.64M output tokens per task, and at deployment the agent uses 4.2M input and 7.2k output tokens per episode, with about 97% of the input tokens being cache hits. The improvement phase costs 117M and 137M input tokens per task on Atomic-Seen and Composite-Seen, where deploy-ment needs 1.2M and 1.9M per episode, since the proce-dures are shorter. Fine-tuning every sub-step raises the de-ployment cost to 5.3M input tokens per episode, because the agent calls and checks more policies, while fine-tuning without a harness needs fewer tokens per episode, 1.5M, but fails more often. No human demonstration is used at any stage. 

5.5. Case Studies 

During training. To see how the agent decides where to train, we analyze the weight rounds of the pilot tasks, which run without a training budget and whose records produce the experience document. Table 9 sorts representative poli-cies by whether π0 could already perform the sub-step and whether fine-tuning improved it on held-out starts. When a sub-step is beyond π0, a few demonstrations can teach it, as twenty demonstrations do for the left door of a French-door refrigerator in GarnishPancake. The task still fails there, because later sub-steps remain unsolved, so learning one motion does not by itself make a long task succeed. Other missing motions are learned only partly, such as picking up the skewer, or not at all, such as closing the toaster-oven door. When π0 already performs a sub-step, training can still raise its reliability, as for the mug in CoffeeSetupMug, but it often leaves the sub-step unchanged or yields a gain in development that does not hold at test, as for the drawer in OpenDrawer. ## 6. Discussion and Limitations 

Deciding when to train. Our results support a simple principle for embodied self-improvement, to change the harness first and to change the weights only for the sub-steps that stay unreliable once the harness has been refined. Our instantiation of this principle leaves two decisions to the agent, and it can fail at either. The agent may find the sub-steps that need training but not obtain a policy that performs them reliably. This happens in the Composite-Unseen tasks that remain unsolved, which chain several sub-steps beyond the executor and succeed only if every fine-tuned policy in the chain succeeds in the same episode. The agent may also adopt a change that looks better on a few development scenes but hurts on the test scenes. In Cutting-ToolSelection, the weight round starts every episode that needs the peeler with a dedicated grasp step, which looked slightly better in development but halves success in these episodes at test, whether the step runs the fine-tuned policy or π0. Both depend on the capability of the agent and on how training is constrained, including the budget, the num-ber of validation scenes and the way demonstrations are col-lected. A more reliable test of necessity and a stronger train-ing loop are natural next steps, and so is restricting the de-ployed agent to the policies that its procedure selects, which our agent may override after repeated failures. 

Limitations. All experiments run in simulation, where privileged state makes it easy to generate and label demon-strations. On a real robot, the same role would have to be played by success detectors, scripted resets or occasional human help, and the cost of a weight change would rise accordingly, which only strengthens the case for training on demand. We are conducting real-robot experiments and will include them in the next version of this paper. 11 7. Conclusion 

We presented AdaRSI, an on-demand recursive self-improvement framework for embodied agents. It lets a cod-ing agent improve its skills and harness first and fine-tune the executor only on the sub-steps that remain unreliable, using demonstrations it collects itself. Across all 50 tar-get tasks of RoboCasa365 and without any human demon-stration, this improves a pretrained VLA on all three suites, most of all on unseen composite tasks. The trained weights pay off where the executor lacks a motion, and there they decide whether a task succeeds, whereas fine-tuning a sub-step that the executor already completes often adds cost without adding success and can even make it worse. Decid-ing where to train thus lets embodied self-improvement gain from new weights at a fraction of the cost of training every-where.
