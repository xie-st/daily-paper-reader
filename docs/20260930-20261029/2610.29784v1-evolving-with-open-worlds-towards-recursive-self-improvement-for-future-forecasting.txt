# Abstract 

Most contemporary artificial intelligence (AI) systems are released with frozen parameters and improve through external updates, whereas an ideal system would keep improving from its own experience, a capacity we call recursive self-improvement (RSI). Yet studying RSI requires an environment that is continuously changing, objectively verifiable, and naturally grounded, posing a fundamental challenge for agent evolution. Here we introduce D E E P EVO LV E , a sustainable evolution agent system grounded in future event prediction, in which agents continuously interact with a dynamically evolving environment and improve by internalizing and externalizing the experience gained through these interactions. A living environment synthesizes verifiable tasks, and a recursive-evolution engine turns the reasoning trajectory behind each prediction back into capability: decoupling search from prediction adapts parameter-level evolution to the contamination-prone live environment, while distilling reusable skills lets the agent evolve from the limited live trajectories such an environment affords. On the external FutureX leaderboard, which evaluates predictions against real-world outcomes and is updated weekly, our self-improved forecaster ranked first across all systems over three consecutive weeks, achieving up to a 195% improvement in rating. On our synthesized evaluation, it ranked among the top three across four base LLMs, outperforming the strongest baseline by up to 10.7 Bradley–Terry points. More broadly, these results suggest that a system built for self-improvement is best understood as an agent that co-evolves with a genuinely living, open-ended environment, pointing to a shift in AI from training static models on fixed data toward cultivating agents that grow stronger from their own experience, with such an open world as the indispensable basis of that growth. Our code and data are available at https://github.com/RUCAIBox/DeepEvolve .

# 1. Introduction 

Intelligence is often measured by what a system can do at a single moment in time [2, 19]. Yet the more consequential question may not be how capable a model is today, but whether it can become more capable through its own experience [9, 14, 23]. Across the natural and social sciences, the systems we regard as most powerful ( e.g., biological evolution, the scientific method, human expertise) are precisely those that improve themselves cumulatively, correcting past errors and compounding past gains [8, 21, 25]. This suggests that an ideal artificial system should be measured less by what it can do at any single moment than by how its capabilities form and grow over time. A system that improves itself from experience is a prerequisite for AI that acts as an autonomous agent and as a genuine instrument of discovery. In this 

1arXiv:2610.29784v1 [cs.CL] 21 Oct 2026 work, we study this form of recursive self-improvement (RSI) in the concrete and demanding setting of real-world event forecasting. Studying recursive self-improvement, however, is not merely a matter of method. It places unusually high demands on the research setting itself. An agent can only improve recursively if its environment keeps posing fresh challenges and can tell, without human intervention, whether the agent is doing better than before. What this calls for is an interactive environment, yet the fixed datasets that dominate today’s evaluation offer no such thing: inert and unchanging, they neither renew the challenge nor judge progress on their own. Real-world event forecasting, we argue, is a natural testbed for such study: it is grounded 

in the unfolding world rather than a hand-constructed dataset, verifiable through future events whose objective ground truth arrives with time, and non-stationary because the world keeps changing. Together these properties close the loop that recursion requires: the agent observes the world, makes predictions, receives future outcomes, learns from the resulting experience, and improves the predictions it makes next (Figure 1a). Whereas prior work on event and time-series forecasting [11, 30] has largely pursued single-shot predictive accuracy, we instead treat forecasting as an environment in which to study how agents improve recursively through continuous interaction with a changing world. Succeeding in this environment first requires a few foundational capabilities for solving any single forecasting task. An agent must be able to gather task-relevant evidence from a vast information space, as exemplified by search-augmented systems [26, 27], and to reason , combining and weighing that evidence to draw multi-step conclusions [1, 5]. Both capabilities are already standard in existing systems, and we treat them as the foundation on which our self-improvement mechanisms build. Recursive self-improvement goes further, requiring a capability different in kind: an agent that not only solves the task in front of it but also changes its own behavior in light of past successes and failures. Such an agent addresses a distinct question: how its capacity to solve tasks can itself keep improving. We take this ability to evolve as the central concern of this work, and reserve the name D E E P EVO LV E for the framework we propose to realize it (Section 4). Realizing such an evolving agent runs against a basic limitation of today’s AI: it is largely static and dependent on outside intervention to advance, a set of parameters frozen at release rather than a system that keeps improving on its own [4, 25]. This shows up on two fronts. Evaluation is static, scoring a model on a fixed dataset [6, 16] that can be memorized or contaminated; and improvement is passive, with capability gains driven from outside by human-curated data and offline retraining [7, 12] rather than by the model’s own experience. This condition also differs from neighboring lines of work: self-training on self-generated data [13, 28] lacks an external, verifiable ground truth and can reinforce a model’s own biases; continual learning [15, 20] operates over static or artificially partitioned task streams rather than real, non-stationary feedback; and reinforcement learning for reasoning [22, 23] optimizes reward on a task but does not, by itself, target improvement that compounds across time. By recursive , we mean exactly this compounding: the parameters, skills, and environment produced by one round of improvement become the input to the next, forming a self-improvement loop rather than a one-off gain [8, 21]. Our central argument departs from most work on self-improvement, which pushes on a single lever, be it the model’s weights [13], its prompts [10], or its memory [24]. What makes self-improvement sustainable ,we argue, is a system that keeps accumulating experience on its own rather than being fed hand-curated data from outside, and our answer is a living environment in which the agent continually acts, observes outcomes, and learns. The logic is directional. The environment is the source, posing progressively harder tasks that supply the experience without which no further improvement is possible; the agent’s parameters 

and skills are the two forms its evolution takes, both shaped from one substrate, the forecasting trajectories 

that are reinforced into parameters, distilled into skills, and mined for the weaknesses that build a harder environment. More importantly, the gains compound rather than saturate: as the agent’s own live trajectories accumulate into a growing pool, the skills distilled from them lift forecasting quality round by round rather than jumping once and plateauing (Figure 6). Our experiments trace this improvement to how environment, parameters, and skills evolve together, each by its own means. The environment closes the loop: when it co-evolves with the agent, synthesizing verifiable tasks aimed at the agent’s demonstrated weaknesses, these otherwise isolated and bounded gains add up to a larger improvement (Figure 9). This is evidence that recursive self-improvement arises from the three levers evolving as one. Taken together, these findings establish recursive self-improvement as the co-evolution of an agent and its living environment. The environment is the abstraction we commit to. Our first contribution is to construct a real, dynamic, and verifiable environment for forecasting: grounded in the unfolding world, non-stationary as that world changes, and scored against outcomes that resolve on their own. This environment lets all three levers improve in concert. Its verifiable outcomes become rewards that sharpen parameters ;its live experience supplies the trajectories that distil into skills ; and its dynamic form lets it be rewritten toward the agent’s exposed weaknesses, so each round feeds the next. Our second contribution turns this opening into method: D E E P EVO LV E formalizes the environment, the agent, and the recursive-evolution engine as three modules in a closed loop (Section 4). More broadly, the development of AI may shift from training static models on fixed datasets toward cultivating evolving agents within living environments, and with it the measure of progress: the rate, ceiling, and durability of a system’s improvement, which only an evolving environment can reveal. Forecasting is the concrete setting we build and study, and the way we construct this environment, and the methodology that evolves an agent inside it, carry over to any setting that is grounded, verifiable, and non-stationary, so the same loop can be extended to far more general scenarios. It also recasts how recursive self-improvement might be pursued, suggesting that progress may come less from any single, ever-larger model than from co-evolving parameters, skills, and the environment that supplies their experience. Such progress is not without cost: it remains bounded by the quality and quantity of available experience, long-horizon evaluation is expensive, and stability under non-stationary drift is not guaranteed. Yet even bounded, it reframes the question with which we began. 42. Results 

2.1. Experimental Design and DeepEvolve Framework 

To forecast future events and to test whether an agent can improve itself from doing so, we develop D E E P -EVO LV E , a framework that turns experience into capability through a closed loop of three components. PRO M E T H E U S , the living data environment, gathers daily news and synthesizes it into forecasting tasks whose ground truth is revealed only once the events resolve. B R A N C H U S , the agentic forecaster, gathers evidence with external tools and estimates the probability of each event, producing a full prediction trajec-tory. A T H E NA , the recursive-evolution engine, consumes these trajectories and drives improvement along three axes. It applies reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) to update the parameters of BR A N C H U S , distills trajectories into reusable forecasting skills, and evolves adversarial task-synthesis skills in P RO M E T H E U S to make the environment harder. Each forecasting trajectory is rich enough to feed all three axes at once: because a future-event prediction forces the agent to gather evidence and weigh it under uncertainty, the same trajectory can be reinforced against its resolved outcome, mined for reusable skills, and analysed for the weaknesses from which a harder environment is built. Experience is thus fed back to improve the agent’s parameters, its skills, and its environment jointly, realizing the recursive self-improvement loop at the heart of this work. The subsections that follow examine these three axes in turn, after first establishing the strength of the system as a whole. We draw forecasting tasks from two complementary sources. The first is FutureX [29], an external benchmark refreshed every week that asks models to predict events in the coming week. Its tasks span four difficulty levels (L1 to L4), take multiple-choice, ranking, and numerical forms, and cover domains from finance and politics to sports, natural disasters, and technology, so strong performance reflects general forecasting ability. The second is a higher-throughput evaluation synthesized by P RO M E T H E U S ,used purely as a task generator with its adversarial evolution disabled so the evaluation stays unbiased. Both sources are genuinely live rather than static or cached. Each day, P RO M E T H E U S ingests fresh news from 125 websites and writes forecasting questions about events that resolve the following day, so BR A N C H U S must predict outcomes whose ground truth does not yet exist. This one-day horizon shortens the verification cycle and lets us evaluate models far more frequently than a weekly benchmark allows. Every question concerns a future event, so predictions are verified against realized outcomes rather than a fixed answer key. For the P RO M E T H E U S evaluation, once an event resolves we task Codex [17] with retrieving the realized outcome from the open web and scoring the prediction against it. Beyond raw accuracy, we report a calibration metric: the model outputs a probability for each event, and we check whether the empirical frequency of those events matches the predicted probabilities. We also stabilize these per-question scores before aggregating. On FutureX we adopt its official sliding-window Bradley–Terry (BT) model [3], which infers a consistent latent strength from head-to-head weekly results. On P RO M E T H E U S , a single day of questions is too small and too variable to rank harnesses reliably, so unless stated otherwise every reported number averages over several consecutive days of live evaluation.

2.3. Search-prediction decoupling adapts RFT and RLVR to contamination-prone live environments 

The first of the three axes is the model’s parameters. The obstacle to internalizing forecasting experience is that the environment is live and easily contaminated: once an event resolves, its answer becomes retrievable from the web, so the task can no longer train a model to predict rather than look up. The clean window is short, yet RFT and RLVR must roll out and refine over the same tasks repeatedly, which leaves too little uncontaminated, verifiable experience to support training at all. The function-oriented 

design of B R A N C H U S removes this obstacle by splitting the incompatible needs across teams organized by function: a time-sensitive search team gathers evidence while a question is still open, and a time-insensitive predict team reasons over it into a forecast. We cache the evidence and both teams’ trajectories while the task is still fresh, so the many RFT/RLVR rounds that follow read from this frozen pool rather than from a web that has since revealed the answer. Because the predict team is trained only on fixed, pre-cached evidence, it cannot influence what it sees, which also closes the on-policy shortcut in which RLVR would manufacture answer-entailing evidence; any gain reflects better reasoning over the same information. Once enough cached trajectories resolve into verifiable labels, we train the predict team with two complementary paradigms: RLVR optimizes against each resolved outcome, and RFT fine-tunes on successful trajectories. Under this function-oriented protocol, RLVR yields consistent gains across both LLMs (Figure 5): choice accuracy rises by 1.3 points on each model, while the numeric score ( 1 − bWIS ) improves by 0.31 and 

0.38 . These clean, consistent gains from a reward computed against resolved outcomes are themselves evidence that the design has kept contamination out of the training signal, since a leaked answer would have collapsed the reward long before it could sharpen the model. The two kinds of metric respond at different rates, and we attribute this to the metrics themselves. Accuracy is binary and insensitive to how belief mass shifts, whereas calibration is continuous and reflects any move toward the true outcome probabilities. Calibration thus surfaces improvements too subtle for accuracy to register, acting as an early indicator that RLVR is tightening the model’s probabilistic judgment even when the top-1 answer is unchanged. RFT, under the same protocol, also improves every axis across two LLMs, again led by calibration (Figure 4): accuracy rises by 0.7 to 2.9 points, while Brier and relative WIS improve consistently. Evolving the environment alongside the model amplifies this effect, since tasks that keep targeting the agent’s current weaknesses turn this incoming information into richer experience for the next round and drive still larger gains, which we discuss in Sec. 2.7. 

2.4. Evolving skills unlock recursive self-improvement from fleeting live trajectories 

The live trajectories that the function-oriented design cached for RLVR can instead be read for the lessons they contain, internalizing the same scarce experience in a lighter, more transparent form: reusable forecasting skills that sit outside the model weights and are invoked at inference time. A single live trajectory thus feeds more than one lever, as RLVR turns it into a parameter update while skill distillation turns the same record into an explicit, inspectable procedure learned without any gradient update. The stakes here are sharper than for parameters, because fresh trajectories are collected only within each task’s short open window, so the axis is useful only if a mere handful of them can already lift the system. We let A T H E NA distill the lessons from B R A N C H U S ’s trajectories into skill entries and build libraries over three evolution rounds, from nested pools of 88 , 186 and 262 live trajectories, each round extending the previous one as it accumulates more trajectories, giving B R A N C H U S the current library at inference so that any change in performance is attributable to the skills alone. A handful of live trajectories already unlocks clear improvements on both axes of forecasting quality (Figure 6). 2.7. A targeted and challenging environment drives sustained system evolution 

The harder questions are valuable not in themselves but because the richer experience they generate flows back into both of B R A N C H U S ’s evolution axes, so a weakness-targeting environment improves the system more than a fixed one does. For the skill axis, the evolved questions probe parts of the task space the agent has not mastered, and the experience distilled from them covers substantially more ground. From the same pool of 262 trajectories, coupling task synthesis to the agent’s weaknesses raises the number of distilled skills from 1312 to 1403 and broadens coverage from 320 to 337 domains and from 331 to 

357 concrete tasks (Figure 9b). The gain outlasts the current round, since a skill library spanning more domains is more likely to match the questions the agent meets next, making experience accumulated under an evolving environment more broadly reusable than experience accumulated under a static one. The parameter axis benefits in the same direction. Relative to plain RFT, RFT on the adversarially synthesized hard problems improves every forecasting metric we track, lowering the Brier score by 1.6% ,raising AUROC by 2.7% , and tightening numeric forecasts by 1.0% in relative WIS and 4.0% in Md-sAPE (Figure 10). Because both axes draw on the same enriched experience, the evolved environment lifts the system through the skills it distils and the parameters it updates alike. The agent and its environment thus enter a productive cycle: each round of environment evolution exposes a weakness, and each round of agent evolution repairs it, so capability compounds rather than saturates. This is our clearest evidence that an evolving environment is an essential part of recursive self-improvement rather than an optional add-on, since a fixed distribution of tasks eventually exhausts what it can teach whereas one that keeps distilling challenges from the agent’s own mistakes holds the loop open. # 3. Discussion 

This study presents D E E P EVO LV E , a recursive self-improvement framework that treats real-world event forecasting as a living environment the agent learns inside, rather than a static task it is scored against. Across a diverse suite of agent baselines spanning model families and domains, D E E P EVO LV E attains the strongest forecasting performance, matching or surpassing every alternative with clear gains in calibration (Section 2.2). Beyond this static advantage, the system keeps improving from its own experience. Skill evolution shows this most clearly: as each round adds its live trajectories to the pool, the skills distilled from the growing pool lift forecasting quality step by step rather than in a single jump. These results show that an evolving agent inside an evolving environment can sustain improvement where prior approaches saturate, reframing progress from training a static model on a fixed dataset to cultivating a system whose capability accrues from its own experience. The central contribution of D E E P EVO LV E is a real, dynamic, and verifiable environment that turns forecasting into a usable training signal. This environment couples three modules: a living data source (P RO M E T H E U S ), an agentic forecaster (B R A N C H U S ), and a recursive-evolution engine (A T H E NA ). Such an environment is difficult to train on directly, because forecasting data expires once an event resolves, and a question answerable only before resolution loses its training signal afterward. D E E P EVO LV E 

addresses this by separating evidence gathering from prediction: a time-sensitive search team caches the evidence available at forecast time, and a time-invariant predict team learns over that frozen context, so resolved outcomes supply verifiable rewards while the agent sees only the information available to it at forecast time. Within this environment the agent retains experience through two complementary routes, compressing it into parameters by reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards and externalizing it into an explicit, reusable skill library distilled from forecasting trajectories. Combining the two yields the fast, inspectable gains of skill accumulation together with the deeper gains that only parameter updates reach, a combination that surpasses any single-lever training. These two routes, one internal and one external, strengthen different capabilities of the agents. Parameter evolution writes experience into the weights: reinforcement learning trains the predict team on cached evidence, so the model draws fuller and better-calibrated conclusions from the information in hand. Skill 15 evolution writes experience into an explicit, reusable library that guides the search team, so the agent gathers evidence more accurately and comprehensively in the first place. The two are complementary rather than redundant: one sharpens reasoning over fixed evidence, the other improves the evidence that reasoning receives. Learning along both routes at once is itself a key technical enabler of recursive self-improvement, because they differ not only in what they strengthen but in what they demand of the experience that drives them (Section 2). Internal learning needs a large volume of trajectories to evolve stably, since reinforcement learning must roll out and refine over the same tasks repeatedly, which makes it hard to apply in a live, contamination-prone environment whose clean, verifiable window is short. External learning is far more stable, as a mere handful of live trajectories already lifts the system within each task’s narrow open window, exactly the regime in which parameter updates run short of data. Running both in tandem thus lets the system keep improving from scarce fresh experience, so the strongest configurations pair a parameter-trained predict team with a mature skill library, and the effective capability of the system is defined jointly by its weights and its accumulated skills. Both weights and skills draw on one source, the experience the environment supplies, which makes the environment the governing term of the system, the one on which the other two depend. A fixed environment fails this role in two ways. First, it saturates: both routes exhaust the same task distribution, as the predict team resamples evidence it has already mastered and the skill library finds only patterns it has already distilled, so added compute relearns the same regularity and further agent-side training struggles to drive the curve higher. Second, it is prone to contamination: once the outcomes of past events become publicly retrievable, the model can exploit a reasoning shortcut, recalling a resolved answer instead of reasoning toward a forecast. To sustain improvement, the environment itself must therefore evolve, and evolve purposefully: it must stay fresh rather than reuse resolved events, and it must grow harder in a targeted way, posing new tasks at the current edge of the agent’s competence. The dynamic environment we construct meets both demands by drawing new events and scoring them against outcomes that resolve only in the future, which provides a signal that cannot have been seen in advance, to force the agent to genuinely forecast rather than recall and to keep the experience that drives recursive self-improvement renewing round after round. This evolution is driven by the cooperation between A T H E NA and P RO M E T H E U S . A T H E NA distills the failures it finds in the agent’s trajectories into task-synthesis skills, and P RO M E T H E U S uses them to generate the next round of tasks, so the environment acts as a teacher whose difficulty tracks the agent’s current weaknesses: as the agent improves and its weaknesses shift, the tasks shift with them, and both sides keep changing. Two boundaries constrain this teacher. First, it is only as discerning as its diagnoses: a wrong prediction is an explicit failure that can be turned into a harder task, whereas a silent weakness such as overconfidence stays hidden from this signal, so the environment improves the agent most where its errors are already visible. Second, its pace is set by the world, because a round resolves only after the events it concerns have occurred, so questions that take months to settle make each round of improvement slow and costly. Several limitations of the present system point to future directions, each bearing on how far recursive self-improvement can run. The first concerns the experience the environment supplies, its fuel: where resolvable, informative events are rare or strongly culture-dependent, trajectories stay thin, so broadening the sources of experience in both quantity and quality is a natural way to raise the ceiling of the recursive loop. The second concerns the skill library as the world drifts, since a skill distilled under one regime may apply differently once the underlying regularity shifts; forgetting, time-weighting, and failure detection would keep the externalized experience reliable, so that improvement accumulates genuine capability. The third concerns the evolution operators themselves: richer forms of parameter evolution that let updates compound round by round as skill accumulation already does, and more expressive forms of skill evolution in how experience is distilled, organized, and retrieved, both offer room to turn the same trajectories into larger gains. Pursuing these directions together is a natural way to strengthen the recursive loop. 16 The lasting product of this work is a methodology, a technical route for exploring recursive self-improvement, portable to any domain that is grounded, verifiable, and non-stationary. Carrying it elsewhere asks only for the three terms forecasting supplies here: a living source of tasks whose outcomes resolve over time, an agent that records its trajectories, and an evolution engine that turns those trajectories into updated weights, reusable skills, and harder tasks. Scientific discovery, software engineering, and strategic planning each meet this description. In every such domain the environment commands the process, as an agent climbs only as far as its environment raises the bar, so building environments that keep evolving is the central problem recursive self-improvement must solve. In place of ever larger static models on ever larger static datasets, the alternative is to cultivate evolving agents within evolving environments, setting the ceiling on progress by the openness of the environment rather than the size of a snapshot. D E E P EVO LV E is an early demonstration that this path can be walked, moving the measure of intelligence from what a system can do at a single moment to whether it can grow stronger through its own experience, observably and reproducibly in a real and verifiable world. 

# 4. Approach 

4.1. Overview 

DE E P EVO LV E is a closed loop in which an agent and its environment improve one another over time, realized by three modules (Figure 1), rather than a feed-forward pipeline from input to prediction. PRO M E T H E U S (Section 4.2), the living data environment, turns real-world news into forecasting tasks whose ground truth is revealed only once the events resolve, supplying both the tasks and an objective, time-delivered reward. B R A N C H U S (Section 4.3), the agentic forecaster, gathers evidence with external tools and reasons over it to issue a probabilistic prediction, producing both an answer and a full trajectory .AT H E NA (Section 4.4), the recursive-evolution engine, converts these trajectories back into capability: it updates B R A N C H U S ’s parameters, distills its reasoning into reusable skills, and analyzes its failures into task-synthesis skills that make P RO M E T H E U S pose harder, weakness-targeted questions. The three thus form a single evolving system, in which a stronger B R A N C H U S meets a steadily more demanding PRO M E T H E U S , while the world remains the external ground of every reward.

4.3. Branchus: Agentic Forecasting System 

BR A N C H U S is the agentic forecaster that answers the questions posed by P RO M E T H E U S : given a question, it gathers evidence from the outside world, reasons over it, and issues a probabilistic prediction, recording the full trajectory that leads to each answer. Two mechanisms underpin its design. A multi-agent team organization separates collecting evidence from turning evidence into a prediction, so each part can be scaled and improved independently. A skill management mechanism equips the agents with an evolving repository of forecasting knowledge, from hand-authored seeds to knowledge distilled from past experience. Together these make B R A N C H U S both a strong forecaster and a producer of the structured trajectories that A T H E NA later learns from.

4.4. Athena: Recursive Evolution System 

AT H E NA is the recursive-evolution engine of D E E P EVO LV E : it extracts experience from the trajectory pool T (𝑡 ) that B R A N C H U S produces and turns it into improvement through the three operators of Equation (4). At the parameter level, Φrl applies reinforcement learning to B R A N C H U S ’s predictions to update its weights 𝜃 . At the behaviour level, Φskill distils reusable skills from its trajectories to strengthen the agent while leaving those weights untouched. At the environment level, Φenv synthesizes adversarial skills from its failures to make P RO M E T H E U S generate harder, weakness-targeted tasks. The first two operators evolve the agent and the third evolves the environment, so that both improve from the same stream of experience.

4.5. Models and Implementation 

Our evaluation pairs established agent harnesses with shared base LLMs as strong external baselines, and adds in-framework comparisons that isolate the core designs of D E E P EVO LV E . To make these comparisons fair, we enforce implementation parity. Every harness runs on the same P RO M E T H E U S 

questions, receives the same external tools under identical tool descriptions and a unified tool-calling format, and uses its own official prompt. The only factors that vary across conditions are the harness design and the base LLM. We avoid cross-paradigm comparisons that would break this parity, such as static-corpus question answering or dialogue, where evidence is already available rather than gathered under uncertainty from an unfolding world. This keeps the gains reported in Section 2.2 attributable to the design of D E E P EVO LV E rather than to mismatched task definitions. We now give the three concrete settings behind this design. 

Base LLMs and Agent Harnesses. We evaluate five representative frontier LLMs that span differ-ent model families and capabilities: GPT-5.5, GPT-5.6-Sol, Claude-Opus-4.6, DeepSeek-V4-Pro, and Qwen3.8-Max. This range lets us test whether forecasting performance and evaluation robustness hold across model families rather than for a single base model. We pair each base LLM with six agent harnesses: ReAct, MAS, OpenClaw, DeerFlow, MiroFlow, and B R A N C H U S , our purpose-built harness for real-world event forecasting. These harnesses cover a broad range of designs: a lightweight reasoning-and-tool-use loop (R E AC T [27]), a multi-agent system (MAS), and three deep-research pipelines (O P E N CL AW [18], DE E R FL OW , and M I RO FL OW ), spanning general-purpose reasoning frameworks and search-oriented systems. This model ×harness grid separates the contribution of the base LLM from that of the harness, and shows how harness design affects open-world forecasting across base models. 

Prompt Specification. For each harness, we use its official prompt as released by the original authors, rather than writing our own. This way, every framework runs in the configuration it was designed and tuned for, so performance differences reflect the harness design rather than our prompt engineering. The complete prompt templates for the three modules of D E E P EVO LV E are provided in Appendix D. 

External Tool Settings. We give the models a web search tool that issues queries and retrieves content, which grounds their forecasts in up-to-date evidence. For P RO M E T H E U S , we adopt the C O D E X [17] harness and use its built-in tools to crawl news from relevant web pages, which supplies the raw material for building questions. We also design three local tools that expose the answer, the reasoning trajectory, 22 and the collected evidence of the search team to the predict team of B R A N C H U S . With these tools, the predict team reuses the search team’s outputs without issuing new external searches, so it reasons over exactly the evidence already collected.

The larger gain is robustness: the worst unguided runs on this question made exactly the errors the skill’s critic checklist blocks (reading early silence as delay, or assuming every outcome is equally likely to be announced in time).
