Abstract 

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) in scientific research aims to improve agents’ research ca-pabilities through their own experimental ex-perience. Scientific agent swarms can pursue this goal when one agent’s findings and imple-mentations advance another’s research. Two obstacles stand in the way. A failed experiment does not reveal whether its hypothesis or its implementation was at fault, and a program without overall gains can hide components that other agents need. We introduce S WARM RSI, a framework in which a swarm improves its re-search by evolving the research states its agents inherit, including hypotheses, programs and ex-perimental evidence. Feedback-Guided Swarm Mutation uses other agents’ feedback to decide whether to revise a hypothesis or its program. Complementary Swarm Recombination tests retained hypotheses on other agents’ programs and combines complementary components. Re-cursive Swarm Evolution returns every out-come to a shared graph that guides which state each agent updates next. S WARM RSI achieves state-of-the-art (SOTA) results on OpenProb-lems and NatureBench. With Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview, it reaches an average score of 76.2 on OpenProblems, and it matches the published SOTA on 44.4% of NatureBench tasks, the highest Match-SOTA rate among compared methods. On MLE-Bench Lite, it wins medals on 81.8% of tasks, above hypothesis and pro-gram evolution agents with the same backbone. With DeepSeek-V4-Pro in one seed over all 90 NatureBench tasks, about 77% of final best programs descend from a rebase, a crossover or an update triggered by feedback. Biologists reproduce two of its programs and use them in follow-up analyses of intestinal and splenic immunity.

Sci-entific agent swarms offer a way to pursue this goal by coordinating idea generation, implementation, and evaluation across specialized agents (Yang et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2025; Yamada et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2026). Persistent memories and shared histories retain experimental experience for later re-search (Lyu et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026; Du et al., 2026; Weng et al., 2026), and accumulated histo-ries can also support improvements to exploration strategies (Zheng et al., 2026). Together, these ca-pabilities create an opportunity for agents exploring different directions to use one another’s findings and implementations in subsequent research. However, sharing experience does not ensure that agents use it to make better research deci-sions. Program evolution refines executable can-didates through execution feedback (Jiang et al., 2025; Nam et al., 2025; Novikov et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025), while hypothesis evolution im-1arXiv:2610.20431v2 [cs.AI] 19 Oct 2026 proves research directions through iterative gener-ation and evaluation (Gottweis et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a; Liu et al., 2026; Jin et al., 2026). As Figure 1 illustrates, promising hypotheses are abandoned after inadequate implementations, and useful components are overlooked in programs that perform poorly overall. We hypothesize that these limitations arise from two interrelated dependencies in scientific research. The value of a hypothesis depends on how it is implemented and how rigorously it is tested. An unsuccessful experiment therefore leaves unclear whether the research direction or its implementa-tion needs revision (Ma et al., 2026; Koo et al., 2026), and feedback from other agents identifies which changes are worth pursuing. Intermediate re-sults also become stepping stones for other search paths (Wang et al., 2019, 2020), and their value depends on combinations with later findings or implementations. Their current performance thus underestimates their contribution to other agents’ future research. This leads to our central question of how scientific agent swarms can evolve toward recursive self-improvement. To address this, we introduce S WARM RSI, a framework that evolves scientific agent swarms for recursive self-improvement. Each agent uses its hypotheses, programs, experimental evidence, and unresolved needs to plan further research, and a shared graph records these inheritable re-search states. In Feedback-Guided Swarm Muta-tion, agents revise hypotheses and programs using other agents’ experimental feedback. Performance differences inform hypothesis revision, and unmet research needs guide implementation changes. In Complementary Swarm Recombination, agents re-visit retained hypotheses on other agents’ imple-mentations and combine program components with complementary strengths. Recursive Swarm Evolu-tion evaluates the resulting candidates, retains use-ful results, feedback and unresolved needs in the shared graph, and uses these states to decide which hypotheses agents revisit and which programs they refine. Each experiment thus updates the states that later agents start from. We evaluate SWARM RSI on OpenProb-lems (Luecken et al., 2025), NatureBench (Wang et al., 2026b) and MLE-Bench Lite (Chan et al., 2025), 118 scientific coding and machine-learning engineering tasks, with two backbones. It achieves SOTA results on OpenProblems and NatureBench and outperforms hypothesis and program evolution agents with the same backbone. With DeepSeek-V4-Pro in one seed over all 90 NatureBench tasks, about 77% of its final best programs descend from a rebase, a crossover or an update triggered by feedback. Biologists reproduce two of its programs and use them in follow-up analyses of intestinal and splenic immunity. Our main contributions are: • We formulate RSI for scientific agent swarms as evolving inheritable research states, so that each experiment updates the hypotheses, programs and evidence that later agents start from. • We introduce S WARM RSI, which updates these states through Feedback-Guided Swarm Muta-tion, Complementary Swarm Recombination and Recursive Swarm Evolution. • S WARM RSI achieves SOTA results on Open-Problems and NatureBench, most of its final Na-tureBench programs build on inherited states, and biologists reproduce its programs for immune analyses. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 LLM Agents for Scientific Research 

LLM agents automate scientific research through hypothesis generation, experiment implementation, and result evaluation (Tang et al., 2025; Yamada et al., 2025). Agents centred on programs search over or refine code with execution feedback (Jiang et al., 2025; Nam et al., 2025), R&D-Agent coor-dinates proposal generation and implementation through specialized roles (Yang et al., 2025), and systems with memory carry execution experience into later experiments (Zhu et al., 2026; Du et al., 2026). Closest to our setting, Arbor links explicit hypotheses to programs and experimental evidence in a persistent hypothesis tree (Jin et al., 2026), and SAGE evaluates competing failure explanations to direct interventions toward hypotheses, experimen-tal design, or implementation (Ma et al., 2026). SWARM RSI likewise grounds hypotheses in exper-iments and lets agents use one another’s feedback and partial results.

3 SwarmRSI 

SWARM RSI evolves scientific agent swarms by up-dating the research states agents inherit. Feedback-Guided Swarm Mutation uses other agents’ feed-back to guide revisions, and Complementary Swarm Recombination reuses their hypotheses and program components. Recursive Swarm Evolution evaluates every candidate and retains its outcome, so each experiment informs subsequent research. Figure 2 shows the shared research state and the execution loop that updates it.

A hypothesis h specifies a modeling or algorithmic mechanism, and a pro-gram p implements it under the task’s constraints. Different programs may test the same hypothesis. Scientific agent swarm. A scientific agent is a research policy for hypothesis proposal, program development, or evidence analysis. At research step t, the acting agent uses a research state 

st = ( Ht, Pt, Et, Ut), (2) containing the hypotheses, programs, experimen-tal evidence, and unresolved needs available for 3its current action. 3.2 Feedback-Guided Swarm Mutation 

To guide revisions of hypotheses and their im-plementations, Feedback-Guided Swarm Mutation uses experimental feedback from the swarm. An unsuccessful run points to an implementation bar-rier, and differences across instances point to a revised research direction. At step t, the agent uses the selected parent pair (ht, p t) and state st, which provides outcomes, implementation evidence, and unresolved needs from its own and other agents’ records. The agent proposes a hypothesis and a revision plan: 

(˜ht, δ t) = Propose( q, h t, p t; st), (3) where δt describes the intended changes and their rationale. Differences across instances guide hy-pothesis revisions, and execution failures or unreal-ized interventions guide program repair. We call a revision that changes the hypothesis an H-mutation ,and one that keeps it ( ˜ht = ht) and repairs only the implementation a P-mutation . The agent then implements the plan on the parent program using relevant state information: 

˜pt = Implement( pt, ˜ht, δ t; st). (4) The resulting candidate (˜ht, ˜pt) retains its par-ent relation and revision rationale in the shared research state. It then enters the shared evaluation loop, which tests validity and measures utility. 

3.3 Complementary Swarm Recombination 

Complementary Swarm Recombination reuses par-tial results across agents, including programs whose overall scores did not improve. 

Hypothesis reuse. For a hypothesis ht with un-resolved implementation needs, an agent selects a valid base bt from another agent by matching these needs to program mechanisms (Wiegand et al., 2001). The rebase operation keeps ht and adapts its implementation to bt, giving (˜ht, ˜pt) = (ht, Rebase( ht, b t; st)) .

4.1 Research Questions 

We address four research questions and illustrate biological applications through case studies: RQ1 :How does S WARM RSI perform across benchmarks and backbones, and at what token cost? RQ2 :How do Feedback-Guided Swarm Mutation, Com-plementary Swarm Recombination, and Recursive Swarm Evolution contribute to performance? RQ3 :How do experimental feedback and reused interme-diate results guide the swarm’s subsequent research within and across tasks? RQ4 : What are the main failure modes, and how frequent are they?

4.4 Evaluation Metrics 

OpenProblems reports normalized task scores scaled by 100 and their unweighted mean. Na-tureBench reports Match-SOTA ( g ≥ 0) and Surpass-SOTA ( g > 0.1) rates, where g is the of-ficial direction-adjusted improvement over SOTA. MLE-Bench Lite reports Above Median and medal rates from official grading on the private test split. Rates retain all tasks in the denominator, including invalid or missing results. Match-SOTA compar-isons with Arbor and AIDE on NatureBench use 95% paired bootstrap confidence intervals and Mc-Nemar tests, and other comparisons are descriptive. Token cost sums uncached input and output tokens over all LLM calls in a search. 

4.5 Implementation Details 

SWARM RSI and rerun baselines use DeepSeek-V4-Pro (DeepSeek, 2026) under matched settings for each benchmark. Results from our runs in Tables 1– 3 are averaged over three random seeds. Published results keep the settings of their sources. We addi-tionally evaluate S WARM RSI with Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (Google DeepMind, 2026). In each run, all agents share one backbone and advance lineages that exchange results through the shared graph. 

5 Results and Analysis 

To answer our research questions, we compare benchmark performance, conduct component ab-lations, examine search trajectories, analyze anno-tated failures, and report biological case studies. 

5.1 Main Results (RQ1) 

Tables 1 to 3 summarize benchmark performance, and we make three observations. • SWARM RSI outperforms domain-specific and general coding agents. With DeepSeek-V4-Pro, it reaches an OpenProblems average of 74.2 against 67.1 for TusoAI and exceeds Claude Code with the same backbone by 21.1 percentage points (pp) in NatureBench Match-SOTA. • SWARM RSI outperforms hypothesis and pro-gram evolution agents. With the same back-bone, its NatureBench Match-SOTA rate exceeds Arbor by 7.8 pp and AIDE by 16.7 pp, both sig-nificantly. Its 81.8% Any-Medal rate on MLE-Bench Lite also exceeds Arbor and MLEvolve, and, as shown in Section B.6, it uses fewer tokens than TusoAI and Arbor. • SWARM RSI remains strong across backbones. 

As shown in Figure 3, with Gemini 3.1 Pro Pre-view it reaches the highest OpenProblems aver-age of 76.2 and the same 44.4% NatureBench Match-SOTA rate as with DeepSeek-V4-Pro, which exceeds every compared method, and it keeps the 81.8% Any-Medal rate on MLE-Bench Lite.

Removing H- or P-mutation lowers Match-SOTA by 10.0 and 13.3 pp, respectively. The two modes are complemen-tary, and program repair contributes more. • Complementary Swarm Recombination im-proves solution quality. Disabling rebase and crossover lowers Match-SOTA by 6.7 pp and Surpass-SOTA from 15.6% to 7.8%, and its drop in mean g excludes zero. RQ3 traces this gain to reused hypotheses and program components in most final programs. • Recursive Swarm Evolution depends on the shared graph and H/P selection. Match-SOTA drops by 7.8 pp without the shared graph, through which agents use other agents’ records, and by 8.9 pp without H/P selection, which also causes the largest drop in mean g.

5.5 Biological Case Studies 

We examine the scientific utility of programs evolved by S WARM RSI. Biologists reproduced two of its programs and interpreted them with measured RNA and protein. 

Enterocyte: infection-associated immune ex-pression. On the contrastiveVI task (Weinberger et al., 2023), the evolved program removes from each infected cell the mean of its healthy neigh-bours before reducing the residuals. On the Haber intestinal instance (Haber et al., 2017), measured enterocyte RNA shows higher MHC-II scores in helminth day 10 than Salmonella infection, with means of 1.17 and 0.34. Figure 5a–c shows the cor-responding expression gradients on the reproduced representation. Within the two helminth day-10 sources, MHC-II scores are locally more coher-ent on this representation than on RNA PCA, with Spearman’s ρ after covariate adjustment of 0.77 and 0.69 against 0.65 and 0.40, whereas antimicro-bial scores are less locally coherent than on RNA PCA in each of the four sources. 

CytoCommunity: spatial context without con-sistent marker shifts. On the CytoCommunity task (Hu et al., 2024), the evolved program com-bines anatomical priors derived from phenotypes with an anatomically constrained Markov random field, raising g from 0.045 to 0.102. Figure 5d–f compares its partitions with manual anatomy and experts’ subsequent grouping of B-cell neighbour-hoods by local cell composition. Across three spleen images, 20–21% of B cells in the manually annotated marginal zone occupy myeloid/erythroid-rich neighbourhoods. Goltsev et al. (2018) reported that B220 and CD79b levels on splenic B cells vary with the local neighbourhood across the spleen. Within the marginal zone, after matching these B cells on location and phenotype within each image, these and four other markers show no consistent shift between neighbourhoods across the three im-ages, and the B220 effect changes sign. 

6 Conclusion 

We began with two obstacles for scientific agent swarms. A failed experiment does not show whether its hypothesis or its implementation was at fault, and a program without overall gains can still hold a component that another agent needs. SWARM RSI addresses both. Feedback-Guided Swarm Mutation uses other agents’ feedback to de-cide whether to revise a hypothesis or repair its pro-gram, and Complementary Swarm Recombination carries retained hypotheses and useful components into other agents’ work. Recursive Swarm Evolu-tion returns every outcome to the shared graph, so each experiment changes the states that later agents start from. It achieves SOTA results on OpenProb-lems and NatureBench and outperforms baselines with the same backbone, most final NatureBench programs build on such reuse, and biologists repro-duce its programs for follow-up analyses of intesti-8nal and splenic immunity. Both frontiers, H- and P-mutation, the shared graph, audits, local refinement, recombina-tion, and total search budget remain unchanged. 14 A.2 Execution and Feedback 

The execution harness and search controller per-form the online audits automatically. The code audit uses execution status, artifact validity and evaluator feedback to check execution and the task contract. The scientific audit screens imple-mentation evidence by comparing parent and off-spring source code and recorded submitted outputs. It flags unchanged source or syntax trees, newly added functions without static name references, or identical submitted outputs when comparable output records are available. The controller uses these source/output flags to gate hypothesis credit. A valid scored candidate without such a flag re-ceives hypothesis credit (Potter and De Jong, 2000). Flagged or unscored attempts retain program-level evidence and unresolved implementation requests. Hypothesis credit records a valid scored candidate passing the source/output checks. Improvement per cost drives H/P selection. Failed states and states without gain remain available in the graph together with their resulting programs, outcomes, and audit evidence. An implementation failure retains the hypothe-sis and emits a deepen request for program refine-ment. Mixed per-instance gains and losses emit a broaden request for hypothesis exploration. An improved incumbent also creates deepen requests for hypotheses previously realized on older pro-grams. These requests retain unresolved needs for later mutation or recombination.

D Human Failure Analysis 

Sample and unit of analysis. We reviewed 35 failed S WARM RSI task trajectories across Na-tureBench and MLE-Bench Lite. The annotation unit was an individual attempt, assessed against the best preceding valid result using each benchmark’s metric direction. The final failure set comprised 644 attempts across these trajectories, after apply-ing the exclusions below. OpenProblems reports continuous normalized scores without a binary suc-cess criterion, so no OpenProblems trajectories en-ter the failure set. The 35 trajectories comprise 32 NatureBench tasks below Match-SOTA and 3 MLE-Bench Lite competitions without a medal. 

Evaluation criteria. Annotators examined the proposed intervention, implementation and exe-cution records, evaluator outputs, and preceding scores. Implementation or execution failure de-notes an intervention that could not be reliably tested because of code or interface errors, execu-tion interruptions, or proposal–implementation mis-matches. No performance gain denotes a faithfully implemented and executed intervention with a valid evaluation that did not improve the preceding best valid result. Equal scores were treated as no gain. Recovered intermediate errors were judged by the final evaluated outcome. Successful verification or restoration of an existing result, and repetition of an attained metric ceiling, were excluded from the failure counts. Attempts with insufficient ev-idence to distinguish the two failure classes were also excluded. 20 Human review and agreement. Two annotators with PhDs in bioinformatics engineering reviewed the evidence using these criteria, with inter-rater agreement of Cohen’s κ = 0 .92 .

Representative cases. On task 00838, a pro-posed amino-acid substitution was overwritten by downstream rules, leaving the evaluated output un-changed This was an implementation failure de-spite a valid score. In contrast, on task 02316, a negative-binomial mixture with a Potts spatial prior ran successfully but collapsed all locations into one cluster, yielding ARI zero. This was a no-gain outcome.

Dataset and evolved program. NatureBench task s41592-023-02124-2 uses three healthy mouse spleen CODEX images associated with CytoCom-munity (Hu et al., 2024): 82,382, 81,925 and 80,973 cells, respectively, with 27 supplied cell phenotypes. S WARM RSI receives coordinates and phenotype labels. The expert follow-up separately examines measured protein intensities. The pro-gram selected by S WARM RSI comes from step 12 of a 15-step search and raises g from 0.04507 to 0.10215. It uses phenotype-derived anatomical priors, 50-neighbour composition and interface fea-tures, Gaussian EM, an anatomically constrained spatial MRF and cleanup of components smaller than 150 cells. Recorded macro-F1 and AMI are 0.639247 and 0.583341. Macro-F1 includes the unannotated reference category, whose F1 is zero. Marginal-zone F1 is 0.600, 0.593 and 0.635. 

Post-hoc neighbourhood descriptions. The analysis includes 35,168, 33,544 and 32,451 conventionally annotated B cells (101,163 to-tal). Experts fit three exploratory neighbourhood-composition prototypes in the first image and then hold them fixed for the other two, giving B/FDC-rich (E1), myeloid/erythroid-rich (E2) and T-cell-rich (E3) groups. E1–E3 describe local cell compo-sition around B cells in this post-hoc analysis. They are distinct from the program’s anatomical parti-tions. The primary comparison uses E2 versus E1 within the manual marginal zone, independently of whether the S WARM RSI program assigned that cell to the marginal zone. Coordinates are analyzed in dataset units. 

Expert protein and geometry checks. The anal-ysis uses processed protein measurements for all 245,280 cells. Within each image and manual marginal zone, matching is exact on original phe-notype cluster and imaging tile, then uses five ge-ometry variables with a 0.5-SD per-variable caliper, joint distance at most 1 and no replacement. There are 815, 995 and 1,025 matched E2–E1 pairs, re-taining 42.5%, 46.2% and 41.1% of E2 cells, re-spectively. Maximum absolute geometry standard-ized mean differences are 0.080, 0.063 and 0.083. Protein contrasts use this matched overlap subset. All six preselected proteins (CD79b, B220, IgD, IgM, MHCII, CD44) are shown in Figure 9b. Mea-surements are transformed as arcsinh( x/ 150) , re-taining negative values. Effects are differences of transformed means divided by the within-image contrast-population standard deviation. Intervals use 1,000 bootstrap resamples of 1,000-coordinate-unit spatial blocks within each image. B220 effects are +0 .082 , −0.130 and −0.190 . Stricter 0.25-SD matching also yields inconsistent directions. In spatially blocked validation, adding neighbourhood labels to a geometry/phenotype protein predictor produces ∆R2 from −0.00098 to +0 .00967 across 18 image–protein combinations. Neighbourhood labels add little predictive information for these six protein markers in spatially blocked validation. 24 F Limitations 

SWARM RSI requires an evaluator that can execute candidate programs, so research without executable feedback is outside its scope. Execution feedback does not always reveal whether the intended in-tervention was faithfully implemented, and per-formance gains alone do not establish its scien-tific mechanism. Rebase selects program bases by matching recorded implementation needs to pro-gram mechanism labels, a heuristic that does not measure what a program can do. Crossover applies only when a benchmark reports scores per instance. H/P selection adapts discounted UCB without in-heriting its guarantees, and its benefit is established only empirically. Evaluation covers comparisons under matched budgets on three benchmark families. The human failure analysis characterizes 644 annotated failed attempts from 35 task trajectories. Cross-task ev-idence comes from six source tasks, 12 held-out tasks and one backbone, and generations are con-founded with the number of source tasks seen. The evaluation does not separately assess lit-erature synthesis (Shi et al., 2025), idea qual-ity (Sauter et al., 2026), or peer-review quality (Guo et al., 2026b). Generalization to complete re-search workflows (Lu et al., 2024; Schmidgall et al., 2025; Lyu et al., 2026) and information-access agents (Kanoulas et al., 2025; Cao et al., 2026) requires additional evaluation. Other untested settings include legal reason-ing (Lyu et al., 2022, 2023a; Wu et al., 2023), zero-shot entity extraction (Wang et al., 2025), conver-sational recommendation (Zhang et al., 2024), and shopping research (Lyu et al., 2025c). These set-tings require task-specific objectives and evaluation protocols. 

G Ethics Statement 

Benchmark gains do not establish factual reliabil-ity or alignment with human preferences, which require separate evaluation and optimization (Lin et al., 2022; Lyu et al., 2024a; Rafailov et al., 2023; Lyu et al., 2025b). We do not assess ro-bustness to dataset shortcuts (Lyu et al., 2023b), social stereotypes (Parrish et al., 2022), or cogni-tive biases (Lyu et al., 2024b, 2025a), nor do we test harmful-content generation. Applications that affect people should preserve user agency and pro-vide domain-appropriate human oversight (Zhang et al., 2025). Generated programs and scientific interpreta-tions require independent validation before use be-yond the benchmark setting. The execution and scientific audits check task validity and implemen-tation evidence; they do not certify security or scientific correctness. The biological case studies analyse existing datasets, and their associations do not establish causal mechanisms or clinical valid-ity. Any deployment should restrict code execution, external-tool access and credentials to the approved scope, and review proposed uses for potential harm. Data reuse should respect source licences, access restrictions and privacy requirements, with prove-nance and intended uses documented (Gebru et al., 2021). We report time budgets, hardware configu-rations and token consumption to support resource-aware comparisons (Schwartz et al., 2020). Token consumption is not a direct measurement of energy use or carbon emissions. 25
