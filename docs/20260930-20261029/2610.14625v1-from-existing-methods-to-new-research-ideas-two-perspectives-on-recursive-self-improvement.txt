# From Existing Methods to New Research Ideas: Two Perspectives on Recursive Self-Improvement 

Leijun Zhou 1,∗, Yanke Yu 1,∗, Zhihao Liu 1, Qu Xiang 1, Tiancheng Hu 2, Yiru Dong 1, Yihan Wu 1,

Equal contribution. 

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) offers a route to AI systems that participate in their own continued development. Recent methods allow language agents to retain changes to their parameters, memory, skills, and code for subsequent execution and improvement. We examine how these methods can inform new research ideas through two complementary perspectives. First, we introduce a unified taxonomy with nine design dimensions organized into four groups: update targets, update organization, update schedule and scope, feedback and selection. This taxonomy places existing methods in a shared design space, where examining individual dimensions and their interactions can inspire new research ideas. Second, we examine how existing RSI methods draw on traditional deep learning, for example through optimization, regularization, data augmentation, continual learning, and adversarial learning. We develop further ideas by examining more specific learning mechanisms and drawing on evolutionary computation. We also review how existing benchmarks assess self-improvement under online, offline, and offline-to-online settings. Together, these perspectives help researchers develop new RSI research ideas from existing designs and learning mechanisms. RSI papers and resources are available at https://prism-shadow.github.io/awesome-rsi/ .

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) is a process in which an AI system participates in modifying itself, retains these modifications, and uses the revised system in subsequent execution and improvement. Its appeal lies in the possibility that an improvement makes the system better at producing further improvements. A growing share of AI development could then be carried out by the systems being developed. This possibility underlies Good’s suggestion that a sufficiently capable machine might be the last invention humans need to make [1]. Recent research has produced numerous RSI methods, including those that update model parameters, revise memory and skills, and modify agent code [2, 3, 4, 5]. Several surveys now organize this literature by improvement targets, autonomy levels, or the degree of loop closure [6, 7, 8]. These classifications help describe the field, but assigning a method to a broad category does not fully explain its design. Two methods that revise agent code, for example, can differ in who proposes changes, how earlier versions are reused, and what feedback determines which changes are retained. Generating new research ideas from existing work requires examining its design dimensions and their interactions to identify limitations of current mechanisms and new ways to improve them. Existing surveys also discuss connections to earlier learning paradigms, but offer less systematic guidance on using specific mechanisms from traditional deep learning to generate new RSI research ideas. This calls for an analysis of how RSI adapts these mechanisms and which principles can be reused. We view existing RSI methods as combinations of design choices. These choices define a high-dimensional design space: each dimension captures an aspect of the improvement process, and each method occupies a point determined by its choices along these dimensions. Locating existing work in this space allows us to examine individual dimensions and their interactions, identify limitations of current mechanisms, and develop new research ideas for agent improvement. To characterize this space, we introduce a unified taxonomy with four groups of design dimensions: update targets , update organization , update schedule and scope , and feedback and selection . These groups encompass nine dimensions, which we use to describe representative methods, allowing methods developed for different settings to be compared within the same space. The accompanying 3Awesome RSI website lets readers browse and filter methods along these dimensions. 1

Traditional deep learning provides a second source of research ideas for RSI. It has developed mechanisms for choosing updates, learning from feedback, and controlling overfitting, all of which are relevant to recursive self-improvement. Recent work already draws on these connections: SkillAdam adapts ideas from Adam to skill revision; TextGrad applies the idea of backpropagation to textual feedback; and RRSI regularizes changes to agent code [4, 9, 10]. We study these correspondences to understand which principles carry over to RSI. We then examine more specific mechanisms within these connections and draw on evolutionary computation to develop further research ideas. We also review benchmarks and evaluation settings for online, offline, and offline-to-online improvement. This accompanying analysis explains what each setting measures and motivates shared evaluation protocols. Our contributions are twofold. • A unified taxonomy of agent self-improvement. We introduce nine design dimensions organized into four groups, grounded in a formal description of the self-improvement loop. This taxonomy places existing methods in a common design space, allowing us to compare their mechanisms and examine relationships across dimensions. Through a concrete example, we illustrate how this analysis can reveal limitations of existing designs and inspire new RSI research ideas. • Insights from traditional deep learning. We analyze how existing RSI methods draw on established learning mechanisms, for example through optimization, regularization, data augmentation, continual learning, and adversarial learning. We explain the problems these mechanisms originally address and how RSI methods adapt them to self-improvement. Building on this analysis, we explore how deeper developments of these mechanisms, together with ideas from broader machine learning and evolutionary algorithms, can generate further RSI research ideas. 

# 2 Background 

We consider an agentic system to perform recursive self-improvement (RSI) when it modifies itself, retains the changes, and uses the updated system to execute subsequent tasks and produce further self-modifications. We first introduce the agentic system and its task-execution loop, then formalize the self-improvement loop and connect it to the design dimensions developed in our taxonomy. 

## 2.1 Agentic systems 

An agentic system consists of a language model and a harness. The harness comprises tools, context, memory, skills, and executable code. Tools are callable operations through which the system obtains information and acts on the environment. Context is the information assembled for a model call, including instructions, task inputs, and relevant interaction history. Memory stores information beyond the current context for retrieval and reuse. Skills package task-specific instructions with optional scripts and reference material as reusable resources. Executable code coordinates model calls, context construction, tool execution, and the use of memory and skills. The environment refers to the external systems that the agent can observe and affect. 

## 2.2 The ReAct Loop 

A basic chatbot produces an answer from a query, the supplied context, and knowledge stored in its parameters. An agent extends this setting by taking actions and using the resulting observations to decide what to do next. It can obtain information, execute operations, and revise its plan as the task proceeds. Feedback can also guide revision of an attempted solution [11]. ReAct organizes this interaction through the interleaving of reasoning and acting [12]. We use ReAct Loop to describe this task-execution cycle. In each iteration, the harness assembles the context for a model call and executes the tool operations requested by the model. Observations from the environment become part of the information available for subsequent decisions. Repeated interaction produces execution traces and task outcomes that can inform changes to the system itself.

# 3 A Unified Taxonomy of Agent Self-Improvement 

An RSI method combines choices about what changes, who produces the changes, when they are made and reused, and how feedback guides their retention. We propose a unified taxonomy that describes these choices through nine dimensions organized into four groups. Together, the dimensions define a method design space. Describing existing methods in this space helps researchers compare their mechanisms and examine how choices along different dimensions interact. This analysis can reveal limitations of current mechanisms and inspire new research ideas for agent improvement. 

## 3.1 Overview of the unified taxonomy 

The nine design dimensions are organized into four groups, as shown in Figure 2. 

1. Update targets: RSI artifact. 

2. Update organization: Artifact updater and RSI topology. 

3. Update schedule and scope: RSI mode, Update frequency, and Experience scope. 

4. Feedback and selection: Feedback source, Feedback type, and Acceptance criterion. Figure 3 organizes the reviewed methods by their update targets. Appendix A lists the method papers, benchmarks, and software systems covered in this survey, together with their classifications. The following sections define each dimension and illustrate its choices with representative methods. 

## 3.2 Update targets 

Update targets describe the parts of an agentic system that are modified and retained for subsequent use. We characterize these targets through the RSI artifact dimension. 

RSI artifact. This dimension distinguishes updates to model weights ( Parametric ) from updates to resources outside those weights ( Non-parametric ). Non-parametric targets are grouped by their role in the system. 

Context supplies information to model calls; Memory stores information for later retrieval; Skill packages reusable instructions and supporting resources; and Harness code implements tool operations and coordinates model calls, tool execution, and control flow. Parametric methods update the language model’s weights using training data and feedback. SEAL generates training material or update instructions and uses them to fine-tune the model. The updated model’s task performance then serves as a reward for learning to generate more effective self-edits [2]. Context updates change the information supplied to model calls and how it is organized. They include revising task instructions and selecting, compressing, or reorganizing observations and interaction history as long-horizon tasks unfold. GEPA uses execution traces and feedback to propose revised instructions, then evaluates the candidates before retaining them [18]. ACON optimizes the guidelines used to compress observations and interaction history. ## 3.3 Update organization 

Update organization describes how the system produces successive versions of itself. It combines two dimensions: Artifact updater identifies who forms a change, and RSI topology describes how a new version builds on earlier versions. 

Artifact updater. This dimension distinguishes Self , Teacher , and Joint updates. Self denotes changes produced by the agent being improved. Teacher denotes changes produced by a separate updating role using the agent’s execution records and feedback. Joint combines contributions from the agent and a teacher: the agent proposes or revises a change, and the teacher further refines, curates, or selects it. SEAL provides a Self example: the model generates material used to update its own parameters [2]. Recuris assigns skill-memory revision to a meta-agent, while GEPA uses a reflective optimizer to revise the evaluated system’s instructions; both illustrate Teacher updates [30, 18]. DGM illustrates Joint updating: a diagnosis guides a self-modification attempt by the selected coding agent, and evaluation determines whether the resulting version enters the archive [5]. 

RSI topology. This dimension describes dependencies between versions through Sequential , Tree , and Graph 

topologies. Sequential improvement repeatedly updates the current version to form a single chain. A Tree allows archived versions to branch, with each child derived from one parent. A Graph allows a new version to draw on retained state, changes, or outcomes from several historical versions or lineages. A-MEM illustrates Sequential updates by incorporating new notes and revising the current memory store [21]. DGM selects an archived agent as a parent and modifies its code to produce a child; repeatedly branching from archived agents forms a Tree [5]. HGM also maintains a branching archive and uses descendant performance to guide the choice of promising branches [31]. Mendel Gödel Machine illustrates Graph dependencies. It compares the target agent’s execution trace with that of an agent from another lineage on the same task, then uses the comparison to guide code changes [32]. GEPA’s Merge variant draws on complementary changes from different candidates [18]. In both cases, a new version can inherit useful information from more than one lineage. 

## 3.4 Update schedule and scope 

Update schedule and scope describes when changes are made and which tasks they are intended to support. Three dimensions specify these choices: RSI mode places improvement relative to system use, Update frequency 

records when individual updates are triggered, and Experience scope describes the intended range of reuse. 

RSI mode. This dimension distinguishes Offline , Online , and Offline → Online improvement. Offline improvement occurs in a separate training or search phase before evaluation or deployment. Online improvement occurs during use, so later tasks can use the newly retained state. Offline → Online first builds a resource offline and then continues to update it during use. DGM and SkillAdam illustrate Offline improvement by searching for code or skill revisions before evaluating the resulting system [5, 4]. A-MEM and ReasoningBank illustrate Online improvement by incorporating new records or distilled strategies as tasks are processed [21, 20]. Agent Workflow Memory studies both offline workflow construction and continued online induction, including the use of offline workflows to initialize online improvement [33]. ACE separately studies offline playbook construction and online updating [15]. 

Update frequency. This dimension describes the trigger or unit of an individual update through Step , Event ,

Trajectory , and Batch . A Step update follows one action–observation step. An Event update is triggered by a specified condition, such as detecting a failure or receiving new information. A Trajectory update follows a complete task execution, while a Batch update uses a group of trajectories or candidates. ReasoningBank extracts memories from completed trajectories, whereas SkillAdam uses batch-level results to guide skill revisions [20, 4]. PILOT illustrates how different update frequencies can coexist. A supervisor 9can redirect an active worker and record reusable skills or memory during execution. After a group of runs finishes, changes from successful runs are merged into the shared state used by the next iteration [34]. These operations correspond to Event and Batch updates, respectively. 

Experience scope. This dimension describes the tasks for which an updated artifact is intended to be reused. 

General artifacts support reuse across task types or domains. Specialized artifacts serve a particular instance, topic, or task family. A system can retain resources with different scopes. DGM develops a reusable coding harness and studies its transfer between SWE-bench and Polyglot, illustrating General scope [5]. SkillAdam refines skill documents for particular task settings, illustrating Specialized scope [4]. AlgoEvo reuses experience both within and across tasks: search records guide further attempts on the current algorithm-design task, while skills distilled from multiple tasks guide subsequent algorithm-design tasks [29]. 

## 3.5 Feedback and selection 

Feedback and selection describes how the system obtains guidance for updates and decides which changes to retain. We separate three dimensions: Feedback source identifies where the guidance comes from, Feedback type describes its form, and Acceptance criterion specifies the basis for retaining a change. 

Feedback source. This dimension groups information by its origin into six categories. A Train/dev set 

provides task examples and reference outputs; a Benchmark supplies results from a standardized evaluation suite. The Environment provides observations and action outcomes, while an Executable verifier produces test or validation results. LLM feedback supplies model-generated evaluations or suggestions. A Human can provide corrections, preferences, or guidance. An update can use several sources. DGM uses coding-benchmark results and executable tests to assess revised agents [5]. SIFT adds model-generated pairwise comparisons between candidate patches to guide parent selection and prioritize execution-based evaluation [35]. SkillHEX combines model-generated failure hypotheses with executable checks: it tests the hypotheses on cached outputs from skill versions and uses the results to guide later revisions [36]. 

Feedback type. This dimension describes the form of feedback, distinguishing Score and Non-score information. Score feedback summarizes an outcome or assessment as a Binary result, such as success or failure, or a 

Non-binary numerical or categorical evaluation. Non-score feedback supplies content for revising the system: 

Ground truth provides a reference answer or behavior, LLM-as-a-judge provides a textual assessment, and 

Other covers observations, execution records, and diagnostics. The type is determined by the returned information: a numerical model rating is a Score, while its explanation is Non-score feedback. GEPA uses execution scores to compare candidates and detailed execution feedback to guide revisions [18]. ReasoningBank judges whether a trajectory succeeded and extracts lessons from both successful and failed attempts [20]. ACE’s reflector analyzes execution and produces guidance for playbook updates [15]. These methods use both compact assessments and information about the behavior that produced an outcome. 

Acceptance criterion. This dimension describes how a system decides whether to retain a proposed change. 

Artifact validation checks whether the modified resource meets requirements such as successful compilation or interface compatibility. Instance result uses performance on an individual task. Benchmark score uses aggregate performance on a benchmark. Combined metrics considers several measures, such as performance, cost, and stability. No validation denotes direct retention without a separate acceptance check. GEPA evaluates candidate instructions before adding successful revisions to its search population [18]. Designer-RSI compares candidate and reference skills on matched replays with the same upstream state and retrieved assets. It admits a change when at least one replayed case improves and no regression is detected on the others [37]. RRSI considers performance gains together with execution cost and evaluation noise, illustrating Combined metrics [10]. ACE and A-MEM illustrate No validation by incorporating curated playbook edits or newly constructed memory records without a separate performance-based acceptance test [15, 21]. 10 3.6 Ideas from the unified taxonomy 

This section illustrates how the unified taxonomy can help generate new RSI research ideas. Starting from existing methods, we examine the relationship between relevant dimensions, identify a limitation of their current designs, and use it to motivate a research direction. We begin with Harness code under the RSI artifact dimension. Code updates can change task execution or the information recorded for failure diagnosis. HarnessFix can modify model-input logging, context snapshots, and state-change records [38]. EvoTrainer also revises its diagnostic metrics and analysis procedures when they do not provide enough information to guide further changes [39]. We illustrate this with an agent that can modify its own harness. During task execution, it uses a web research tool to find papers published within a given date range, but its requests repeatedly fail. During self-improvement, the same agent examines its logs, discovers that the request parameters are missing, and edits its logging code to record them. When it runs the search task again, the new logs reveal a date format that the tool does not accept. In the next round of self-improvement, it fixes the code that prepares the dates. The first edit does not resolve the search failure, but supplies the evidence needed for the later repair. Whether the first change is retained, however, depends on the Acceptance criterion . Ecdysis, for example, retains a candidate harness only when its overall training score exceeds that of the current version [40]. Under such a rule, a logging change that leaves the score unchanged would be rejected before it could support a later repair. This reveals a mismatch between the two dimensions: code updates can improve failure diagnosis, while an acceptance rule based on immediate task scores may overlook that contribution. This mismatch motivates a research direction: acceptance criteria that account for a change’s contribution to subsequent self-improvement. For harness code, the criteria could consider both current task performance and whether an edit helps the agent diagnose and repair failures in later updates. The taxonomy guides this idea by exposing a gap between what the modified resource can contribute and what the current acceptance rule measures. 

# 4 Insights from Traditional Deep Learning 

Traditional deep learning can inform the design of RSI methods. We first examine how existing work uses learning and optimization mechanisms for agent self-improvement. We then explore how further developments of these mechanisms can inspire new research ideas, extending the discussion to broader methods in machine learning and evolutionary computation. 

## 4.1 Connections in Existing RSI Methods 

Existing RSI methods draw on traditional deep learning to guide how agents learn from feedback and modify themselves. This section examines the problems these learning mechanisms were developed to address and how representative RSI methods apply them to self-improvement. Figure 4 summarizes these connections. 

4.1.1 Optimization 

Optimizers in traditional deep learning determine the direction and magnitude of parameter updates. Adam, for example, uses information accumulated from current and past gradients to compute its parameter updates [41]. Iterative skill revision in RSI faces a similar problem: deciding what to modify and how much to change at each iteration. SkillAdam draws on this optimizer design to guide the revision of agent skills [4]. It records identified problems, attempted fixes, and their outcomes, allowing later revisions to build on earlier attempts. It also compares task performance before and after a revision to determine how much of the skill may be changed next. When the gains vary substantially across evaluation cases, it reduces the amount of editing allowed in the next iteration; more consistent gains permit broader changes. These mechanisms use the history and results of previous revisions to guide both what to change and how much to change. 11 Traditional deep learning RSI

4.1.2 Backpropagation 

Training a neural network requires using errors in its predictions to adjust parameters across its layers. Backpropagation works backward through the computation graph to calculate how the loss changes with each parameter, providing gradients for parameter updates [42]. RSI systems face a related problem: using feedback on a task result to guide changes to individual components. TextGrad draws on this idea to propagate textual feedback through a system [9]. It represents operations and their input–output relationships as a computation graph, then passes feedback on the final result backward through that graph. At each step, a language model uses the local inputs, outputs, and feedback to generate revision suggestions for upstream variables. An optimizer then uses these suggestions to revise selected variables, such as instructions and code. For RSI, this suggests a way to turn task-level feedback into specific changes by following the steps that produced the result. 

4.1.3 Reinforcement Learning 

Reinforcement learning uses rewards from interaction to improve decisions. Value-based methods estimate the expected return of available choices and use these estimates to guide selection [43]. This principle can also guide memory retrieval: an agent can use task outcomes to learn which memories are useful and adjust its selection on subsequent tasks. MemRL applies value estimation to this selection process [3]. It first retrieves a pool of memories by semantic similarity, then selects from this pool using both relevance and learned utility values. After the agent executes the task, the resulting reward updates the utility values of the memories supplied to the agent. Memories 12 associated with higher rewards are consequently more likely to be selected for later tasks. With the language model’s parameters fixed, feedback improves how the agent selects from its accumulated records. 

4.1.4 Meta-Learning 

Meta-learning uses performance after learning to improve how learning is carried out. For example, it can optimize a model’s initialization or learn a parameter-update rule by evaluating how well the model performs after training [44, 45]. In RSI, this principle can guide the production of training data and update instructions: their quality can be assessed through the learning they produce. SEAL implements this idea by training a model to generate its own self-edits [2]. Given new information or a task, the model produces training materials or instructions for updating its parameters. The proposed update is applied through fine-tuning, and the updated model is evaluated on the relevant task. Its performance then serves as a reward for reinforcement learning of the self-edit generator. This creates two connected learning processes: one updates the model using generated materials, and the other improves how those materials and update instructions are produced. 

4.1.5 Distillation 

Knowledge distillation transfers behavior from a teacher model to a student by training the student to match the teacher’s output probabilities [46]. These probabilities provide guidance about alternative outputs beyond a single target answer. A self-improving system can use this mechanism while also constructing and updating the source of its supervision. RISE combines distillation with reinforcement learning using verifiable rewards [47]. It compares the model before and after a reinforcement learning update, then constructs a teacher by extending the observed change farther in the same direction. This extrapolation can operate on model weights or output log-probabilities. The student is trained to match the resulting teacher’s token probabilities. As reinforcement learning and distillation alternate, each new training update provides the basis for another teacher. RISE thus incorporates teacher construction into the improvement process, using the model’s own training trajectory to produce supervision for subsequent learning. 

4.1.6 Regularization 

Regularization aims to reduce overfitting and improve generalization beyond the training data. Common approaches constrain model complexity or introduce perturbations during learning. L0 regularization penalizes the number of nonzero parameters to encourage sparsity [48]. L1 regularization encourages sparsity by penalizing the sum of absolute parameter values, while L2 regularization discourages large weights by penalizing the sum of squared parameter values [49]. Dropout introduces perturbations by temporarily masking units during training, reducing reliance on particular combinations of features [50]. RSI faces a similar difficulty when repeated harness or skill revisions fit the tasks used for improvement but transfer poorly to new tasks. RRSI applies complexity control to harness evolution [10]. It gradually reduces the number of independent edits allowed in each proposal, limiting how many changes can be introduced in response to a round of feedback. Components that repeatedly fail to yield gains are marked for removal. Candidate selection also requires increased execution cost to be justified by improved task performance. SkillEvoReg adapts dropout to skill revision [51]. It randomly masks instructions or procedural blocks in the current skill. The agent executes a training task with this partial skill, and the updater uses the resulting execution to propose a revision. The system then restores the masked content and integrates the proposed changes into the complete skill. The aim is to reduce the system’s reliance on particular existing instructions when revising a skill. 13 4.1.7 Data Augmentation 

Data augmentation expands training data by constructing variants of existing examples. In image classification, for example, crops and horizontal reflections provide additional views of labeled images [52]. For a self-improving agent, task variants can similarly provide further practice on the reasoning or operations required by the problems it encounters. TTCS uses a question synthesizer to generate variants of test questions [53]. It changes problem objects, settings, or constraints while aiming to preserve the underlying reasoning structure. The solver trains on both original and generated questions, using agreement among sampled answers as a learning signal. Solver feedback also updates the synthesizer. The system thus learns from the additional data while improving how those data are generated. 

4.1.8 Continual Learning 

Continual learning concerns learning from successive tasks or data while retaining earlier capabilities. Rehearsal reuses past examples during later learning; iCaRL combines this with distillation to preserve previously learned classes [54]. Agent self-improvement raises the same retention problem when a new skill revision disrupts tasks that the agent could previously complete. SEEK uses historical replay to screen such revisions in an industrial search evaluator [55]. Reviewed errors guide changes to the operational instructions in a skill bank. Each candidate is then checked on emerging cases and a fixed set of historical examples. Acceptance requires improvement on the new cases while limiting regression on the historical set. The retained examples serve as a check on successive skill updates, extending the role of past data from parameter training to the acceptance of reusable instructions. 

4.1.9 Pruning 

Pruning removes network weights or structures that contribute little to the desired behavior, often followed by further training [56]. For agents, repeated revisions can similarly accumulate instructions and resources whose contribution no longer justifies keeping them. SkillProx implements a related simplification step after skill revision [57]. It measures the utility of skill sections and reference groups by removing them and rerunning validation cases. Low-utility units become candidates for deletion, demotion, or consolidation. Each proposed simplification must reduce complexity, preserve structural validity, and pass performance checks. The resulting skill becomes more compact through measured changes to its contents. The method also draws on proximal optimization by separating performance-oriented revision from subsequent complexity reduction. 

4.1.10 Ensemble Learning 

Ensemble learning combines predictions from multiple learners to use their complementary strengths [58]. In agentic systems, this connection concerns how several agents contribute to a solution and how their participation is learned. DMoA learns a router that selects agents at successive reasoning steps, combines their responses into the next step’s context, and uses a summarizer to produce the final answer [59]. Its test-time training setting first runs all candidate agents on an initial set of queries and collects predictive entropy as supervision. It then updates the router and uses the learned selection on subsequent queries. This protocol illustrates how a system can learn to coordinate an ensemble from its own outputs while keeping the underlying language models fixed. 

4.1.11 Adversarial Learning 

Adversarial learning trains interacting models with competing objectives. In generative adversarial networks (GANs), a discriminator learns to distinguish real data from generated samples, while a generator learns to produce samples that the discriminator classifies as real [60]. As the two models are updated, each learns against a changing opponent. In RSI, a related interaction can couple task solving with the search for failures in current solutions. 14 The code-generation framework ACE illustrates this interaction through a solver and an adversary [61]. The solver generates candidate programs, while the adversary generates test inputs intended to trigger execution failures. The system evaluates the programs using both existing correctness tests and the generated inputs, then fine-tunes the solver on selected programs. Execution results also provide preferences for training the adversary: tests are favored when they cause some candidates to fail while others still execute successfully. Each new round uses the updated solver and adversary, allowing failure discovery and code generation to improve through continued interaction.

4.2.1 Further Developments in Traditional Deep Learning 

We first deepen the connections established in Section 4.1, considering more specific mechanisms from traditional deep learning. 

The Lottery Ticket Hypothesis. SkillProx uses pruning to simplify skills while preserving their task performance, as discussed in Section 4.1.9. The lottery ticket hypothesis takes pruning a step further: a large network can contain a small subnetwork that, when trained from its original initial weights, reaches comparable accuracy in a similar number of iterations [62]. For RSI, the lottery ticket hypothesis suggests examining which resources an agent needs at the start of self-improvement. A system with an extensive collection of skills and code might need only a subset of its early resources to develop comparable capabilities through subsequent revisions. If such a starting point exists, it could enable agents to improve effectively with fewer resources. 

Adversarial Dropout. Dropout randomly masks units during training. Adversarial dropout develops this idea by seeking bounded changes to the mask that most increase prediction error, then training the network to cope with them [63]. SkillEvoReg already applies random masking to agent skills during execution and 15 revision [51]. This connection suggests using targeted masking to expose strong dependencies in skills and encourage alternative procedures. For example, a masking agent could identify the skill content on which a web research agent strongly depends. If hiding a template tailored to one website causes extraction to fail, it could use that disruption to encourage the system to explore other extraction procedures, such as locating information through page headings and nearby text. As the skills improve, the masking agent could seek further masks that expose weaknesses, providing new challenges for subsequent self-improvement. 

Maintaining Plasticity. Section 4.1.8 describes how past examples help retain earlier capabilities. Continual learning also requires plasticity: the ability to learn new behavior through further training. This ability can decline as a network learns successive tasks. Continual backpropagation addresses this problem by selectively reinitializing a small fraction of low-utility hidden units during training, allowing them to learn new features [64]. An agent that improves itself through parameter updates can use its own verified task solutions as training data. However, prolonged training may gradually reduce the model’s ability to learn new ways of solving tasks. Drawing on continual backpropagation, the system could reinitialize a small fraction of low-utility neurons during training, then resume training so these neurons can learn new features. This could help the agent maintain its ability to learn from new tasks across successive rounds of self-improvement. 

4.2.2 Insights from Broader Machine Learning 

Broader machine learning methods can also inspire new research ideas for RSI. We illustrate this with a concrete example below. 

Bayesian Optimization. Bayesian optimization uses a probabilistic model of previous evaluations to guide the choice of subsequent evaluations. Freeze-Thaw Bayesian Optimization extends this approach to partially completed training runs [65]. It uses a Gaussian process to predict future performance from observed learning curves and estimate the uncertainty of these predictions. This information guides decisions to continue a training run, pause it, or resume a previously paused run. An RSI system exploring several branches of self-improvement could use this mechanism to decide which branches to develop further. Following Freeze-Thaw, a lightweight Gaussian process predictor could use the performance recorded after successive self-modifications to estimate what additional rounds might achieve. For example, one branch of a web research agent might score well but make little recent progress, while another starts lower and continues to improve. Predicted performance and its uncertainty could guide which branch to continue, pause, or resume. Paused branches would retain the agent state and revision history needed to continue their self-improvement. As further revisions are evaluated, their results would update the predictor and guide the allocation of subsequent improvement rounds. 

4.2.3 Insights from Evolutionary Algorithms 

Evolutionary algorithms can likewise inspire new research ideas for RSI. We illustrate this with a concrete example below. 

Evolution of Modularity. Changing one part of a skill can disturb another part that already works. Separating procedures that can change independently could make later revisions easier. ModularRSI, for example, improves predefined harness modules separately [66]. Evolutionary experiments suggest that useful divisions can also emerge as task requirements change. Kashtan and Alon [67] repeatedly switched between goals made from different combinations of shared subgoals. The networks evolved modules that could be reused when the goal changed. For RSI, consider a research agent that initially uses one skill to retrieve webpages and produce a comparison table. Later tasks sometimes require a written report from the same webpages, and sometimes require the same table from PDF documents. During self-improvement, the updater could identify which procedures need changing in each case: a new source requires different retrieval steps, while a new output format requires different reporting steps. It could then separate these procedures into reusable skills. The agent could subsequently learn to read a new document type while retaining its report-writing skill, or improve report writing while retaining its retrieval skill. 16 5 Evaluation and Benchmarks

## 5.1 Online evaluation 

Online evaluation allows a system to update itself during task execution and use retained changes in subsequent work. It examines whether the system improves through continued use and how its performance changes as new tasks arrive. The relationships among tasks and the feedback they provide shape the opportunities for 17 improvement. To examine reuse across tasks, Evo-Memory organizes reasoning, question-answering, and interactive tasks into streams [68]. The system retrieves memory before solving a task and updates it after the interaction, using records of its execution and correctness feedback. Evaluating later tasks with the accumulated memory measures how well the system uses information from earlier interactions. The benchmark also varies task difficulty order and feedback to examine how these conditions affect learning. Tasks that share a procedure provide a more specific test of reuse. SkillFlow constructs task families around a common workflow [69]. Agents start without skills, solve the tasks sequentially, and use execution traces and feedback based on scoring rubrics to create or repair skill documents. The updated library remains available for later tasks in the family. ContinualSkillBench arranges related tasks by difficulty and opportunities for skill reuse [77]. It compares independent task execution, accumulated context, and explicit skill maintenance. These settings examine whether what is learned from one task helps solve related tasks, and how the form of retained information affects that transfer. Changing the task sequence makes transfer and retention observable. AgentStream organizes agent tasks into isolated, sequential, and interleaved domain settings [70]. This allows comparisons between learning within one domain and carrying updates across domains. PATH-Bench places recurring probe tasks among histories designed to help or interfere with them [71]. Revisiting the probes reveals whether later updates preserve earlier capabilities or cause forgetting. The sequence therefore forms part of the problem being evaluated: performance on a task depends on what the agent encountered before it. Online evaluation can also track successive attempts at an ongoing research objective. In RSIBench-Data, a research agent repeatedly revises training-data strategies for a target model [78]. A fixed training and evaluation pipeline returns results after each experiment, which inform the next revision. This setting examines how an agent uses experimental feedback to improve its research decisions over a sequence of attempts. 

## 5.2 Offline evaluation 

Offline evaluation separates improvement from testing. The system first learns or searches on training tasks, then solves held-out tasks with further self-improvement updates disabled. The evaluation asks whether the changes acquired during improvement generalize. The relationship between training and test tasks specifies the kind of transfer being examined. MemoryBench tests transfer from feedback on training tasks to separate test tasks [72]. A simulated user provides feedback on the system’s training responses, and a separate evaluation process scores its test responses. The tasks span several domains, languages, and input–output formats. This setup examines whether feedback can support learning that is useful beyond the examples that produced it. Some benchmarks explicitly construct the connection between learning and test tasks. GDPevo distributes business rules across training tasks and recombines them in held-out enterprise workflows [73]. The system must apply rules it has learned in combinations it has not practiced. EvoAgentBench links training and test tasks through shared procedural requirements [79]. These designs make the expected transfer concrete by specifying which procedures learned during improvement should help with a later test task. Offline evaluation also tests whether harness revisions improve performance on unseen tasks. HarnessOpt-Bench supplies an initial harness and feedback for revising it, then compares the selected version with the initial one on separate test tasks [74]. HarnessDev evaluates both harness construction and subsequent improvement, testing the resulting programs on unseen tasks after their code is frozen [80]. To reveal the effects of harness revision, Evo-Bench prioritizes tasks whose scores reflect differences in harness quality, then divides them into separate sets for improvement and final testing [81]. Offline evaluation also examines learning from exploration. In S3Gym, an agent explores text-based games and judges its own behavior before stricter held-out assessment. It can carry information forward through interaction history, summary memory, or parameter updates [75]. This setup tests how information acquired through exploration supports later task solving. 18 5.3 Offline-to-online evaluation 

Offline-to-online evaluation begins with resources acquired during an offline phase and allows further updates during evaluation. It examines how initial preparation supports subsequent tasks and how the system continues learning from new interactions. Both the source of the initial resources and the relationship between earlier and later tasks are therefore part of the problem setup. Agent Workflow Memory (AWM) provides an example through its combined evaluation on Mind2Web [33]. It first induces reusable web workflows from training examples, then adds workflows learned while processing test tasks. The cross-task, cross-website, and cross-domain splits vary how far those tasks depart from the training distribution. Comparing the combined setting with offline-only and online-only settings examines how previously learned workflows interact with those acquired during continued use. The memory-initialization experiments in Mem 2Evolve study a related setup [76]. The system first builds memory from a subset of benchmark tasks, then continues to use and update memory while processing the remaining tasks. Another setting initializes memory from GAIA before moving to other benchmarks. These arrangements examine how resources learned within the same task family or elsewhere support continued improvement. 

## 5.4 Toward unified evaluation 

Evaluation settings differ across studies. Online, offline, and offline-to-online evaluation allow self-improvement at different stages and assess different aspects of improvement. Even within the same mode, studies can differ in the benchmarks they use, the order of evaluation examples, and the feedback they provide. For example, what a system learns from earlier examples can help it solve later ones, while reference answers, test results, and model-generated assessments provide different information for revision. Comparing the gains reported by different methods therefore requires considering these evaluation conditions. Establishing shared benchmarks and evaluation protocols is important for RSI research. Within each evaluation mode, common task organization, feedback conditions, and scoring rules would make comparisons more informative. Reporting task performance together with the gain over the same system before improvement would further clarify the contribution of self-improvement. This common basis would help identify designs that support generalization, continued improvement, and retention of acquired capabilities. 

# 6 Conclusion 

This survey systematically reviews existing RSI methods through a unified taxonomy and examines how some of these methods draw on traditional deep learning. The taxonomy places existing methods in a shared high-dimensional design space, providing a basis for understanding and comparing them. Examining individual dimensions and their interactions can reveal limitations of current mechanisms and suggest new design directions. Through concrete examples, we explore how more specific theories and mechanisms within established learning methods can inspire new RSI research ideas, and extend the search for inspiration beyond traditional deep learning. 19 References
