Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) are increas-ingly used to automate AI research, includ-ing language-model (LM) development, of-fering a potential path toward recursive self-improvement. However, turning experimental feedback into a learned ability to propose ef-fective improvements across models faces two challenges. First, training such an ability with real feedback is expensive: reinforcement learn-ing (RL) requires evaluating tens of thousands of proposals, each potentially requiring a full model-training run. Even at ten GPU-hours per run, reward collection alone would con-sume hundreds of thousands of GPU-hours, motivating a cheaper surrogate. Second, sur-rogate feedback can become unreliable as pol-icy optimization favors overestimated propos-als when the proposal distribution shifts. To address these challenges, we propose LM2LM, which trains LLMs with RL to generate effec-tive LM improvement proposals. To provide scalable feedback, we use a research world model (RWM), a frozen LLM that predicts pro-posal gains from real experimental records pro-vided in context, as the reward model for RL. To maintain feedback reliability, we periodi-cally execute current-policy proposals ranked highly by the RWM and add their measured outcomes to its context. Across eight autore-gressive and diffusion LM development envi-ronments, real-execution evaluations on held-out tasks show that LM2LM enables Qwen3.8-27B to outperform GPT-6-sol, even when GPT-6-sol is given 300 real experimental records in context, while requiring 33% lower data-generation cost. LM2LM also generalizes to a model family unseen during training. 

1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) (Radford et al., 2018, 2019; Touvron et al., 2023) are increasingly being adopted as agents for automated AI research (AutoResearch) (Karpathy, 2026), where they pro-

Figure 1: Overview of automated LM development. 

pose ideas, implement changes, and execute exper-iments (Si et al., 2026; Novikov et al., 2025). A promising application is language-model develop-ment (LM development), where agents explore ar-chitectural, optimization, and training choices and measure their effects on model performance (Chi et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026a). These experiments generate feedback that could help LLMs learn how to improve language models. Developing this capa-bility could accelerate model development and sup-port progress toward recursive self-improvement (RSI) (Chi et al., 2026) (Fig. 1(a)). Despite these advances, deciding what to try, i.e., research judgment , remains a bottleneck for LLM-based research agents. Agents can imple-ment and execute complex experiments, yet still struggle to identify changes that yield meaning-ful improvements (Li et al., 2026; Si et al., 2026; Chi et al., 2026). In LM development, effective choices depend on the target model and its training configuration: a change that benefits one setting may not benefit another. With expensive training experiments, selecting promising changes is essen-tial to making effective use of limited experimental resources. We therefore ask: how can language models learn to make better model-improvement decisions and transfer this capability to new mod-

1arXiv:2610.23344v1 [cs.CL] 16 Oct 2026 els and training configurations? 

A natural way to improve research judgment is to learn from past experiments. Experimental records can inform an LLM’s decisions through in-context learning (ICL) (Brown et al., 2020; Novikov et al., 2025; Si et al., 2026), and can also improve predic-tions of experimental outcomes and the selection of promising proposals (Wang et al., 2026b). This suggests using experimental knowledge as feed-back for parameter learning to improve an LLM’s proposal-generation capability beyond in-context learning. However, learning from this feedback raises two key challenges (Fig. 1(b)-(c)). First, how can we obtain cheap and scalable training feedback? Training LLMs with reinforce-ment learning (RL) to generate better proposals requires feedback on numerous sampled proposals. The most direct feedback comes from executing each proposal and measuring its outcome (Si et al., 2026), but this requires costly model-training runs. For example, training a 486M-parameter model on 8B tokens takes approximately 45 B200 GPU-hours. A GRPO-style RL (Shao et al., 2024) setup with 100 updates, 32 training states per update, and 8 proposals per state would require 25,600 pro-posal evaluations. Executing these proposals at the same per-run cost would consume approximately 1,152,000 GPU-hours, costing over $16 million. This motivates low-cost surrogate feedback with-out executing every proposal. Second, how can we keep surrogate feedback reliable as learning pro-gresses? Accurate predictions on past experiments do not ensure reliable feedback throughout train-ing. As learning changes which proposals the LLM generates, the surrogate must evaluate proposals that its experimental records may not cover. RL may then favor overestimated proposals, increas-ing surrogate rewards without corresponding real gains (Gao et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2026). Ascalable learning approach must therefore keep its feedback grounded in real outcomes on the propos-als the current LLM generates. To address these challenges, we propose LM2LM, a framework that trains LLMs with RL to generate effective proposals for LM develop-ment. To obtain cheap and scalable training signals, we use a frozen LLM as a research world model (RWM) (Wang et al., 2026b) to predict experimen-tal gains relative to a reference configuration with-out executing proposals. Grounded in an initial set of real experimental records provided as in-context knowledge, the RWM supplies predicted gains for constructing RL rewards (Yu et al., 2026). To keep this feedback reliable as training progresses, we introduce on-policy calibration : we periodically execute a subset of the current policy’s proposals that the RWM ranks highly and incorporate their measured outcomes into its in-context knowledge. This targets real execution at proposals favored by RL, where overestimation can misdirect learning, and helps maintain prediction reliability as LLMs’ proposals change. Our contributions are: (i) Method. We propose LM2LM, which trains LLMs with RL using RWM-based rewards grounded in real records and cali-brated through targeted real execution; (ii) Exper-iments. Extensive real-execution experiments on held-out environments show that LM2LM enables Qwen3.8-27B to outperform frontier models such as GPT-6-sol and surpass baselines leveraging past experimental records at lower data-generation cost; (iii) Analysis. We show that RWM-based feedback provides informative rankings of proposal gains, while on-policy calibration mitigates overestima-tion and largely preserves ranking quality as train-ing progresses, unlike a fixed-record RWM that eventually performs worse than random. 

2 Related Work 

AutoResearch with LLM Agents. AutoResearch aims to automate empirical AI research through iterative idea generation, experimental implemen-tation, execution, and analysis. Early work shows LLM agents’ ability to generate research ideas and conduct end-to-end experiments (Baek et al., 2025; Si et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2024; Weng et al., 2026), with benchmarks evaluating these capabilities in machine-learning engineering and AI R&D (Huang et al., 2024; Chan et al., 2025; Wijk et al., 2025; Chi et al., 2026). To improve research outcomes, some methods iterate on candidate solutions with execution feedback while keeping the underlying agent fixed (Jiang et al., 2025; Novikov et al., 2025; Karpathy, 2026). Others instead train the agent on execution feedback, for machine-learning engineer-ing (Yang et al., 2026a) and LLM model develop-ment (Si et al., 2026). However, these methods rely on costly real execution to obtain training sig-nals, limiting learning at scale. In contrast, we train LLMs with feedback from a world model that predicts experimental outcomes, enabling scalable learning with fewer real executions. Full details of related works are in Appendix A. 2Figure 2: Overview of LM2LM. 

3 Methodology 

In this section, we present LM2LM. We first for-malize AutoResearch for LM development and mo-tivate RL-based proposer training (Sec. 3.1). To provide scalable feedback, we use a frozen LLM as a research world model (RWM) to predict ex-perimental gains without executing proposals, and use its predictions as RL rewards (Sec. 3.2). To maintain reliable feedback as the proposer evolves, we periodically execute high-rated proposals from the current policy and incorporate their measured outcomes into the RWM’s context (Sec. 3.3). An overview of LM2LM is shown in Fig. 2.

3.2 Scalable Feedback: Research World Model as the Verifier 

RLVR typically relies on inexpensive verification, e.g., checking the correctness of a mathematical an-swer (Cobbe et al., 2021). However, directly apply-ing RLVR to AutoResearch for LM development is 3computationally expensive, as evaluating each pro-posal requires training the target model to measure its experimental gain. Since RL samples numerous proposals across research states and training up-dates, obtaining execution-based rewards at scale becomes prohibitively costly. To address this chal-lenge, we use a research world model (RWM) to predict experimental gains without executing every proposal, and train the proposer with DAPO on rewards built from these predictions. 

Research World Model. An RWM predicts the outcomes of AI research experiments without ex-ecuting them (Wang et al., 2026b). We instantiate it as a frozen LLM W . To improve prediction reli-ability, we ground it in real experimental records. Specifically, we collect N0 experimental records from training tasks to form the initial record set M0.Each record (e, a, g ) contains a task, an executed proposal, and its measured gain. Formally, given a task e, proposal a, and experimental records M , the RWM predicts the experimental gain ˆg(e, a ; M ) as 

ˆg(e, a ; M ) = W (e, a ; M ), (4) where M is provided as in-context evidence. Thus, evaluating a proposal requires only an LLM call rather than a model-training run.

4 Experiments 

In this section, we first compare LM2LM with base-lines on proposal quality and data-generation cost (Sec. 4.2) and evaluate its generalization to an un-seen model family (Sec. 4.3). We then conduct ablation studies to understand the contribution of on-policy calibration (Sec. 4.4) and analyze why RWM feedback can guide RL and how calibration sustains it (Sec. 4.5). 

4.1 Experimental Setup Environments. We construct language-model training environments spanning four autoregres-sive (AR) families: OLMo-3 (Olmo et al., 2025), Qwen3 (Yang et al., 2025), NanoChat (Karpathy, 2025), and Marin (Hall et al., 2025); and four dif-fusion families: E2D2-SW, E2D2 (Arriola et al., 2026), MDM (Nie et al., 2025), and BD3LM (Ar-riola et al., 2025). Each task specifies a model fam-ily, model size, training budget, and reference train-ing recipe. Each experiment trains the target model from scratch. Further details are in Appendix C.1. 

Data Construction. We split the tasks into 40 training and 10 held-out tasks. We curate and exe-cute 300 proposals from a library of training-recipe mechanisms on the training tasks, yielding 300 ex-perimental records. Of these, 100 serve as the ini-tial records M0 of LM2LM, grounding the RWM. We construct initial RL training states s = ( e, h )

with histories h containing 0–8 records of task e

from M0, expanded during RL (Eq. (7)). For eval-uation, we construct 60 AR and 18 diffusion held-out research states using only real experimental histories. More details are in Appendix C.2. 

Baselines. We compare LM2LM with training-free and fine-tuning-based methods. (i) Training-free proposers : We evaluate LM2LM’s base model and a frontier LLM (GPT-6-sol) in both zero-shot and in-context learning (ICL) settings. For ICL, we use the above 300 experimental records as in-context examples. (ii) Fine-tuned proposers : SFT fine-tunes the base model on 300 (state, proposal) pairs constructed from the same records, and RFT-Real applies rejection-sampling fine-tuning (Yuan et al., 2023), keeping only the pairs whose proposals have positive measured gains. Both use the same base model and builder as LM2LM.

We measure experimental gains using val-idation bits per byte (BPB) for AR models and negative log-likelihood (NLL) for diffusion mod-els. We report Avg@4, Best@4, negative-gain rate (Neg.), and pairwise win rates based on Avg@4. We use Qwen3.8-27B (Qwen Team, 2026) as the proposer backbone, GPT-6-sol (OpenAI, 2026b) as the RWM, and GPT-6-astra (OpenAI, 2026a) as the builder, both at high reasoning effort. 4.5 In-depth Dissection of LM2LM 

We further analyze why RWM feedback can guide RL and how calibration keeps it reliable. For this analysis, we extend training from 96 to 128 steps with a fourth calibration of LM2LM (236 real runs) and compare it with the static variant whose RWM keeps M0 throughout; each variant is scored by the RWM it is trained with. 

Why Can RWM Feedback Guide RL? We ex-amine the alignment between RWM-predicted and real-execution feedback by measuring the cosine similarity of group-relative advantages and the within-state pairwise ranking accuracy over four executed proposals per held-out AR research state. As shown in Figs. 5(a) and (b), LM2LM maintains advantage cosine of 0.50–0.71 and pairwise accu-racy of 0.61–0.69, above chance levels of 0 and 0.5. Since DAPO optimizes group-relative advantages, this indicates that the RWM provides informative relative feedback for policy optimization. 

How Does On-Policy Calibration Help? To un-derstand the gains from calibration in Sec. 4.4, we examine how the feedback of the static vari-ant evolves as RL changes the proposer’s outputs (Fig. 5). We observe that (i) Calibration maintains reliable relative feedback. As shown in Fig. 5(a), the static RWM’s advantage cosine declines from 0.69 at step 16 to −0.42 at step 128, so its pre-dicted advantages become negatively aligned with real ones, whereas LM2LM keeps it positive. Like-wise, its pairwise accuracy drops from 0.72 to 0.37, below chance (0.5), while LM2LM maintains 0.61 (Fig. 5(b)). We further observe that scoring the same LM2LM proposals with M0 instead of cali-brated records lowers pairwise accuracy from 0.61 to 0.46 at step 96 (Appendix I.2). These results in-dicate that on-policy calibration keeps the predicted advantages that DAPO optimizes aligned with real ones. (ii) Calibration limits reward overestimation. 

As shown in Fig. 5(c), the predicted–real reward gap of the static RWM rises sharply after step 64, reaching about 2.0 at step 96, whereas LM2LM’s gap stays below 1 and decreases from 0.85 at step 96 to 0.17 at step 128 after the fourth calibra-tion. Thus, calibration also keeps predicted rewards close to real ones, mitigating reward overoptimiza-tion (Gao et al., 2023). For example, our case studies in Appendix I.3 show that the initial RWM overestimates a NanoChat optimizer change (pre-dicted +0 .7 to +1 .25 vs. real −1.46 ), so the static variant raises its share of NanoChat training propos-als from 30% to 79% by step 64. In contrast, the first calibration of LM2LM adds its real outcomes to the records, and the share falls to 8%. More metrics and case studies are in Appendix I. 

5 Conclusion 

We presented LM2LM, which trains LLMs with RL to generate effective proposals for LM develop-ment, using a frozen LLM grounded in real experi-mental records as an RWM and keeping it reliable through on-policy calibration. Extensive experi-ments across autoregressive and diffusion LM envi-ronments show that LM2LM enables Qwen3.8-27B to outperform frontier models such as GPT-6-sol and achieve higher gains than baselines leveraging past experimental records at lower data-generation cost. Our analysis shows that RWM-based feed-back provides informative rankings of proposal gains, while on-policy calibration mitigates re-ward overestimation and preserves ranking qual-ity as training progresses. These findings high-light RWM-guided RL as a promising paradigm for learning research judgment from experimental feedback, offering a potential path toward RSI. 86 Limitations 

Our study focuses on proposal-level improvements in LM development rather than end-to-end research campaigns. Although the RWM provides informa-tive rankings and substantially reduces the need for real execution during RL, its predictions remain imperfect, and on-policy calibration still requires a limited number of additional model-training runs. Future work could extend our framework to broader research environments, including LM post-training, and evaluate its effectiveness in longer, end-to-end research campaigns. 

7 Ethics Statement 

This work studies the use of LLMs to automate research on language-model development. Such systems may help reduce the cost of experimenta-tion and accelerate scientific progress, but they also introduce risks, including unreliable experimental conclusions, increased computational resource con-sumption, and the potential misuse of automated model-improvement capabilities. Our framework evaluates proposals in controlled training environ-ments and uses real experimental outcomes to cali-brate surrogate feedback. Nevertheless, predicted rewards and experimental gains do not guarantee the safety or broader usefulness of proposed model changes. We therefore emphasize the importance of human oversight, independent evaluation, and appropriate safeguards when applying automated research systems to more capable models or higher-stakes settings.

12 A More Details of Related Works 

A.1 World Models for LLM Agents. 

A world model predicts the consequences of an agent’s actions, enabling planning or learning with-out interacting with the real environment (Sutton, 1991; Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2023). Recent work uses LLMs as world mod-els for reasoning (Hao et al., 2023), web naviga-tion (Chae et al., 2025; Gu et al., 2024), text-based simulation (Wang et al., 2024), and agent train-ing in synthetic environments (Chen et al., 2026). In AutoResearch, concurrent work WMRL (Yang et al., 2026b) uses a frozen LLM as the world model to predict execution outcomes as RL rewards and rescales them with a monotone mapping fitted on real executions at every training step. In contrast, LM2LM uses a frozen LLM as the world model that learns research knowledge from real experi-mental records in context (Wang et al., 2026b), and improves its predictions on the current policy’s pro-posals by adding the outcomes of a few top-rated ones to these records. 

A.2 Reinforcement Learning for LLMs 

Reinforcement learning (RL) (Shao et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2026a; Wu et al., 2026) has become an effective approach to improving LLM capabilities, particularly through reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) (Lambert et al., 2025). Methods such as GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and DAPO (Yu et al., 2026) optimize LLM policies using group-relative feedback without a learned value function. While RLVR typically relies on inexpensive verification, such as checking mathe-matical answers or executing code, LM develop-ment requires costly model-training experiments to measure proposal quality. Recent work applies RL to automated AI research using execution feed-back (Yang et al., 2026a; Si et al., 2026), but obtain-ing rewards through real execution at every training step limits scalability. In contrast, LM2LM uses RWM-predicted experimental gains as RL rewards, while periodically calibrating the RWM with se-lected real executions to maintain feedback relia-bility. 

A.3 Self-Evolving LLMs and Agents 

Self-evolving methods improve a model or agent with data or feedback it generates itself. One line trains LLMs on self-generated solutions that are filtered or rewarded by verifiers or by the model itself (Zelikman et al., 2022; Gulcehre et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2024). Another line lets agents mod-ify their own code, prompts, or workflows and keeps the changes that improve benchmark perfor-mance (Lin et al., 2026b; Yin et al., 2025; Robeyns et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2026c,d). These methods rely on feedback that is cheap to obtain, such as answer checking, code execution, or benchmark scores. In LM development, measuring a sin-gle proposal requires a full model-training run. LM2LM addresses this setting by learning from RWM-predicted gains and calibrating the RWM with a small number of real executions, and it tar-gets the ability to improve LM development, which recursive self-improvement ultimately requires.

Each family shares an architecture, training-data distribution, and evaluation metric across its tasks.

• OLMo-3 (Olmo et al., 2025): OLMo-3 small models trained on samples of Dolma 3 and eval-uated by mean bits per byte (BPB) across ten downstream tasks (ARC-Challenge, ARC-Easy, HellaSwag, four MMLU subsets, HumanEval, MBPP, and MATH-500). • Qwen3 (Yang et al., 2025): The official Qwen3 ar-chitecture and tokenizer. Training data (Dolma 3) and evaluation (ten-task BPB) are shared with OLMo-3. • NanoChat (Karpathy, 2025): depth-parameterized transformers trained on ClimbMix and evaluated by BPB on a held-out ClimbMix validation shard. • Marin (Hall et al., 2025): Marin architectures with the same training data and evaluation as OLMo-3. 

Diffusion Families. All four families are trained on SlimPajama with a 2,048-token context and evaluated by the negative log-likelihood of true to-kens accumulated along the model’s greedy, most-confident-first unmasking order, measured in nats per token. • E2D2 (Arriola et al., 2026): an encoder-decoder masked diffusion model with a clean-token en-coder and a cross-attention decoder for noisy to-kens. Noise levels are sampled per block, yield-ing block-autoregressive modeling. • E2D2-SW (Wang et al., 2026a): This is an E2D2 variant with a token-causal encoder and a decoder trained on packed sliding windows of blocks but evaluated on non-overlapping blocks. • BD3LM (Arriola et al., 2025): a block diffusion model using a single network over concatenated clean and noisy sequences with block-structured attention. • MDM (Nie et al., 2025): a full-sequence bidi-rectional masked diffusion model with one noise level per sequence; it appears only among the training tasks and has no held-out task.

Validation Metrics. We measure model perfor-mance using: • BPB (Bits per Byte): Average number of bits needed to encode each byte of text, enabling com-parisons across tokenizers (Gao et al., 2020). We use it for AR models, evaluated on held-out text as specified per family (Appendix C.1). • NLL (Negative Log-Likelihood): Negative log-probability assigned to validation sequences. For diffusion models, we compute the exact nega-tive log-likelihood of the true tokens accumu-lated along the model’s greedy, most-confident-first within-block unmasking order, with each revealed position set to the true token, reported in nats per token. Lower values indicate better model performance. 

Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate proposal quality using: • Avg@4 / Best@4: Mean / maximum real gain among four proposals per state, averaged equally across states. • Win rate: Fraction of states where one method achieves higher Avg@4 than another, with ties counted as 0.5. • Neg.: Fraction of proposals with negative real gains. Experimental gains are reported in 10 −3 BPB (AR) and 10 −2 nats per token (diffusion).

E Additional Implementation Details 

E.1 Models and Prompts. 

The proposer’s backbone is Qwen3.8-27B. The RWM, GPT-6-sol, is queried with a single API re-quest per proposal at high reasoning effort, without tools. The builder, GPT-6-astra, runs as a Codex agent (OpenAI, 2025) ( codex exec ) at high rea-soning effort. Tables 13, and 14 list the prompts of RWM, and builder, respectively. 

E.2 RL Training 

We use DAPO with asymmetric clipping ( ϵlow =0.2, ϵhigh = 0 .28 ), a token-level policy-gradient loss, and no KL or entropy regularization. For dynamic sampling, we sample 277 states and re-tain 256 per rollout; importance-sampling ratios are clipped at 2.0. Proposals are sampled at tem-perature 1.0 with a maximum of 20,000 generated tokens. Each rollout is followed by eight policy updates with a global batch size of 256. We use Adam ( β1 = 0 .9, β2 = 0 .98 ), weight decay 0.1, and an initial learning rate of 10 −6, multiplied by 0.7 after each training segment. 18 E.3 On-Policy Calibration 

The candidates Cu in Eq. (9) are the proposals sampled by the current proposer on training states whose outcomes are not yet in Mu; in practice, we spread the selected top-rated proposals across training tasks and states. Each selected proposal is executed once on its training task, and the three calibrations add B0 = 31 , B1 = 38 , and B2 = 31 

records. Calibration outcomes enter only the RWM records, not the histories of training states. 

E.4 SFT and RFT-Real 

Each SFT pair uses a record’s executed proposal as the target, and a state of the record’s own task whose history contains 0–7 other records of that task from the 300 records, chosen to diversify his-tories across pairs. RFT-Real keeps the 114 pairs whose measured gain is positive (76 AR and 38 diffusion). We train separate LoRA adapters for AR and diffusion tasks with the configuration in Table 12, and sample proposals with the same de-coding settings as LM2LM. 

F Additional Main Results 

F.1 Per-Family Results 

Tables 7 and 8 break the results down by model family, and gains vary across families: • LM2LM attains the highest Avg@4 and Best@4 on OLMo-3, Qwen3, E2D2, and BD3LM and the highest Best@4 on Marin, but falls behind GPT-6-sol-ICL in Avg@4 on Marin and E2D2-SW and behind Base-ICL in Best@4 on E2D2-SW. • Although its AR advantage is largest on Qwen3, LM2LM still achieves the highest AR Avg@4 and Best@4 when excluding Qwen3 (4.42 and 10.68, vs. at most 3.09 and 7.58). • On NanoChat, headroom is limited: no method improves by more than about 2σe (Best@4 at most 1.23 × 10 −3 BPB), and GPT-6-sol-ICL is the only method with a positive Avg@4. These results show that LM2LM can bring a smaller open-weight model to frontier-level perfor-mance in proposing effective LM improvements. 

F.2 Statistical Robustness 

We express experimental gains in units of task-specific seed noise σe, defined as the standard deviation of the reference configuration’s valida-tion loss over three runs. Table 9 reports Avg@4 and Best@4 in units of σe, the fraction of propos-als with gains exceeding 2σe, and Avg@4 rela-tive to the reference loss. On AR tasks, LM2LM achieves an Avg@4 of 7.29 σe, compared with at most 4.91 σe for the baselines. Moreover, 50 .8% 

of its proposals exceed 2σe, compared with 22 .5% 

for Base and 40 .0% for GPT-6-sol-ICL, while its Avg@4 corresponds to a 1.28% reduction in refer-ence loss.

We use the following metrics, with 95% bootstrap intervals over states (2,000 resamples): • Advantage cosine : the cosine similarity between the predicted and real advantages, concatenated over all states. • Pairwise accuracy : over all pairs of proposals of a state whose real rewards differ, the fraction whose predicted order agrees with their real order, where a pair with equal predicted rewards counts as 0.5; pairs are pooled over states, so random rewards attain 0.5. • Sign agreement : the fraction of proposals with nonzero ˆAi and Ai whose signs agree. • Wrong-reinforcement rate : Pr( Ai < 0 | ˆAi > 0) ,the fraction of positively reinforced proposals that perform below their group’s real mean. • Top-1 hit rate : over states whose real rewards differ, the fraction in which the proposal with the highest predicted reward also has the highest real reward. • Real advantage of reinforced proposals : the mean Ai over proposals with ˆAi > 0.We also report the mean predicted and real rewards and their difference (Fig. 5(c)). 

Results. Fig. 9 complements the advantage align-ment and ranking results of Sec. 4.5 by examin-ing whether individual proposals are reinforced in the correct direction. Early in training, the static 21 RWM provides feedback comparable to that of LM2LM, but its reliability deteriorates as the pro-poser evolves. From step 16 to step 64, its sign agreement drops from 0.78 to 0.52 and its wrong-reinforcement rate rises from 18% to 46%, reach-ing 0.43 and 55% at step 128. In contrast, the sign agreement of LM2LM is 0.68, 0.65, and 0.62 at steps 16, 64, and 128, and its wrong-reinforcement rate stays between 30% and 36% at these steps. As a result, the proposals that the static RWM reinforces have a negative mean real advantage from step 96 onward ( −0.31 ), i.e., RL increasingly favors proposals that underperform their group, whereas those reinforced by LM2LM remain posi-tive (0.18 to 0.59). Fig. 11 shows the same trend at the level of individual proposals. 

I.2 Effect of Updating Records on Identical Proposals 

The comparison in Sec. 4.5 involves two proposers that diverge during training. To isolate the effect of the records, we score the same proposals of LM2LM with the initial records M0 and with the calibrated records. With M0, the pairwise accuracy drops to 0.49 at step 64, 0.46 at step 96, and 0.44 at step 128, whereas with the calibrated records it is 0.64 with M2 at step 64, 0.61 with M3 at step 96, and 0.61 with M4 at step 128 (Fig. 10). Since the proposals are identical, these differences come from the records alone. 

I.3 Case Studies Family-dependent optimizer changes. The RWM rewards an orthogonalized-momentum optimizer with +1 .0 to +2 .0 on OLMo-3 and Qwen3 train-ing tasks but with −2 to −3 on NanoChat. The proposer follows this feedback: the share of the change in its OLMo-3 proposals rises from 7% to 78%, while on NanoChat it drops to 0%. Real execution confirms both decisions: on held-out states, the change improves 93% of the OLMo-3 and 100% of the Qwen3 proposals that apply it, by up to 21 .6 × 10 −3 BPB on a single OLMo-3 state, whereas on NanoChat, where Base proposes it, it loses 44 × 10 −3 BPB on average.
