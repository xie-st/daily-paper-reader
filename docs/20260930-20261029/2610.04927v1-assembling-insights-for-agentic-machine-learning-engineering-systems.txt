PENGYU NIE, University of Waterloo, Canada Agentic machine learning engineering (MLE) is an emerging AI4SE application, targeting complex machine learning tasks and taking a step toward the recursive self-improvement of AI systems. Recent agentic MLE systems highlight the importance of collecting and leveraging insights from closely-related MLE tasks: some systems condition code generation on expert domain knowledge, which is implicitly curated from peer MLE tasks; some systems have a loop of solving an MLE task, gathering validation feedback, and refining their prompts, skills, or memory to benefit future tasks. However, collecting and injecting insights is ad hoc, and existing evaluations do not control the sources of insights, leaving data leakage a threat to validity. To close these gaps, we systematically study how insights assembled from closely-related MLE tasks affect an agentic MLE system, and how their effect depends on the type of insights and the way they are injected. We first collect a new benchmark, MLE-InsightBench, consisting of 160 Kaggle competitions clustered into 12 domains. MLE-InsightBench is designed to keep target task information out of the insights: one competition from each domain is held out as a target task, and only the remaining 148 competitions, none of which can have been influenced by the target tasks (e.g., as a later edition of the same series or by reusing their solutions), serve as source competitions. We then design and implement an insight assembly agent, MLE-InsightForge, that explores the source competitions, their top human-written solutions, and writeups, and assembles them into three types of insights: 12 domain insights (summarizing domain-level best practices), 148 competition insights (describing competition-level winning solutions), and 16 improvement insights (detailing generic debugging and improvement strategies). The extracted insights can be injected into an agentic MLE system as skills, which the agent reads selectively on demand, or as a playbook placed upfront in its initial context. In our evaluation, the extracted insights improve the normalized private-test score of an agentic MLE system by 123.9% over the baseline without insights. Further controlled experiments show that: the three types of insights are complementary, the better injection mode (skill vs. playbook) varies across tasks, with skills being more cost-effective overall, and the improvements from insights generalize across backend LLMs. 

1 Introduction 

Machine learning engineering (MLE) is the end-to-end work of turning a dataset and a modeling objective into an executable machine learning (ML) solution, including data inspection, model selection, validation design, debugging, and compute allocation. ML solutions are typically written in Python, as Jupyter notebooks or scripts [19, 51], and the SE community has studied them as software artifacts with distinct engineering challenges, such as data management, code quality, and reproducibility [1, 31, 46, 51]. This makes ML solutions a natural target of LLM-based coding agents, but also a challenging one. Existing code generation benchmarks, including those for data science code, check a generated function, notebook cell, or patch against tests or reference outputs [30, 37, 70]. An ML solution, in contrast, has no test oracle: its quality is a metric value on unseen data, which can only be estimated by executing long-running training code and improved by repeatedly revising the solution. Agentic MLE is an emerging application of coding agents in which LLMs perform this work by iteratively writing, executing, and refining code [7, 27, 29]. Agentic MLE systems target complex

ML tasks, such as Kaggle competitions, and are a step toward the recursive self-improvement of AI systems, since a system that can engineer ML solutions can, in principle, contribute to building better AI systems [7]. Evolution has been rapid: on MLE-bench [7], the fraction of competitions in which the best system earns a medal grew from 17% in late 2024 to over 60% in early 2026. Recent agentic MLE systems highlight the importance of collecting and leveraging insights from closely-related MLE tasks. Some systems generate code based on expert domain knowledge, which is implicitly curated from peer MLE tasks, either by searching the web for state-of-the-art models and winning solutions of similar tasks [8, 50] or by drawing on knowledge bases synthesized from open-source repositories, competition platforms, and competition discussions [12, 13, 48]. Other systems have a loop of solving an MLE task, gathering validation feedback, and refining their prompts, skills, or memory to benefit future tasks [18, 73, 77]. However, in these systems, the process of collecting and injecting insights is ad hoc: each system fixes one design, without a controlled comparison of the alternatives. Each system collects insights from different sources (web search, curated knowledge bases, or its own past runs), represents them differently, and injects them into the agent’s context in its own way. The ablation studies in these work typically remove the entire insight source, e.g., the knowledge base or the cache of prior wisdom [12, 77], which shows that insights help as a whole but leaves open which types of insights contribute and how they should be injected. Moreover, existing evaluations of these systems do not control for the sources of insights, which leaves data leakage as a threat to validity: the sources are chosen by each system rather than fixed by the benchmark, so whether they exclude the target tasks is left to each system and rarely verifiable. For example, web search at solving time [8, 50] can retrieve the winning writeups of the very competition being evaluated, and knowledge bases built from competition platforms may contain its solutions; even when the benchmark competitions are excluded by name [68, 77], closely-related editions of the same competition series can remain. Such concerns were raised on the MLE-bench leaderboard [62], which has since paused new submissions to ensure fair and comparable results. To bridge these gaps, we systematically study how insights assembled from closely-related MLE tasks affect agentic MLE systems, and how their effect depends on the type of insights and the way they are injected. The main contributions of this work are threefold: 

• Benchmark . We collect MLE-InsightBench, a benchmark of 160 Kaggle competitions clustered into 12 domains, that provides a controlled environment for studying insights: its held-out split makes the sources of insights explicit and keeps target task information out of them. 

• Technique . We design and implement MLE-InsightForge, an agent that assembles evidence-based domain insights, competition insights, and improvement insights from human-written solutions and writeups, and injects them into agentic MLE systems as skills or playbooks. 

• Experiments . We systematically evaluate the impact of assembled insights on an agentic MLE system, including their overall effectiveness, the contributions of the three types of insights, the effectiveness and cost of the two injection modes, and the generalizability across backend LLMs. 

2 Background 2.1 Agentic MLE Systems 

An MLE task asks for an executable ML solution rather than only a fitted model: it involves un-derstanding the data and the metric, designing a validation protocol, implementing preprocessing and training, diagnosing failures, and producing an artifact that satisfies a submission contract. These decisions are coupled: for example, the validation split must reflect the deployment distri-bution, while model and feature choices must respect runtime and memory limits. Competitions on Kaggle [32] expose MLE tasks through a common interface consisting of a task description, datasets, an evaluation metric, a compute environment, and a submission format. Figure 2 shows an example Kaggle competition, Jigsaw – Agile Community Rules Classification [60], which asks whether a Reddit comment violates a given rule, including rules unseen in training, together with a solution developed by an agentic MLE system. The solution prepares features from the training data, validates the model by holding out one rule at a time to mimic the unseen rules, retrains on all training data, and writes the predictions for the test data in the submission format. 2.2 Insights in Agentic MLE Systems 

We use insight to denote a reusable, natural-language statement about how to solve MLE tasks, derived from tasks other than the one being solved. Recent systems obtain insights in two ways: 

• External knowledge: Insights are curated from external sources, such as solutions of similar MLE tasks on open-source repositories and competition platforms, API documentations, and forum dis-cussions. Some techniques retrieve them on the fly, typically via web search (e.g., MLE-STAR [50] and MARS [8]); others build a knowledge base offline which is used for retrieval-augmented gen-eration (RAG) at solving time (e.g., DS-Agent [20], MLEvolve [13], AutoMLGen [12], KAPSO [48], AIBuildAI-2 [73], and FM Agent [38]). 

• Self-improvement across tasks: Insights are distilled from the system’s own runs on previous tasks, usually generated by the agent itself or a separate surrogate agent. Examples include the accumulated memory of Agent K [18], the “prior wisdom” cache of ML-Master 2.0 [77], and the takeaways of completed runs of AIBuildAI-2 [73] (which augments its external knowledge base).

Regardless of their source, insights reach the agent as text in its context, and systems differ in when this happens. In the skill mode [2], only compact metadata of each insight (a name and a description) is placed in the context, and the agent loads the full text on demand, a design known as progressive disclosure. In the playbook mode [72], the insights are placed directly in the initial context as a playbook; unlike a human-written prompt, a playbook is generated and maintained by LLM agents. Skills save context but depend on the agent retrieving the right insight, whereas playbooks make every insight available but consume context even for irrelevant insights. 

2.3 Data Leakage in Evaluating Agentic MLE Systems 

An evaluation of an agentic MLE system suffers from data leakage when the system has access, during solving, to information about the target task that would not be available when solving a genuinely new task, most notably the task’s own winning solutions. This differs from train–test leakage in a generated solution, which some systems check [13, 50], and from contamination of the backend LLM’s pre-training data, which several papers acknowledge as a limitation [50, 63]. Insights are a direct channel for such leakage, because their sources are usually the same public competition materials from which target tasks are drawn. Web search at solving time is not reported to be restricted to materials of other tasks [8, 50], and knowledge bases built from competition platforms typically do not report whether the target tasks were excluded [12, 13]. Some work did exclude the target tasks by competition name [68, 77], which does not cover later editions of the same competition series. For example, MLE-InsightBench contains two AI Mathematical Olympiad: Progress Prize 1 [67] and Progress Prize 2 [16]; if the first were a target task, excluding it by name would still admit the second, whose description states that it builds upon the first and one of whose gold-medal notebooks reuses code from the winning solution of the first. Cross-task memory raises a related concern about the order of tasks. Accumulating experience over a sequence of tasks, i.e., online learning, is not leakage by itself, as it mirrors how practitioners gain experience over time. However, when the memory keeps accumulating across the target tasks themselves, a task may be solved after another task that it influenced. Avoiding leakage thus requires a chronological or causal ordering of the tasks. These practices have been raised as a fairness concern on the MLE-bench leaderboard [62], which is one of the reasons that the leaderboard has paused new submissions. Contamination-free benchmarks in code generation address a similar problem by evaluating only on problems released after the model’s training cutoff [28]; MLE-InsightBench (§3) applies a similar principle to insight sources by using only competitions that cannot have been influenced by the target tasks.

Domain clustering . We group the candidate competitions by clustering their natural-language descriptions (overview and dataset description), and then manually assign a human-readable label to each cluster. We embed each description with harrier-oss-v1-27b [45], a text embedding model, by instructing it to cluster competitions by ML task formulation and application domain. Leakage control . MLE-InsightBench controls the sources of insights at three points. First, the materials of the target tasks (datasets, descriptions, writeups, and solutions) are excluded from insight assembly, so no assembled insight can be derived from a target task’s own winning solutions. Second, because the split only requires the source competitions to be causal non-descendants of the target task, earlier editions of a recurring competition series (e.g., March Machine Learning Mania) remain legitimate source competitions, as they would be for a practitioner. Third, the agentic MLE system under evaluation is not given web-search tools and receives only the public data of the target task; the private-test inputs are substituted only when the final solution is graded (same as how Kaggle grades submissions), and the private-test labels are never exposed. 

4 Methodology 4.1 MLE-InsightForge: Insight Assembly Agent 

MLE-InsightForge explores the descriptions, datasets, top human-written solutions, and writeups of the source competitions and assembles three types of insights from them. Separating what 

insights are assembled from how they are injected (§4.2) lets us vary the two independently. 

4.1.1 Agent Architecture. MLE-InsightForge (Figure 4(a)) is a ReAct-style agent [69], using 

gpt-5.6-sol-pro as its backend LLM during our study. Each run of the agent performs one assembly task, specified by the instructions of its stage and the list of source files it must read. The agent’s tools read files and notebooks, inspect datasets, run targeted analyses in a sandboxed 

, Vol. 1, No. 1, Article . Publication date: October 2026. 8 Bihui Jin, Yinxi Li, Kaiyuan Wang, and Pengyu Nie 

container (e.g., checking a preprocessing step or a validation split claimed in a writeup, without rerunning the solution end-to-end), and read the materials linked from writeups, such as web pages, PDFs, code repositories, images, and videos. 

Leakage prevention . The source materials are mounted read-only in the agent’s workspace, which contains only the source competitions and not the target tasks. Because some URLs in the source materials may also link to the target tasks, web access goes through a blacklist of 1,985 URL patterns, which we extract from the descriptions, writeups, and solution notebooks of the target tasks; links to Kaggle or GitHub the competition’s or repository’s all sub-pages, while general documentation and tutorial sites (e.g., library documentation and Wikipedia) are manually exempted. 

Evidence-based submission . The agent submits each insight through a validated submission interface, which rejects the submission until the agent has read every required source in full, otherwise returns the incomplete insight to the agent for repair. To minimize hallucination, the interface also requires an evidence record for every insight, listing the original source passages it is based on; this ensures that every insight can be traced back to its original source. 

Assembly stages . Figure 4(b) shows the four stages in which MLE-InsightForge runs. It first assembles one competition insight per source competition and then synthesizes one domain insight per domain from the competition insights of that domain. Independently of these two types, it assembles raw improvement insights from the writeups and consolidates them by clustering. 

4.1.2 Insight Types. MLE-InsightForge assembles 148 competition insights, 12 domain insights, and 16 improvement insights, at a total API cost of $903 USD at the list prices of gpt-5.6-sol-pro 

($565, $22, and $317 USD for the three types, respectively). 

Competition insights . MLE-InsightForge first reads the description to identify the objective, data, metric, and constraints, and then reads every collected writeup in full. It consults the solution notebooks to disambiguate implementation details and to check that a technique claimed in a writeup is actually present, and inspects the datasets to understand their characteristics and to check claims about them. The resulting insight is organized around the decisions that distinguished the winning solutions and states the conditions under which each decision was made, rather than reproducing a particular solution, since the insight is used for other tasks, to which a complete solution tailored to the source competition’s data and metric does not transfer. 

Domain insights . A target task has no insight of its own, but it shares task properties with the other competitions in its domain. MLE-InsightForge reads the descriptions and competition insights of all source competitions in the domain and reconciles recommendations that recur, complement each other, or conflict. It replaces competition-specific prescriptions with conditional guidance; for example, it associates a validation strategy with grouped subjects or temporal deployment rather than as universally applicable. Improvement insights . While the other two types describe the decisions behind the final solutions, improvement in-sights capture how their authors diagnosed and fixed prob-lems along the way, by connecting an observed problem (e.g., a validation split that does not match the deployment setting, or an inference pipeline that exceeds the runtime budget) to the resolution that fixed it. For each retained writeup, it then assembles standalone insights, keeping only those whose outcome is directly reported in the writeup; this

step yields 1,068 raw improvement insights. Because many improvement strategies recur across competitions, exposing all of them individually would make them costly to discover and highly redundant. We therefore cluster the raw improvement insights by their descriptions, using the same approach as Domain Clustering (§3), and let MLE-InsightForge synthesize each cluster into one consolidated insight under the same evidence constraints. Specifically, we embed the text of each raw insight with harrier-oss-v1-27b , using an instruction to identify the pre-diction task type, data modality, and application domain that the insight targets, and cluster the embeddings with UMAP (15 dimensions, 20 neighbors) and HDBSCAN (minimum cluster size 20, minimum samples 5); we select these hyperparameters by a grid search for clusters that are stable across random seeds, align with the data modality and solution stage of the insights, and cover most insights. A manifest records the member insights and evidence of each cluster, so that every improvement insight remains traceable to its raw ones. 

4.1.3 Insight Representation. A competition insight or domain insight consists of insight items following the trigger–action pattern: a trigger (a task or data condition that can be checked before acting), one recommended action, and a rationale for its expected effect. Triggers are phrased in terms of general task and data properties, so the agentic MLE system can skip the items that do not apply to its task; conflicting findings are kept as items with mutually exclusive triggers. The items are grouped by five aspects of an ML solution (data modality, data, model, training, and other) and ordered within each aspect by the cost of following them. An improvement insight is a single item whose trigger is the observed problem and whose action is the resolution, elaborated with its limitations, cost, and how to check whether it worked. Each insight has a name and a description; its evidence records are stored separately and enter the context only if explicitly read. 

4.2 Usage of Insights 

4.2.1 Base Agentic MLE System. The assembled insights can be injected into any agentic MLE system that accepts textual context. In our study, we inject them into a ReAct-style agentic MLE system that develops solutions in an isolated container with access to the public data of the target task, whose workflow is shown in Figure 6. The system can inspect data, edit files, execute candidate solutions, and validate submissions. Each execution is recorded as a node in a solution tree, so the system can branch from an earlier candidate when a modification fails, similar to AIDE [29]. At the deadline, the selected solution is rerun from scratch on the private-test inputs and graded outside the agent loop. Without insights, this system is the Baseline of our evaluation (§5).

Skill mode . Following the progressive-disclosure de-sign of Agent Skills [2], the initial context lists only compact metadata (name and short description) of each available insight. This mode preserves context for task observations and execution feedback, but its benefit depends on the system recognizing a relevant insight from its description and retrieving it. 

Playbook mode . Following the use of evolving playbooks as agent context [72], the full texts of all available insights are rendered directly into the system prompt before the system begins. This mode removes the retrieval decision and makes every insight immediately available, at the cost of a longer context that may contain insights irrelevant to, or inconsistent with, the target task. 

4.2.3 Application to New Competitions. The assembled insights are not tied to MLE-InsightBench: a new competition from outside it can use them in the same way as a target task. For one or a few new competitions, the only step needed is to determine the domain of each competition, either manually or by embedding its description as in Domain Clustering (§3) and assigning it to the closest cluster; the domain insight and competition insights of that domain and all improvement insights are then directly usable without further assembly. Once a new competition has ended and its writeups and solutions are available, it can be added as a source competition by an incremental update that reruns only the affected assembly tasks. MLE-InsightForge assembles the competition insight of the new competition and re-synthesizes the domain insight of its domain; if its writeups yield new raw improvement insights, they are assigned to the existing clusters, and only the improvement insights of the clusters that receive them are re-synthesized. Re-clustering is needed only when many competitions have been added, which may shift the cluster structure, or when a new competition belongs to no existing domain.

4.3 Running Example 

We illustrate the assembly and usage of insights with the competition Energy Behavior of Prosumers, the target task of the Time Series Prediction domain. The task asks for hourly forecasts of the electricity produced and consumed by Estonian customers with solar panels, given weather forecasts, energy prices, and client data such as the installed photovoltaic capacity, and is evaluated by mean absolute error (MAE). Predictions are made through a time-series API that serves the test data day by day, in which the latest revealed targets and client data lag two days behind the prediction times.

The four source competitions of the domain forecast COVID-19 cases, microbusiness density, and retail sales, and none of them involves energy. 

Insights . Figure 8a shows excerpts of the three types of insights available for this task. Three aspects of the domain insight have triggers that hold for Energy Behavior of Prosumers: 1 target lags anchored at least one horizon behind each target match the two-day delay of the revealed targets, 2 the denominator normalization matches the production that scales with the installed capacity, which grows over time, and 3 aligning external features to their publication vintage matches the two-day delay of the client data. Impact on the solutions . Over six runs each, the agentic MLE system reaches a private-test MAE of 228 ± 76.8 without insights and 64.8 ± 6.15 with insights (lower is better, since MAE measures the prediction error). Figure 8b compares the final solutions of a run without insights and a run with insights, in which all three insights are injected via the playbook mode. The solution without insights anchors its rolling target means only two hours before each prediction time and joins the client data of the same day, although the API reveals the targets and client data only two days later. Its hold-out validation on the training data cannot detect this mismatch and reports an MAE of 29.24; at prediction time, the rolling means and the capacity are missing (the client data served by the API is ignored), filled with zeros, and the private-test MAE rises to 234.15. The solution with insights makes three changes that implement the highlighted insight parts: 1 it computes its rolling means only from target lags of at least 48 hours, 2 it learns the production per unit of installed capacity and rescales its predictions by the capacity, and 3 it joins the client data as of two days earlier, both in training and from the data served by the API. Its validation MAE (31.91) is worse but reflects the deployment setting, and its private-test MAE is 57.12.

5.2 RQ1: Effectiveness of Insights 

As shown in Tables 3 and 4, injecting the assembled insights improves the normalized score from 0.302 for Baseline to 0.676 for Full-skill (123.9%) and 0.673 for Full-playbook (122.8%). 5.3 RQ2: Contribution of Insight Types 

We compare Full-playbook with the single-type ablations (last three columns of Table 3). Each type of insight alone improves the normalized score over Baseline (0.302): to 0.595 with domain insights (96.9%), 0.608 with competition insights (101.4%), and 0.611 with improvement insights (102.1%). All three remain below Full-playbook (0.673). Hence, the three types are comple-mentary rather than redundant, and their benefits accumulate when combined. The accumulation is not additive, but the combination is more robust: the best single type varies across tasks (e.g., I-playbook on Ariel Data Challenge 2025 and CSIRO Biomass, and C-playbook on PhysioNet ECG Digitization), whereas Full-playbook has a higher mean score than all three single-type conditions on 7 of the 12 tasks. They are also less consistent: their improvement is significant on only 9, 8, and 8 tasks (2, 4, and 0 after the correction), compared with 11 (10) for Full-playbook (Table 4). Figure 9(c, d) shows the validation scores of the ablation modes during development. All three single-type modes are ahead of Baseline from their first scored solution on, by similar margins in the first 100 minutes (3.0, 2.2, and 3.1 points); afterwards, C-playbook pulls ahead and ends 6.1 points above Baseline, compared with 4.4 and 3.2 points for I-playbook and D-playbook. On the five tasks graded on per-item correctness (CAFA 5, Jigsaw, March Mania, NFL, and RSNA), we also compare the private-test items that each condition predicts correctly, i.e., that at least four of its six runs predict correctly. Each type also yields private-test items that no other condition predicts correctly (Figure 10(b)): 12,345 with competition insights, 4,566 with improvement insights, and 1,238 with domain insights, compared with 1,184 for Baseline. 

Answer to RQ2: The three types of insights are complementary: each type alone improves the score, but significantly on fewer tasks and less than the combination of all three types; competition insights contribute the most to the late-stage improvements and to the predictions that no other condition gets right.

5.5 RQ4: Generalizability across LLMs 

We repeat Baseline, Full-skill, and Full-playbook using GPT-6.1-Sol , a proprietary LLM perceived to be stronger than deepseek-v4-flash-0731 , on two of the target tasks: Image Matching 2025, where the insights bring the largest gain (680.8% with Full-skill, 613.4% with Full-playbook), and POG Series S01E01, the only task with no significant improvement (7.2% with Full-skill, -24.2% with Full-playbook). Answer to RQ4: The improvements from insights generalize to GPT-6.1-Sol , a stronger proprietary LLM from a different model family: the insights improve its normalized score by 80.3% (Full-skill) and 61.9% (Full-playbook), lifting it beyond the gold-medal threshold on Image Matching 2025. Still, some MLE tasks remain challenging to LLM agents: on POG Series S01E01, neither deepseek-v4-flash-0731 nor GPT-6.1-Sol reaches the median of the human leaderboard, with or without insights. 

6 Limitations 

Task scope . MLE-InsightBench is derived from Kaggle competitions, which provide realistic end-to-end MLE tasks but do not represent every production setting. Kaggle emphasizes predictive performance under a fixed metric and submission interface, whereas deployed machine learning systems also require maintainability, monitoring, fairness, and privacy. 

Domain construction . The domains of MLE-InsightBench are induced by clustering competition descriptions. Other representations or clustering choices could change which competitions are considered closely related and thus contribute insights to a target task. 

Insight quality . The insights are assembled by an LLM-based agent. To reduce the risk of hallu-cination and misrepresentation, MLE-InsightForge must read every required source in full and back each insight with an evidence record, which links the insight to the human-written passages it is based on and makes it traceable. 

Leakage beyond the benchmark . MLE-InsightBench excludes the materials of every target task from the insight sources, but it cannot control what the backend LLMs have learned during pre-training. Because Kaggle writeups and notebooks are public, a backend LLM may have seen solutions of the target tasks; this affects all conditions equally, including Baseline, and therefore may inflate absolute scores. Selecting the most recent eligible competition of each domain as the target task reduces, but does not eliminate, this risk. 

Models and costs . The main results use one backend LLM for the agentic MLE system and one LLM for MLE-InsightForge, so they may depend on these models’ knowledge, tool-use behavior, and long-context capabilities; RQ4 studies the former dependency. The 12-hour, GPU-backed budget also limits accessibility and may favor approaches that spend more compute. 7 Related Work 

Agentic MLE . MLAgentBench [27] evaluates LLM agents that design, run, and revise ML ex-periments, and MLE-bench [7] evaluates end-to-end solutions of Kaggle competitions against private-test labels. The systems on the MLE-bench leaderboard mostly advance the search over candidate solutions [29, 38, 40, 63, 64, 68] and, increasingly, the use of insights from other tasks (§2.2). Systems that use insights couple a particular source of insights with a particular agent scaffold, and evaluate the combination as a whole on MLE-bench. Our work is complementary: rather than proposing another scaffold, we hold the agentic MLE system fixed and study how the assembled insights, their types, and their injection modes affect it on a benchmark whose source competitions are fixed and disjoint from the target tasks. 

Program synthesis for ML code . Before LLM agents, program synthesis targeted ML code by searching a restricted space of programs. BLAZE [65] synthesizes tensor transformations in a MATLAB-like DSL from input–output examples, and TF-Coder [57] enumerates expressions over TensorFlow operations, prioritized by learned models; INTENT [76] lets users decompose hard tensor transformations, and HySynth [3] guides the search with a grammar learned from LLM completions. Nam et al. [49] predict the sequence of PyTorch and TensorFlow API calls that transforms the inputs into the outputs to guide enumerative search. At a coarser granularity, AutoML-Zero [53] evolves complete ML algorithms from basic mathematical operations, NEAR [56] and dPADS [10] search for differentiable programs in a DSL, and Marinescu et al. [42] search ML pipelines defined by a context-free grammar. These techniques synthesize short programs or pipelines within a predefined search space, whereas an agentic MLE system writes an end-to-end solution in a general-purpose language; the insights assembled by MLE-InsightForge play a role similar to their learned priors, steering the system toward decisions that worked on related tasks. 

Experience, memory, and context for LLM agents . Reflexion [58] stores verbal feedback from earlier trials of the same task, ExpeL [75] extracts natural-language lessons from successful and failed trajectories and retrieves them for new tasks, and Agent Workflow Memory [66] induces reusable workflows from past trajectories. ACE [72] treats the agent’s context as an evolving playbook that is incrementally refined from execution feedback, and Agent Skills [2] package instructions and resources that the agent loads on demand. These approaches mainly learn from the agent’s own trajectories. MLE-InsightForge instead assembles insights from human-written solutions and writeups of peer tasks, organizes them into domain, competition, and improvement insights, and compares the skill and playbook modes of injecting the same insights. 

Mining Kaggle artifacts . DistilKaggle [46] curates Kaggle notebooks together with code-quality measures, and Jin et al. [31] mine Kaggle notebooks and use MLE-bench-style grading to study their reproducibility under modern execution environments. We use Kaggle descriptions, writeups, and top solutions as sources from which to assemble insights for solving unseen tasks. 

Data contamination in benchmarks . Contamination of evaluation data threatens the validity of LLM benchmarks, and LiveCodeBench [28] mitigates it by continuously collecting problems released after the models’ training cutoffs. For agentic MLE systems, the relevant contamination also comes from insight sources available at solving time (§2.3); MLE-InsightBench controls these sources by construction, using only causal non-descendants of the target tasks as sources.

8 Conclusion 

We studied how insights assembled from closely-related MLE tasks affect an agentic MLE system. We collected MLE-InsightBench, whose held-out split of 160 Kaggle competitions in 12 domains keeps target task information out of the insights, and designed MLE-InsightForge, an agent that assembles evidence-backed domain insights, competition insights, and improvement insights from the source competitions and injects them as skills or a playbook. The assembled insights improve the normalized private-test score by 123.9% as skills and 122.8% as a playbook; the three types of insights are complementary, skills are more cost-effective, and the improvements generalize. These findings suggest treating insight assembly as an explicit component of agentic MLE systems whose sources are controlled in evaluations.
