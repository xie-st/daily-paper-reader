Abstract 

Conventional neural-network training typically follows a predefined optimization recipe, motivating a LLM agent that monitors training dynamics and adjusts optimizer settings as training progresses, but a fixed agent policy does not accumulate experience across runs. We introduce RSI-Opt , a plug-and-play Recursive Self-Improvement Optimizer Agents which evolves the knowledge that guides this process: a prompt, a playbook of procedures, a library of tested optimizer code, and a decision cadence. A meta-agent rewrites this policy using evidence from earlier runs, and an archive retains evaluated policies for further evolution. Across 5 generations on three training tasks, the meta-agents develop reusable procedures and code and conduct their own CPU proxy experiments. RSI-Opt substantially improves optimizer-agent performance, with representative raw-space summary z-scores increasing from +10 .48 to +14 .33 on the evolution suite with Opus 5.5 and from −0.36 to +3 .11 on held-out tasks with GPT-5.5 through cross-model policy transfer. On the Burgers PDE, it reduces mean relative L2 error by approximately 2 .7-fold with Opus 5.5 and 8 .8-fold with GPT-6.1 over the fixed-prompt agent baseline, although PDE transfer varies across LLMs and tasks. Controlled comparisons show that the evolved policy combines reusable optimization knowledge with adaptation to each task: initial decisions capture most observed gains on seen tasks, while continued adaptation improves outcomes on unseen tasks. These findings demonstrate how procedures and executable tools accumulated across training runs can improve optimizer-agent performance and support transfer to scientific machine learning. 

# 1 Introduction 

Neural-network training usually begins with an optimizer and schedule chosen in advance. Their effectiveness can depend on the architecture, data, and training dynamics, yet selecting a new recipe often requires repeated runs and manual intervention. A coding agent offers another form of adaptation: it can inspect a training run and revise the optimizer’s program while the model trains. Concurrent work on reactive optimizers, OPAL [2], studies this within-run setting. A single LLM agent observes training dynamics and revises the update rule, learning rates, schedules, and diagnostics during an individual run. Its conversation persists within that run, but its fixed initial policy does not retain lessons for the next one. A useful optimizer change, a misleading diagnostic, or a tested implementation must be rediscovered when a new run starts.We study whether optimizer agents can improve this initial policy across runs through recursive self-improvement. RSI-Opt 

makes the agent’s accumulated knowledge an explicit, editable object: a prompt, a playbook of procedures and lessons, a library of optimizer and diagnostic code, and a decision cadence. An 1arXiv:2610.32026v1 [cs.AI] 23 Oct 2026 outer meta-agent reads evidence from earlier runs, modifies a parent policy, and evaluates the child on a training suite. An archive supports continued exploration and inheritance, following the archive-based approach of the Darwin Gödel Machine [35]. The underlying LLM weights, meta-agent instructions, and evaluator remain fixed; the policy used to improve optimizers is the object that evolves (Figure 1). This separation makes the learned knowledge inspectable, but it also exposes a generalization question. A policy can improve its evolution score by accumulating broadly useful procedures or by fitting recipes to a few familiar tasks. Better training-suite performance alone does not distinguish the two. Nor does a growing code library show whether an agent still needs to adapt during a new run. We therefore examine the content of the evolved policies, their transfer to unseen tasks and other LLMs, and the contribution of decisions made after the initial optimizer change. Our contributions are: • An explicit policy-evolution framework for optimizer agents. RSI-Opt adds cross-run policy evolution to the within-run optimizer-agent setting. Our experiments connect a coding agent to an editable optimizer interface, allowing it to monitor training dynamics and revise update rules during a run. fixed baseline prompt, and four example tasks, with attribution and implementation details in Section 4. The contribution is the evolving policy, outer search, and study of the resulting knowledge. • An evaluation of transfer and online adaptation. We evaluate nine single-GPU tasks with three in-run LLMs, fresh evaluation seeds, comparisons of reported means, and controls using an unedited library template, a frozen first optimizer change, a one-decision agent, and the full agent. Three tasks guide evolution; three ML tasks and three PDE problems test transfer beyond that suite. • Evidence of selective transfer and specialization. Evolved policies improve the training suite for all three LLMs. GPT-5.5 and GPT-6.1 improve the mean summary score in every task group; Opus 5.5 improves on four of six held-out tasks but has a lower aggregate held-out summary score. Initial adaptation captures most of the observed benefit on seen tasks, while later decisions improve unseen-task performance for both baseline and evolved agents. Held-out ML transfer peaks early as continued evolution increasingly fits the training suite. The meta-agents accumulate substantive artifacts: reusable update modules, parameter-role checks, scheduling procedures, and CPU proxy experiments. We analyze these artifacts alongside their measured outcomes rather than treating higher archive scores as sufficient evidence of general improvement. The harness, policies, transcripts, and results are released. 

# 2 Related work 

Learned and discovered optimizers. Learned optimizers [1, 20] meta-train an update rule across tasks, while symbolic search has discovered update algorithms such as Lion [4]. The resulting update program is typically fixed before a target training run, although its updates can depend on the current state. In RSI-Opt , the evolved object is the optimizer agent’s policy, which guides further program edits during each target run. Established optimizers such as Adam [13], AdamW [16], Shampoo [8], SOAP [30], and Muon [12], together with recent extensions such as spectral orthogonal gradient projection for continual learning [17], supply building blocks that agents can select and compose. 

Agents that improve programs and policies. LLMs have been used to optimize prompts and solutions [33], evolve prompts [7], improve code-generation scaffolds [34], design agentic systems [10], and evolve algorithms against an evaluator [21]. The Darwin Gödel Machine [35] maintains an 2archive of modified coding agents and samples parents for further exploration, drawing on the idea of a self-improving Gödel machine [26]. Our outer search adopts this archive-based structure. Persistent natural-language lessons [27], skill libraries [31], and self-evolving procedural memory learned from contrasting multi-model trajectories [5] provide related mechanisms for carrying experience across episodes. RSI-Opt combines procedures and executable tools in an optimizer-agent policy. Every valid evaluated child enters the archive; scores guide subsequent parent and final policy selection rather than acting as a strict improvement filter. 

Optimizer agents within a training run. We found concurrent work, OPAL, introduces a coding agent that monitors training dynamics and revises optimizer and diagnostic programs within a single run. Each new run starts from a fixed prompt, without retaining experience from previous runs. RSI-Opt addresses the complementary problem of improving the agent’s policy across runs by accumulating and refining optimization procedures and executable tools. We examine how this knowledge transfers to unseen tasks and other LLMs, and when continued evolution leads to specialization. Population-based training [11] also adapts training hyperparameters through selection and replacement across a population of runs. 

Optimization for physics-informed training. PINN training can suffer from imbalanced gradients across residual and boundary losses [32] and ill-conditioned loss landscapes, motivating approaches such as Adam followed by L-BFGS and other second-order methods [25], as well as adaptive optimizers for hard-constrained PINN training [18]. Our PDE problems test transfer beyond the ML tasks used for evolution. They assess the applicability of evolved optimizer-agent knowledge; they do not establish a new PDE discretization or a universally better PINN optimizer. 

# 3 Method 

3.1 Within-run adaptation 

The inner loop exposes the optimizer’s update rule as an editable program, allowing a coding agent to inspect training losses and diagnostic reports and revise the optimization procedure at scheduled decision points. The training script, model architecture, data order, loss, batch size, and number of parameter updates are fixed. At D scheduled decisions, the agent receives the training-loss history since its previous decision, reports from its analysis program, errors, and timing information. It can inspect and edit files in its workspace and run shell commands without network or GPU access. The agent edits two programs. coder receives gradients at each update and modifies the parameters in place. Analyst computes read-only diagnostics every updates. A new updater is tested over a short trial window and rolled back if it raises an exception. Training pauses while the agent decides, so agent latency affects wall-clock cost rather than the number of model updates. Conversation history persists across decisions within the run. The final evaluation metric is withheld from the in-run agent and used by the outer evaluator after the run. Our single-agent baseline uses fixed prompt without an accumulated playbook or library. This prompt supplies the task description, protocol, and program interfaces. The same template is instantiated for each run, and experience from completed runs is not retained in the next run’s policy.

The outer loop maintains an archive of evaluated policies, following the archive-based search of the Darwin Gödel Machine [35]. To generate a child, it samples an eligible parent πp with probability 4proportional to sigmoid( λ(sp − ˜s)) 1 + np

where sp is the parent’s score, ˜s is the archive median score, and np counts its children. The score term favors stronger parents, while the child count reduces repeated selection of the same branch. A separate meta-agent receives a copy of the parent policy and digests of its evaluation runs. Each digest contains the final metric and z-score, the in-run agent’s recorded rationales, final optimizer program, and notes. The meta-agent also sees archive entries with per-task scores and changelogs. It edits the policy and is instructed to record its changes, supporting evidence, and expected effects. Its CPU-only shell permits unit tests and proxy experiments. The instructions encourage transferable lessons and prohibit task names and task-specific branches; this instruction does not itself prevent specialization. Validation checks required template fields, Python parsing, and size limits. A child that fails these checks is returned to the meta-agent for repair, at most twice. Every valid child is evaluated on the training suite and added to the archive, including children that score below their parent. Thus the archive retains explored alternatives, while performance guides further parent selection and the final policy choice.

Scope of recursive self-improvement. The meta-agent improves the policy by which the in-run agent improves optimizers. Later generations inherit and revise the procedures and tools accumulated by earlier ones. The underlying LLM weights, meta-agent instructions, scoring rule, and designated task splits remain fixed. This is recursive improvement of an agent policy, without modification of the meta-agent’s own improvement procedure. The experiments measure the resulting policies; a separate matched-budget control would be needed to isolate the benefit of inheritance over independent proposals. 

3.4 Implementation 

Both the in-run agent and the meta-agent operate through a tool-calling loop on the API, with access to four tools: bash , read_file , write_file , and edit_file . The backend is self-contained and leaves the inner-loop training code untouched. For isolation, each agent’s shell executes in its own network namespace with no access to GPUs or API credentials. When a conversation grows beyond 160k tokens, the agent writes a summary of its progress, which then replaces the earlier history. Runs are scheduled across GPUs through a file-based queue, and because training halts whenever the agent is deciding, multiple runs can share a single GPU efficiently. Every LLM is configured with high reasoning effort. Filesystem restrictions and the extent of their coverage are detailed in Section A.

# 5 Results 

5.1 What knowledge evolves? 

Evolution produced 21 policies over 5 generations of four children, with no invalid policies (Figure 2). Generation 0, which augments the fixed prompt with references to an empty playbook and library, scored +10.30. The best archive score reached +14.63 for policy p016 in generation 4. Re-evaluating the three highest-scoring eligible candidates on two additional seeds selected p016: its four-seed mean was +14.55, compared with +14.42 and +14.19 for the other candidates. These archive scores guide search and selection; the remaining comparisons use fresh seeds.

The resulting library included a hybrid updater using Muon on selected tensors and the starting rule elsewhere, schedule helpers, a parameter-role classifier, diagnostics, and interface checks. A recurring procedure was to make an early, evidence-based change and retain it unless later observations justified another edit. Nine of the 20 meta-agents, including the one that produced p016, reached the 50-call or 30-minute limit before writing a changelog entry. A proxy-supported optimizer change. The largest observed task-score jump followed a hypothesis that a meta-agent tested using its own CPU proxy. It trained a small GroupNorm UNet by flow matching from a constant-rate AdamW baseline. Adding Muon on hidden convolutions reduced validation loss in every configuration it tested, including 0.188 to 0.174 at 3000 steps. Within that proxy sweep, the learning rate on the remaining Adam-updated tensors mattered more than Muon’s own rate. The meta-agent recorded the proposal as an untested transfer from the proxy to the actual task. Subsequent in-run agents applied it, and the UNet’s evolution score rose from about +9 to +19 on both seeds in generation 3. The proposal also appeared in other lineages after their meta-agents read archive summaries. This sequence connects a recorded hypothesis, executable tests, and a later gain; isolating the change’s causal contribution would require a controlled parent-to-child replay. 

Exposure and exclusions. Before filesystem restrictions were added, one generation-1 meta-agent read description of its noisy-label example, a designated held-out task. That policy and its descendant (2 policies) were excluded from parent selection and final selection. The audit identified no additional direct reads of held-out material. Because archive summaries can transmit information across branches, direct-read and ancestry checks alone do not establish the absence of indirect exposure (Section A).

5.3 What does the evolved policy transfer? 

New seeds on seen tasks. With Opus 5.5, RSI-Opt raises the training-suite score from +10.48 to +14.33, a summary-score difference of +3.85. Fresh seeds show that the selected policy’s training-suite gain is not restricted to the seeds used for search. The tasks themselves remain those used during evolution. 

Unseen tasks with the evolution model. On the six held-out tasks, Opus has a lower aggregate summary score: +5.22 for the baseline versus +4.87 for RSI-Opt . The held-out ML and PDE summary-score differences are -0.48 and -0.21, respectively. Per-task effects are mixed: RSI-Opt 

reduces mean Burgers error by about 2 .7×, reduces heat error by about 2 .2×, and adds approximately 3 accuracy points on noisy CIFAR-10, but loses 1.1 points on the ViT and increases fractional-PDE error. 5.6 Transfer to PDE training 

PDE problems provide the largest shift from the evolution suite. Several agent configurations substantially reduce solution error relative to the original Adam recipes (Table 2). Heat benefits particularly from schedules that reduce the learning rate near the end of training; the static recipe’s final errors span 0.008–0.73 across seeds. These gains should be interpreted alongside the limited static tuning performed here. The incremental effect of the evolved policy varies by LLM and task. Opus’s PDE summary score falls from +1.72 to +1.51: Burgers and heat improve, but fractional-PDE error worsens. GPT-5.5 improves from -1.90 to -0.32, reducing mean Burgers error by about 1.7× and heat error by about 15 ×, although its aggregate PDE summary score remains below zero. GPT-6.1 improves from +1.61 to +1.74, with an approximately 8 .8-fold Burgers improvement and 2.0-fold heat improvement. Both GPT models improve the mean metric on all three PDE tasks. PDE transfer is therefore selective, even when the same policy is applied unchanged.

# 6 Discussion and limitations 

Inspectable knowledge with selective transfer. RSI-Opt stores its accumulated knowledge in text and code rather than changing the underlying LLM weights. Recent work suggests that such harness evolution can reach a ceiling beyond which weight training becomes necessary [28]. Policies can be inspected, tested, and used by other in-run models. The experiments show that such portability does not guarantee a benefit on every task: GPT-5.5 and GPT-6.1 improve the mean raw-space summary score in every task group, while Opus gains on the evolution suite but has a lower aggregate held-out summary score despite improving four of six held-out task means. GPT-6.1 improves mean Burgers error by approximately 8 .8× but worsens LSTM loss. Reusable priors and continued adaptation. The budget controls locate most of the seen-task benefit in the initial optimizer change. On unseen tasks, both baseline and evolved agents benefit from later decisions. The tested unedited library template fails on some architectures, so code reuse alone does not explain the full agent’s outcomes. These comparisons support task-conditioned selection and online adaptation; they do not isolate reasoning from additional observations, code modifications, or compute. A stronger frozen recipe selected on development tasks could improve on the particular template tested here. 

Specialization and selection. Evolution uses three tasks and two seeds per policy. The in-run agent adds stochastic variation beyond data and initialization seeds, and archive selection can favor unusually strong evaluations. Re-evaluating the top three candidates on two additional seeds reduces reliance on a single estimate; fresh final seeds further separate reporting from search. Neither step makes the evolution tasks unseen. The early peak in held-out ML performance is consistent with specialization to this small suite. Broader task coverage, additional independent evolution runs, and a separate policy-validation suite would strengthen claims about generalization and the reliability of the search procedure. 

Aggregation and uncertainty. A z-score measures improvement relative to the static recipe’s seed spread. Small reference variability can make a task dominate the mean, even if its raw improvement is modest. Final aggregate summaries use the raw-space means and static standard deviations printed in Table 2, including for PDEs; they are descriptive recomputations and differ from per-seed log-error normalization. Historical control and intermediate-checkpoint ML scores retain their original reporting precision; PDE control and checkpoint aggregates are omitted from the corrected comparisons when the raw per-task metrics are unavailable. Raw metrics, signed differences, and per-task outcomes are therefore necessary alongside aggregates. With three final seeds per cell, uncertainty estimates have limited precision. An interval that includes zero does not show equivalence, and the magnitude of a z-score alone does not establish statistical significance. 

What the current controls do not establish. We do not isolate the effect of recursive inheritance against a matched-budget search that always proposes from generation 0, nor the value of the archive against a restricted-parent search. The evidence concerns the policies produced by the reported trajectory. Our static comparisons also cover only the starting recipes, an oracle learning-rate sweep, and one unedited library template. Schedule-matched Adam, standard Muon/AdamW hybrids, and suitable Adam-to-L-BFGS PINN recipes [25] would better separate known optimizer choices from agent adaptation. Comparisons involving L-BFGS should match gradient evaluations 15 or compute rather than nominal update counts. 

Scale and cost. Runs use a single GPU and relatively short update budgets. Transfer to longer, multi-GPU training is not tested here. Equal decision limits do not equalize actual API charges, diagnostics, optimizer work, or wall-clock time. A full cost comparison would include outer-search evaluations, CPU proxies, GPU usage, cache pricing, and LLM pauses before claiming amortized savings. Hosted-model changes can also affect reproducibility; preserved prompts, programs, and transcripts document the tested behavior but do not freeze the provider’s implementation. 

Execution boundaries and exposure. Agent shells use network and mount namespaces to restrict access to other runs, evaluation data, and credentials. These restrictions were introduced during the project rather than applied uniformly from its start. Section A reports a known read of a held-out task description and the exclusion of the exposed policy and its descendant. Since archive summaries can transmit information between lineages, the direct-access audit alone does not certify the absence of indirect exposure. In addition, generated optimizer code runs inside the training process; shell isolation is not by itself a complete account of that code’s access to process state. The wrapper’s checks and the documented sandbox reduce specific risks without proving that evaluation manipulation is impossible. More broadly, the safety and robustness of reasoning-augmented LLM agents remain open concerns [29].
