Recent advances in self-improving LLM agents have demonstrated the potential of AI systems that drive their own intelligence. However, many domain-specific applications rely on specialized foundation models that possess strong domain expertise but remain dependent on human-designed improvements. Extending recursive self-improvement to these models presents a key challenge: as a specialist improves, the strategies used to advance it must also adapt. To address this challenge, we propose Heterogeneous Agentic CoEvolution (HACE) , a framework that jointly evolves specialized foundation models and LLM agents through reciprocal learning. HACE improves the specialist through supervised learning while training the agent with on-policy reinforced self-training, guided by verified performance gains on the current specialist. The agent’s exploration, in turn, informs subsequent specialist training, enabling both models to adapt to each other’s progress. To sustain this reciprocal learning, HACE further employs evolutionary search to optimize their interaction harness, adapting how the models exchange information and select training experiences as their capabilities evolve. We evaluate HACE across three challenging domains: recommendation systems, protein function prediction in biology, and crystal stability prediction in material discovery. We show that HACE outperforms the evaluated self-improving methods on the primary task metrics across all three domains. These results shed light on heterogeneous coevolution as a universal approach to improving specialized foundation models through reciprocal interaction with a learning agent.

1arXiv:2610.16078v1 [cs.AI] 12 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

Lately, large language model (LLM) agents are beginning to automate the experimental work behind model development. Agents can revise their implementations, improve their interaction harnesses, and modify the procedures that generate further improvements (Zhang et al., 2026a; Lee et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026b). They can also continue training on experience collected during adaptation and exploration (Zweiger et al., 2025; Yuksekgonul et al., 2026). These advances suggest that model development itself can become an autonomous learning process. Extending this progress to scientific AI requires bringing the domain expertise of specialized foundation models into this process. In scientific AI, specialized foundation models generally learn reusable domain knowledge through large-scale pretraining. Protein language models learn biological regularities from sequences (Lin et al., 2023), while pretrained interatomic potential models learn the energies and forces governing material behavior (Deng et al., 2023). In recommendation, architectures such as HSTU (Zhai et al., 2024) and Wukong (Zhang et al., 2024) extend this scaling approach to large collections of user interactions. These models acquire domain expertise that often exceeds that of general LLM agents due to specific designs. Yet they still cannot self-evolve like LLM agents. Once supervised training converges and further training yields no improvement, researchers have to manually identify useful designs to improve the specialized foundation model, such as better feature engineering or algorithmic changes, as shown in Figure 1. Recent developments in agentic systems pave a feasible path to automate this process. Their ability to interpret feedback, explore alternatives, and revise executable procedures allows them to influence how another model learns. For example, Eureka (Ma et al., 2024) demonstrates this possibility by using LLMs to design reward programs for training robot policies autonomously. However, most self-evolving systems still rely on proprietary LLMs whose parameters remain fixed while agent code and interaction harnesses evolve (Zhang et al., 2026a; Lee et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026b). The experience collected during improvement thus does not update the underlying language model. For specialized foundation models, the challenge is for the LLM agent model to keep improving the specialized model as the specialized model advances. An intervention that improves the initial model can become redundant after further training. An agent that learns to address the initial model’s weaknesses, therefore, faces a changing task as those weaknesses are resolved. Continued progress requires the agent to learn continually with the model when it improves. We refer to this mechanism as reciprocal learning .To close the gap, we propose a novel framework, Heterogeneous Agentic Coevolution (HACE) , which coevolves a specialized foundation model (i.e., the specialist) with a heterogeneous LLM agent which learns to guide the coevolution. The key idea is to improve the specialist through supervision while also training the agent to keep advancing it under an adaptive curriculum. Agent learning is guided by verified gains over the current specialist. The agent’s exploration, in turn, guides the selection of experiences for subsequent specialist training. As the specialist improves, it changes which agent contributions are useful. As the agent improves, it changes the learning opportunities available to the specialist. This reciprocal feedback loop seamlessly connects supervised specialist learning with on-policy reinforced self-training. We need to ensure that these models interact and develop their capabilities in a continuously improving direction. What the agent sees and which experiences reach specialist training determine the opportunities that either model can use. Thus, we also propose an evolutionary interaction harness in HACE that governs information exchange and training experience selection. Evolutionary search evaluates harness changes through their effects on task performance and specialist learning. A better harness can expose opportunities that the current models have not utilized, while model updates change which harness designs remain effective. HACE thus improves the specialist while learning how to organize its continued improvement. Domain expertise, agentic capabilities, and the process connecting their learning coevolve together. To show the effectiveness, we comprehensively evaluate HACE in three challenging domains, including recommendation systems, protein function prediction in biology, and crystal stability prediction in material discovery. In each domain, we begin with specialists already trained to convergence , where continued training under the original supervised procedure yields no further improvement. This setting stress-tests whether interaction with an evolving agent can create opportunities for learning beyond that plateau. Across diverse specialist architectures and large-scale datasets, HACE achieves state-of-the-art performance and outperforms 2all evaluated evolution methods. These results demonstrate that HACE enables specialists to continue improving beyond supervised convergence. HACE brings their existing domain expertise into autonomous self-improvement while developing the agentic capabilities needed to advance it. 

> To summarize, our main contributions are as follows:

• We introduce HACE, an evolutionary framework for autonomous self-improvement that brings specialized foundation models and LLM agents into a shared coevolution process. • We propose a novel reciprocal learning mechanism in which verified agent contributions guide agent learning, agent exploration also informs specialist training, and an evolving harness adaptively coordinates their reciprocal interaction. • We empirically demonstrate the effectiveness of HACE across three challenging domains: recommendation, protein function prediction, and material discovery. The results show that HACE can substantially push the domain-specific performance beyond the capability boundary. 

# 2 Related Work 

Agent and harness optimization. Recent work uses execution feedback to improve agent programs, interaction harnesses, and the procedures that propose further improvements (Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026a; Lee et al., 2026; Luo et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026b; Cemri et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026b). Other methods improve the underlying model through parameter updates. Reinforced self-training learns from responses selected by task rewards (Singh et al., 2024), while self-adaptation and test-time learning use generated experience or task feedback for further training (Zweiger et al., 2025; Yuksekgonul et al., 2026). HACE connects harness search with parameter learning in both a specialized foundation model and an LLM agent. The harness controls their interaction and the experience used to train each model. 

Coevolution and reciprocal learning. Coevolution can be traced to reciprocal evolutionary responses between interacting populations in biology (Janzen, 1980). In machine learning, many methods have connected the development of reward programs, environments, or optimization procedures with the learners they guide (Ma et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2026c; Wang et al., 2019; Dennis et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026). HACE studies this dependence between models with different capabilities and learning signals. Agent exploration can change which instances train the specialized model, whose predictions in turn determine which agent responses pass verification. Our analysis characterizes this dependence through the distribution of verified experience and identifies when an update to either learner changes its allocation across instances. 

Agent distillation. Agent distillation transfers reasoning and tool use from LLM agents to smaller language models (Liu et al., 2026a; Kang et al., 2025). MAD-OPD derives supervision from teachers that debate over the student’s current trajectories (Wang et al., 2026). These methods update the student while keeping teacher parameters fixed. HACE is distinct from this line of work and takes a step forward, where an LLM agent with lower overall domain performance can still improve on the specialized foundation model. Agent exploration guides instance selection for specialist training, while verified contributions guide response selection back for agent learning. Both models can therefore coevolve reciprocally without assigning either a fixed teacher role.

3.1 Reciprocal Learning 

Let S denote the specialist, A the agent, and H the harness. Round t = 0 , 1, . . . starts from ( St, H t, A t). We propose a harness H′ by modifying a parent from an archive of accepted harnesses. Its fields control the task information and specialist context shown to the agent, how responses are interpreted, and which examples train each learner. Let I index the finite nonempty training pool of labeled instances ( xi, y i). Under H′, the agent receives a prompt constructed from a training input xi and the predictions of St, without access to its label yi. We evaluate each response τ against yi using the same task constraints as the specialist. Let qAi (τ ) and qSi denote their respective task qualities, with larger values preferred. Appendix B.3 specifies the task quality and verification rules. The harness also determines which prediction candidates the agent can access. # 4 Experiments 

In this section, we empirically evaluate whether HACE can push the capability boundary with the converged specialized foundation models across three important domains (recommendation systems, protein function prediction, and material discovery), compared with other self-improving methods. 

Experimental setups. By default, we use Gemma4-E4B (Gemma Team, 2026) as the LLM agent. We also conduct more experiments with various LLM agents (Gemma4-E2B, Gemma4-26B-A4B) in Section 4.3. On the specialized foundation models, for recommendation tasks, we evaluate on MovieLens 1M (ML-1M) (Harper and Konstan, 2015) and four Amazon categories (McAuley et al., 2015) using three mainstream foundation models SASRec (Kang and McAuley, 2018), HSTU (Zhai et al., 2024), and Wukong (Zhang et al., 2024). For protein function prediction, we use frozen ESM-2 (Lin et al., 2023) and ProtT5-XL (Elnaggar et al., 2022) backbones on CAFA (Jiang et al., 2016). For crystal stability prediction in material discovery task, we use CHGNet (Deng et al., 2023) on WBM (Wang et al., 2021; Riebesell et al., 2025). Base denotes the initial specialists, which are already trained to convergence before coevolution. Unless stated otherwise, reported scores apply the fusion in Equation 3 with the weight selected by cross fitting, for Base and for all methods alike. At round 0 the agent is untrained and cross fitting selects a zero weight. Table 7 shows that HACE is agnostic to the specialist architectures and their adaptation scope, including causal attention models, a factorization network, protein language models, and a graph neural potential model. We compare against popular self-improving methods, including ReST-EM (Singh et al., 2024), Meta-Harness (Lee et al., 2026), OpenEvolve (Sharma, 2025), DGM (Zhang et al., 2026a), and TTT-Discover (Yuksekgonul et al., 2026). The recommendation task performs ranking on candidate items, the protein prediction task predicts the function terms, and the materials discovery task ranks candidate crystal structures by stability within a defined chemical system. These experiments measure improvements in prediction with their pretrained representations. 

Evaluation metrics. Hit Rate (HR@10) measures the fraction of recommendation queries with the target item among the first ten predictions. NDCG uses binary relevance, discounts each relevant candidate at rank 

r by 1 / log 2(r + 1), and normalizes by the ideal ranking within the same reference pool. We average NDCG@5 and NDCG@10 on CAFA, and NDCG@3 and NDCG@5 on WBM according to the baseline implementations. For protein function prediction, Fmax is the maximum harmonic mean of precision and recall over prediction thresholds (Jiang et al., 2016). For material discovery, we use F1 to measure their harmonic mean for identifying stable structures (Riebesell et al., 2025). Higher values indicate better performance for these metrics. 4.1 Main Comparison 

Recommendation. HACE achieves the highest mean HR@10 in all 15 settings in Table 1. Relative gains over Base range from 2 .9% to 18 .5%, averaging 12 .1% across settings. On ML-1M, several evolution methods fall below the initial SASRec and HSTU models, while HACE improves all three architectures. On the four Amazon categories, Wukong has the strongest initial scores among the three architectures, yet HACE further improves its HR@10 by 12 .9% to 16 .6%. Contribution rewards train the agent against the current specialist throughout this process. 

Protein function prediction. Table 2 shows improvements with both protein backbones. HACE raises NDCG from 0 .6285 to 0 .6745 with ESM-2 and from 0 .6241 to 0 .6739 with ProtT5-XL. Existing evolution methods improve over Base by at most 0 .0040 and 0 .0032, respectively, compared with 0 .0460 and 0 .0498 for HACE. HACE also leads in Fmax , AUPR, and Recall@10. These gains cover both the ordering of function terms and their classification. Table 5 also reports semantic distance, for which DGM has the lowest mean on ProtT5-XL. The ablations below examine how these gains depend on harness search and specialist optimization. 7Material Discovery. On WBM, all competing methods improve NDCG over Base, from 0 .6388 to at least 0.6474. HACE reaches 0 .6541, exceeding the strongest competing mean by 0 .0028. HACE also achieves the best mean on all four additional metrics in Table 6. Removing specialist optimization reduces NDCG to 0.6395, close to Base at 0 .6388. The contribution of supervised specialist updates is therefore particularly clear on this task. Supervised adaptation improves numerical predictions, while the ranking objective favors recovering stable candidates near the top.

# 5 Conclusion 

We introduced HACE, a novel framework for reciprocal self-improvement between specialized foundation models and LLM agents. By evolving both the learners and their interaction with an updating harness, HACE makes each model a source of learning for the other. Our analysis identifies sufficient conditions for specialist learning from adaptively selected experience. Experiments in three domains demonstrate improvements beyond initially converged specialists across different architectures. These results show how heterogeneous AI models can support each other’s development. Future work can study sustained coevolution, collaboration among multiple specialists, and autonomous model development for scientific discovery.

# A Limitations 

HACE coevolves a single specialized foundation model with a single LLM agent. We do not study coevolution among multiple specialists or agents, which may require different mechanisms for interaction and learning. The compared methods differ in which components they can update. Some support parameter updates, others evolve the harness or agent program with the specialist held fixed, and several assume a closed-weight LLM. We follow each method’s original formulation rather than introducing specialist adaptation beyond its design. These comparisons therefore evaluate the methods as complete systems, while Table 3 separately examines the contribution of each HACE component. Our findings are based on the evaluated domains, architectures, and computational budgets. Further work can study how long coevolution sustains useful learning and how to allocate computation among harness search, specialist adaptation, and agent training.

# C Additional Experiments 

C.1 Evaluation Details 

We fix disjoint training, validation, and test sets before coevolution. Specialist adaptation, agent learning, and training instance selection use only Dtrain . At each round, we compare the proposed and current specialist and harness pairs on the same batch sampled from Dval , holding the agent fixed. Both pairs use the same two folds, but fit their own λ on one fold and are evaluated on the other. We then exchange the folds and average the resulting instance scores for joint selection. Internal specialist calibration uses a separate validation batch disjoint from the comparison batch. Validation data guide selection throughout coevolution, while Dtest is reserved for final evaluation and does not inform training, calibration, or selection. For recommendation, we partition users across the three sets and train the initial specialist on histories excluding each user’s last two interactions. During coevolution, we use the second to last interactions of training users for learning and those of validation users for calibration and selection. Final evaluation uses the last interaction of each test user. For protein function prediction, we follow the CAFA temporal split, restrict inputs to information available at t0, and keep instances sharing a protein name in the same set. For material discovery, we partition by chemical system, keeping all candidate structures from the same system in one set. Below we describe the evaluation metrics. 

Ranking metrics. Hit rate at cutoff k (HR@ k) is the fraction of recommendation queries whose held out target item appears among the first k predictions. We report HR@10 in the main comparison. Normalized discounted cumulative gain (NDCG) assigns a binary relevance label to each candidate. For a ranking π, its discounted cumulative gain is DCG@ k(π) = 

> min( k, |π|)

∑

> r=1

rel( πr )log 2(r + 1) . (42) NDCG@ k divides this sum by the largest possible DCG@ k within the same fixed reference pool. It rewards both recovering relevant candidates and placing them earlier. The reported CAFA score averages NDCG@5 and NDCG@10, while the WBM score averages NDCG@3 and NDCG@5. 

Protein function metrics. Precision is the fraction of predicted function terms that are correct, and recall is the fraction of relevant terms recovered. Recall@10 measures the fraction of relevant terms recovered among the first ten predictions. At prediction threshold t, their harmonic mean is F1(t) = 2 P (t)R(t)/(P (t) + R(t)). 20 The maximum score over thresholds is Fmax = max t F1(t). The area under the precision–recall curve (AUPR) summarizes the tradeoff between precision and recall across thresholds. Minimum semantic distance ( Smin )measures errors using the information added by each ontology term given its parents (Jiang et al., 2016). At each threshold, remaining uncertainty ru( t) is the average information in missed terms, and misinformation mi( t) is the average information in incorrectly predicted terms. The metric is

> t

√ru( t)2 + mi( t)2. (43) Lower values indicate less semantic error. 

Materials metrics. For crystal stability prediction, F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall for the stable class. The discovery acceleration factor (DAF) divides precision by the fraction of stable structures in the evaluation pool (Riebesell et al., 2025). A value of one matches the expected precision of random selection. Mean absolute error (MAE) averages the absolute differences between predicted and reference target values. The coefficient of determination ( R2) equals one minus the ratio of squared prediction error to the squared error of predicting the mean reference value. It equals one for perfect predictions, zero for this constant baseline, and is negative when predictions have greater squared error. Higher values are better for all metrics except Smin and MAE.
