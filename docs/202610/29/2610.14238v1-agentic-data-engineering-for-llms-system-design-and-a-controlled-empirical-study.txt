## ABSTRACT 

LLM data engineering requires iterative experimentation to determine how data selection, rewards, and curricula affect model learning, making it a natural setting for AI-for-AI (AI4AI) research. Can agents use experimental feedback to pro-gressively improve strategies, and do the resulting strategies improve target-model performance on held-out data? We study how agents use experimental feedback to develop data-engineering strategies under fixed training and evaluation protocols. Our Agentic Data Engineering (ADE) framework lets agents pursue, revise, or combine research directions using cumulative findings. Strategies are selected by in-loop scores and evaluated on held-out benchmarks. Across four tasks—math and code data selection, math reward design, and math curriculum learning—selected strategies achieve absolute gains over generic baselines of up to 13.34% on AIME25 Pass@32 and 5.85% averaged across four held-out benchmarks under fixed training conditions. In-loop scores improve over successive experiments, while held-out trajectories show that later in-loop gains do not always improve generalization. Comparisons of research organization and feedback access favor combining evi-dence analysis with cumulative findings. Research trajectories show how earlier findings inform strategy revisions and combinations across research directions. Our code is released at https://github.com/datajuicer/agentic-data-engineering. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

AI-for-AI (AI4AI) seeks to use AI systems to automate and improve the research process behind model development, including the design of training data, learning algorithms, and feedback (Wu et al., 2026). LLM data engineering offers a concrete setting for studying this research capability. Data selection determines which examples a model learns from, curriculum design determines their allocation over training, and reward design determines the feedback assigned to model behavior. The value of these interventions depends on what a target model learns under given training conditions, making downstream experiments central to strategy development. Recent work on data-centric improvement likewise frames automation as an iterative loop in which evaluation guides successive data interventions (Zou et al., 2026). Developing such strategies requires agents to propose changes, interpret their effects, and decide what to investigate next. At the same time, a fixed training paradigm makes these consequential research decisions experimentally comparable. Assessing this research capability requires examining both how strategies develop and their resulting effectiveness, motivating two questions. Question 1 : Can agents use experimental feedback to guide subsequent research decisions and develop progressively stronger strategies? Autonomy over execution is not sufficient for AI4AI; the relevant autonomy is over evidence-conditioned research decisions (Lim et al., 2026). A strong final strategy may reflect best-of-many sampling rather than feedback-guided research. Question 2 : Can agent-developed strategies improve target-model performance on evaluation data withheld from the research process? Repeatedly using evaluation feedback to develop and select strategies can favor the particular examples or metrics exposed during search (Wang et al., 2026a; Xia et al., 2026a). Answering these questions requires assessing progress over experiments, the links between prior feedback and subsequent decisions, and held-out effectiveness.

We introduce Agentic Data Engineering (ADE) through two principles (Figure 1). Principle 1 :

Give agents control over research decisions under fixed experimental protocols ( →Q1). Agents use cumulative memory of prior findings to choose subsequent research directions. The Harness validates actions and controls information access; the Engine executes admitted experiments under task-specific conditions. We examine how Agents use feedback to develop stronger strategies. Principle 2 : Select in-loop; evaluate held-out ( →Q2). Strategies are selected using only in-loop scores; the selected checkpoint is then frozen and evaluated on disjoint held-out data. These results never enter the research process, providing an independent assessment of effectiveness. Across four tasks—math and code data selection, math reward design, and math curriculum learning— ADE improves target-model performance over generic baselines, with absolute gains up to 13.34% on AIME25 Pass@32 and 5.85% averaged across four held-out benchmarks. Successive experiments progressively improve strategies: for math data selection, mean in-loop Pass@32 rises from 45.56% for the first completed strategies to a final 54.44%. Ablations of research organization and feedback access favor the complete ADE system, which exceeds the strongest alternative per metric by 6.67% in in-loop Pass@32 and 2.62% averaged across four held-out benchmarks. Research trajectories link earlier findings to later revisions and combinations. Our contributions are threefold: • We introduce ADE, giving agents control over research decisions under fixed experimental protocols and separating in-loop selection from held-out evaluation. • We evaluate progressive strategy improvement and held-out effectiveness across four tasks under fixed training conditions, examining both sequential and parallel research. • We assess research organization and feedback use through ablations and trace how earlier findings inform subsequent strategy revisions and combinations.

## 3 EXPERIMENTAL FRAMEWORK FOR AGENTIC DATA ENGINEERING 

Overview. ADE develops strategies through a Harness -governed state machine (Figure 2) with task-defined strategy interfaces and experimental contracts. Under Principle 1 , Agents use in-loop feedback from Experiment Packages and cumulative Memory to direct research; the Experimental Engine executes admitted strategies under fixed experimental protocols; the Harness validates actions, controls information access, and commits state transitions. Under Principle 2 , selection follows in-loop scores; External Evaluation assesses the selected checkpoint on held-out data. A Run is one bounded research process containing Plans , each specifying a research direction and its commitment. A Trial is one realization and experimental evaluation of a Plan’s fixed commitment. We next describe the experimental contract (Section 3.1), agent research and Memory (Section 3.2), Harness enforcement (Section 3.3), and external evaluation and oversight (Section 3.4). Appendix C formalizes the state transitions. 3.1 EXPERIMENTAL CONTRACT AND CONTROLLED EXECUTION 

The experimental contract defines the intervention boundary. Each task specifies a strategy interface with authorized inputs, required outputs, and constraints. Agents express each strategy as an executable implementation of this interface. Across Trials of the same task, the experimental configuration B fixes the target model, candidate data and evaluation splits, training budget, opti-mization recipe, checkpoint rule, evaluator, and resource policy. The admitted strategy is the only agent-controlled intervention; the Engine executes it under B (Figure 2a). Task-specific interfaces thus connect different interventions to a common research workflow. 

Checkpoint selection and Trial comparison use separate evaluation budgets. For Trial k in Plan 

p, the Engine trains the target model and performs two stages of evaluation on the same in-loop dataset Din (Figure 2b). Structured experimental feedback supports subsequent research. The Engine returns an im-mutable Experiment Package containing the in-loop score, selected checkpoint, executed strategy, and task-permitted execution, training, and model-behavior records (Figure 2c). Agents inspect these records alongside in-loop performance to analyze strategy execution and target-model responses. Each record identifies its source Trial. The package supplies feedback for analysis and subsequent Memory updates; the Harness enforces its information boundaries (Section 3.3). Appendix C.4 specifies the task interfaces and package schemas. 3.2 AGENT -D IRECTED RESEARCH WITH CUMULATIVE MEMORY 

Agents choose research directions using prior findings. The Coordinator (Figure 2g) draws on 

Run Memory (RM), a Plan Catalog of existing directions, and the current Trial ranking. It chooses between exploring a new direction and revisiting, challenging, or combining earlier Plans; rankings inform but do not dictate this choice. Each proposed Plan cites its motivating findings and specifies a hypothesis, mechanism, expected observations, rejection conditions, and controls. The accepted commitment defines what subsequent Trials within the Plan test. 

Builders implement strategies under a fixed research commitment. The Builder (Figure 2h) implements the strategy from a fixed Plan snapshot with its commitment and available Plan Memory 

(PM), plus approved prior strategies records. Bounded Harness feedback guides implementation revisions within the same Trial. These development checks do not train the target model; drafts and probes enter neither Trial rankings nor Memory. Later Trials refine the strategy using updated PM under that commitment; changing the hypothesis or research direction requires a new Plan. 

Independent analysis precedes hypothesis assessment. The Analyzer (Figure 2d) receives the Experiment Package without the Plan, hypothesis, or Memory. It can request bounded record-level review from a Review Worker , then synthesizes observations and experimental evidence independently of the expected answer. The Plan Summarizer (Figure 2e) uses these findings and the Plan’s commitment to assess how the observations relate to the proposed hypothesis. Each Trial keeps this model-generated assessment separate from Harness-provided conclusions on implementation fidelity and improvement over the frozen in-loop comparison target. Appendix C.1 specifies the role contracts and outcome categories. 

Cumulative Memory connects findings to subsequent decisions. After each terminal Trial, the Plan Summarizer updates Plan Memory using its prior contents, the Plan, and the Trial’s results and analysis. Plan Memory preserves the direction’s findings, contradictions, and unresolved questions. The Run Summarizer (Figure 2f) integrates this update with existing Run Memory and current Run-level results for subsequent Coordinator decisions. Findings retain their source Trials, scope, and assessment status, making their use in later research decisions traceable; the Harness governs when updates become available to other Plans (Section 3.3). 3.3 HARNESS ENFORCEMENT OF THE RESEARCH PROTOCOL

Before execution, it checks the strategy’s interface, authorized inputs, constraints, and executable behavior, then binds the accepted strategy to fixed configuration B in a frozen Engine command. Role-specific inputs define the information boundary. The Harness builds role-specific input packages from a fixed research-state version: Run-level findings for Coordinators, Plan snapshots and approved source strategies for Builders, and Experiment Packages for Analyzers (without the Plan, hypothesis, or Memory). The Engine receives the executable strategy, fixed configuration, and data references. Agents are instructed to use only supplied inputs. Appendix C.1 specifies role contracts and information-access rules. 

Versioned commits preserve the basis of research decisions. Plans execute asynchronously, while each planning call retains a fixed snapshot of shared findings. The Harness commits Plan Memory before queuing its update to Run Memory, processes Run Memory updates one at a time, and enables sharing and strategy reuse only after both updates are committed. Later calls can use new findings; running calls retain their original inputs. Recovery preserves the frozen inputs and committed state of the interrupted action. Held-out results remain outside subsequent research decisions. Held-out evaluation tests whether strategy improvements extend to data withheld from research. Held-out examples, responses, and scores are stored in an external evaluation record and excluded from checkpoint selection, Plan and Trial rankings, Memory, and subsequent research decisions. If they guide a later revision, they become in-loop feedback and that revision requires fresh held-out evaluation. Held-out results assess effectiveness within the configured evaluation scope. 

Operational oversight remains outside research decisions. The Operations Monitor reports resource availability and run status to the Human Researcher (Figure 2). After recoverable failures, such as worker crashes or stalled training, recovery replays the frozen experiment, preserving its Plan, strategy, configuration, and recorded results. Recovery actions are logged separately from research Memory. The Human Researcher uses domain expertise and held-out results to review ADE’s final strategy and decide whether to adopt it. Appendix C.8 specifies operational boundaries. Together, these mechanisms give agents control over research decisions under fixed experimental protocols (P1) and separate in-loop selection from held-out evaluation (P2).

Does ADE discover stronger strategies over successive experiments, and how does research capacity affect progress and computational cost? • RQ3: Research organization and experimental feedback. Both long chain-of-thought SFT (Long-CoT SFT) tasks use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and select 384 trajectories from a shared fixed mixed-domain pool of 3,840 OpenThoughts trajectories (Guha et al., 2026), with task-specific evaluation objectives. Both RFT tasks use Qwen2.5-0.5B, 3,072 MATH problems (Hendrycks et al., 2021), and 100 policy optimization steps; curriculum learning varies a precomputed data schedule with fixed outcome-only reward. Base model denotes the target before training. We compare C = 1 versus C = 3 concurrent Coordinator lanes, budgeting five Plans per Coordinator and one Trial per Plan, with three Runs per full ADE setting. Research agents use GPT-5.6 Sol in Codex with extra high reasoning effort; Qwen3.6-35B-A3B provides record-level review. Appendix D details training, agent budgets, and comparison protocols.

## 5 CONCLUSION AND DISCUSSION 

We studied agent-directed data engineering across four tasks under fixed experimental protocols, examining strategy effectiveness, research progress, organization, and feedback use. Selected strategies improve target-model performance over generic baselines; gains vary across held-out benchmarks and metrics. Strategies progressively improve on benchmarks used during research. Organization and feedback ablations favor the complete system; trajectories link earlier findings to later revisions and combinations beyond direct strategy inheritance. Results support assessing AI4AI research capability through progressive improvement and feedback-guided decisions, alongside held-out effectiveness. 

Limitations and future work. Adaptive search raises a general challenge for AI4AI: repeated optimization of an in-loop signal can overfit the feedback available to the research agent. Our held-out trajectories expose this risk alongside the gains from search, highlighting the value of evaluating generalization throughout research. LLM Agent stochasticity poses a second challenge: the same inputs can lead agents to different strategies and experimental paths. Comparing different research agents could clarify how their reasoning and exploration choices shape research outcomes. Research capability and evidence reuse (Q1). Experiment histories, reflective archives, shared artifacts, and diagnostic skills provide different routes for carrying evidence into later decisions. Our study examines both improvement in in-loop strategy scores and recorded links from earlier findings to subsequent revisions and combinations. The traces document how prior evidence informs later decisions, complementing the research-organization and feedback comparisons in Section 4.3. 

Evaluation independence and effectiveness (Q2). Final scoring can differ from search feedback in timing, sampled responses, data, or training realization. RSIBench-Data explicitly reports task-subset reuse despite a separate official evaluation. AI4AI-Bench’s boundary depends on the task, so a single binary label would hide material differences. DataAgent evaluates the selected dataset through retraining; ADE evaluates the already selected checkpoint. Our effectiveness question concerns performance on evaluation examples withheld from in-loop evaluation, with results excluded from research and selection. This tests effectiveness beyond the evaluation feedback used to develop the strategy. 

Earlier foundations for feedback-driven data and reward design. DataEnvGym formulates data generation as sequential decisions informed by student errors and skill feedback (Khan et al., 2025). Curation-Bench fixes the model, training recipe, and evaluation suite while agents inspect data, implement selection policies, and revise them from training feedback (Kang et al., 2026). Its analysis of local policy tuning and method-guided exploration directly informs the study of research behavior within a fixed training setup. Eureka uses policy-training feedback to revise executable rewards for control tasks (Ma et al., 2024); ADE studies reward design alongside selection and curricula for LLM 14 training. These works establish precedents for feedback-driven intervention design and controlled training. Our study combines these ingredients with agent-directed research organization, feedback ablations, and evidence-linked trajectories across the four data-engineering tasks. 

The scope of a research decision. The meaning of strategy revision depends on the permitted intervention. Lim et al. (2026) study choices of training paradigm, data regime, and stage structure. Curation-Bench examines changes among data-policy families within a fixed training setup. ADE also fixes the training paradigm: its decisions concern data-engineering hypotheses, their implementation, and the allocation of experiments. Evidence of revision at this level addresses a complementary question to revising the training paradigm itself. MARS already studies cross-branch transfer of reflective lessons (Chen et al., 2026b); our trajectories examine such evidence reuse within the controlled data-engineering setting. 

What changes in recursive self-improvement? RSI work places the improvement loop at several levels. STOP applies a code improver to its own scaffolding, using a fixed language model (Zelikman et al., 2024). Darwin Gödel Machine selects coding agents from an archive and uses them to modify their own implementations, also with frozen foundation models (Zhang et al., 2026). These systems demonstrate that the improvement procedure can change without updating model weights. SEAL instead learns to generate data and update directives for its own weight adaptation, rewarding self-edits by downstream performance (Zweiger et al., 2025). Autodata’s reported meta-optimization evolves the data scientist’s prompts through code edits (Kulikov et al., 2026); Frontis-MA1 trains research operators from executable experience (Yang et al., 2026). A-Evolve-Training revises a rolling research policy across post-training rounds while retaining an immutable reference stack (Shi et al., 2026). Each provides a different route for carrying experience into the next improvement process. 

Proposed recursion and demonstrated closure. The scope of the experiments is as relevant as the RSI framing. HELIX proposes build–update–rebuild and demonstrates harness evolution and verified training-data export; its Section 9.5 states that it does not train an updated model (Fan & Huang, 2026). ScienceBuddy reports successive harness-refinement and RL cycles, with a separate fixed auxiliary model proposing harness edits (Xue et al., 2026). This evaluates changes to the task model and its harness while retaining the auxiliary improver. Beyond its main cross-model benchmark, RSIBench-Data reports an exploratory same-family experiment in Section 5.5; its adapted candidates remain below the unadapted reference (Meng et al., 2026). Frontis-MA1 distinguishes training the improver from a sustained loop in which successors improve their own production process (Yang et al., 2026). The recent roadmap of Duan et al. (2026) likewise distinguishes autonomy over improvement strategies from recursive meta-improvement. These distinctions motivate asking what an improved system contributes to the next research round. In ADE, strategies and Memory change while the researcher models and Harness remain fixed. This isolates data-engineering research as a component of AI4AI and provides experience from which future systems could learn to conduct better research. 

## B DISCUSSION AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS 

AI4AI can improve both the models produced by research and the process that produces them. ADE studies a concrete connection between these two objectives: agents develop data-engineering strategies, observe their effects on learning, and carry findings into later experiments. The results motivate moving from better strategies within a research campaign to better research across campaigns. Work on RSI offers several ways to make this transition, including revising an improver’s code, learning research operators, and alternating model and harness updates (Zelikman et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026; Xue et al., 2026). The following directions concern what should improve, how experience should persist, and how to measure the value of the resulting research process. 

From improving a target model to improving the researcher. A stronger target model could become a data generator, evaluator, or researcher in a later campaign. These roles require different capabilities: gains on math or code benchmarks alone do not establish better experimental judgment. A direct next experiment would train a researcher from ADE trajectories, then compare it with its predecessor on fresh data-engineering tasks under matched budgets. The record would need to preserve the evidence available when each decision was made, including unsuccessful interventions and their subsequent corrections. Frontis-MA1’s training of research operators provides a precedent 15 for learning from executable research experience (Yang et al., 2026); SEAL provides a complementary example of learning which self-generated updates improve a model (Zweiger et al., 2025). For ADE, the relevant outcome would be improved research yield: better strategies, fewer wasted experiments, or more accurate predictions of where an intervention helps. Repeating this comparison across researcher generations would test whether better research experience produces better future research, connecting the present setting to recursive meta-improvement (Duan et al., 2026). 

Letting the research procedure evolve alongside its models. The usefulness of a diagnostic tool or planning policy may change as the researcher and target models improve. Autodata illustrates improvement of the data scientist through prompt evolution (Kulikov et al., 2026), while A-Evolve-Training revises the policy governing subsequent research rounds (Shi et al., 2026). HELIX motivates revisiting harness design after model updates; ScienceBuddy implements alternating harness revision and RL (Fan & Huang, 2026; Xue et al., 2026). For ADE, this suggests allowing versioned changes to diagnostic tools, Memory retrieval, or coordination between campaigns. A useful first study would compare the original and revised research procedures with both the original and updated researcher models. This would identify whether their benefits transfer or depend on a particular model–procedure pairing. Each campaign could retain a fixed Engine and evaluation contract, making successive system versions comparable while expanding what the outer research loop can change. 

Turning experiment histories into transferable research knowledge. The code case in RQ4 shows why a winning artifact is only part of the research output. An unsuccessful shortening intervention helps motivate a later strategy that controls repetition while matching length. The useful inheritance is a conditional finding about what to test, even when the original artifact is abandoned. Reflective memory in MARS and the archives in POISE already preserve evidence across branches (Chen et al., 2026b; Xia et al., 2026b). A next step for ADE is to store findings with the model, data distribution, training budget, and diagnostic evidence under which they were obtained. Retrieval should distinguish a replicated result from an unresolved explanation. Testing this knowledge on new model families would measure whether agents can predict when an earlier finding applies, revise it when conditions change, and avoid repeating resolved experiments. Such records could support both inference-time Memory and training of future researchers, with their value measured on new campaigns. 

Valuing experiments that change what the agent knows. The feedback comparisons in RQ3 and the code case motivate an objective beyond improving the current best score. An experiment can be valuable because it separates competing explanations. For example, matched length exposure can help distinguish a benefit from removing repetition from a benefit due to shorter reasoning. An Analyzer could make the competing predictions explicit; the next intervention would test the factor on which they disagree. This also creates a concrete target for researcher learning: does the agent choose experiments whose outcomes improve later decisions? Evaluation could compare such a policy with score-driven refinement under a common budget, measuring both final performance and the accuracy of predictions on new interventions. Curation-Bench’s distinction between local tuning and method-guided exploration provides a related view of research progress (Kang et al., 2026). 

Measuring whether research gains repay their cost. Greater research capacity improves mean in-loop endpoints in RQ2, but consumes more resources and encounters plateaus. For AI4AI, the return depends on how often a discovered strategy will be reused. An expensive search may be useful if its strategy improves many subsequent training runs; a cheaper strategy may be preferable for one target. Future studies could report performance at fixed total budgets that include researcher inference, candidate training, validation, and researcher updates. They could also measure the cost of reaching a predeclared performance target across repeated campaigns. Budget-aware search in MARS offers a starting point (Chen et al., 2026b). ADE adds a scheduling question: when should an agent launch another experiment, and when should it wait for pending evidence that could change several branches? Comparing adaptive allocation with fixed allocation would test whether better coordination increases research yield. 

Maintaining useful feedback across successive generations. As research systems learn from more campaigns, previously reserved results can become development feedback through memory or researcher training. The evaluation unit must therefore include the history inherited by each system version. Studies could partition entire research tasks across researcher training, development, and final assessment, refreshing final tasks as generations advance. They could also test whether gains survive changes in target model, training budget, and objective. RSIBench-Data’s exploratory same-family 16 Table 7: Role-specific evidence and decisions. D.6 PROTOCOL FOR THE COMPARED RESEARCH METHODS 

The Data Selection–Math comparisons in Section 4.3 use the same task-defined candidate pool, target-model initialization, training recipe, per-candidate training budget, checkpoint rule, benchmark splits, and in-loop metric ordering. The alternatives compare different research organizations and feedback settings. 

Unified Agent. One persistent session plans, implements, and analyzes candidates using raw experimental evidence and execution facts. Prescribed role handoffs and structured Plan/Run research memory are removed, while the agent may retain its own notes. The ADE Controller still allocates five search slots, executes candidates, and resumes the session after each experiment. Failed candidates consume their allocated slots; same-Trial retries do not create extra search opportunities. This comparison changes role decomposition and explicit Memory organization jointly. Separate Builder reflection calls are disabled, while candidate realization, validation, and repair remain. 

Independent Search. Each repetition consists of five independently developed initial candidates, with no access to findings from the other candidates and no later-Plan refinement. Repetition and selection follow the shared reporting protocol below. 

Outcome-only Feedback and Raw Evidence Feedback. Both retain the Coordinator/Planner and Builder and permit iterative revision within five search slots. Outcome-only Feedback supplies candidate history, scalar scores, and execution facts. Raw Evidence Feedback additionally exposes accepted raw experimental evidence to the Planner, which performs its own analysis. Both remove the independent Analyzer, Review, and research summarization workflow. Full ADE combines these roles with structured research memory. In both reduced variants, Plan/Run memory versions contain deterministic outcome records rather than model-generated research summaries, and existing role conversations remain available. Raw Evidence Feedback exposes accepted historical experiment packages, including the bootstrap baseline, to the Planner; Builder retains its current and source Plans, code, fixed task inputs, and candidate implementation feedback. Analyzer Review is distinct from the task Judge: the reduced variants retain the task-defined Judge capability and fixed training/evaluation interfaces. The initial proposals address three distinct potential weaknesses in the baseline selection. All begin from the same fixed pool and evaluation setting, but they operationalize different training-data properties. 33 C1/P1 — Complete targets. C1 first asks whether training on cutoff-exceeding responses is a source of avoidable supervision loss. The baseline contains 26 selected records marked to exceed the training cutoff; its evaluation also contains 197 length-terminated generations without extracted executable code. The proposal replaces cutoff-exceeding baseline records with unused, non-truncating candidates of approximately matched length, preserving the 384-record budget and the baseline’s 142/107/135 short/medium/long allocation. C2/P1 — Code-oriented allocation. C2 instead asks whether a mixed-domain candidate pool should be selected using observable alignment with the downstream coding task. It prioritizes prompts explicitly requesting executable code through a named interface or a standard-input/standard-output contract. It uses length and cutoff strata as matching controls rather than relying on noisy semantic domain labels. C3/P1 — Executable-target integrity. C3 investigates whether definite defects in machine-checkable programming targets can be removed using extraction, parsing/compilation, requested-interface checks, and runnable prompt examples. Its interface checks give C3/P2 a concrete component to combine with C2/P1’s stronger code-oriented subset. H.4 FEEDBACK , R EFINEMENT , AND RECOMBINATION 

The next proposals test explanations suggested by the initial results and combine components from different branches. Two unsuccessful interventions—shortening and Judge-based filtering—later help shape C1/P3. 

C2/P2 — Distinguishing long reasoning from unproductive reasoning. The code-oriented parent contains substantial long-form supervision, and its failed evaluation generations are longer than successful ones. C2 tests a moderate-length preference by replacing non-truncating long records with medium-length records of the same prompt-contract subtype. The realized intervention moves 43 records from the long band to the medium band, but both metrics decline, to 42.82% Pass@3 and 31.21% Average@3. This decline later motivates C1/P3 to match length while targeting repetition. The subsequent search therefore separates reasoning length from repetition within the reasoning. 

C3/P2 — Combining task alignment with callable integrity. C3 explicitly combines C2/P1’s code-oriented source with C3/P1’s executable-integrity direction. Inspection of the stronger source found parseable Python blocks that nevertheless omitted a requested top-level function. The new proposal therefore targets exact requested-callable presence, rather than treating successful parsing as sufficient compliance. It obtains 42.14% Pass@3 and 30.52% Average@3. The broader definition-and-invocation defects identified during this experiment are later cited by C1/P4 when formulating a more specific wrapper-closure experiment. 

C1/P2 — Testing semantic validity as a selection signal. The task Judge scores candidate question– response pairs against a rubric, separately from benchmark grading. Building on C1/P1, C1 uses this Judge to identify substantively invalid targets and replace them with same-family valid candidates. Reviews of the complete-target subset had flagged major errors, including an association between severe meandering and correctness problems. The new result slightly increases Pass@3 to 42.60%, but lowers Average@3 to 28.78%, violating the proposal’s secondary-metric condition. The candidate judgments contain thousands of passes but no fails, providing no failing group for the proposed replacement rule. These observations later influence C1/P3’s choice not to use Judge output as its selection criterion. 

C2/P3 and C2/P4 — Separating an exploratory direction from its combination. After the unsuccessful length refinement, C2/P3 starts a formally new direction: prioritize programming targets qualified by execution and prompt-example checks, then spread them across prompt-family/length cells. Its proposal consults the earlier executable-integrity findings and the failed moderate-length 34 experiment, but does not declare either as an artifact parent. It reaches 46.47% Pass@3 and 31.89% Average@3, becoming the primary-metric leader. C3/P4, proposed before C1/P3 has completed, starts from the leader available at that time. Weak hard-problem performance motivates a bounded preference for greater branch complexity in the Python abstract syntax tree (AST) among otherwise matched execution-qualified targets. It reaches 45.79% Pass@3 and 32.27% Average@3. C3/P4 therefore uses the strongest completed source available at proposal time. H.5 CROSS -C OORDINATOR FINDINGS AND STRATIFIED REPETITION CONTROL 

C1/P3 adopts C2/P1 as its formal parent and proposes Stratified Repetition Control . Three findings, marked K1–K3 in Figure 6, explain the choice of intervention: • K1: Target repetition. C2/P2’s unsuccessful shortening motivates matching length while changing repetition. • K3: Use a deterministic signal. C1/P2’s uninformative Judge gate motivates an observable repetition measure instead of another semantic threshold. The proposal also takes account of active Plans already exploring execution qualification and code-oriented complete targets. Their proposed directions help C1/P3 focus on repetition while other branches explore complementary properties of the training data. The strategy replaces highly repetitive parent records with low-repetition candidates matched jointly by prompt-contract subtype, truncation status, and length band, then by nearest token count within each stratum.
